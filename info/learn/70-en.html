EIA Weekly Crude Oil Inventories

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

EIA Weekly Crude Oil Inventories, published every Wednesday at 10:30 a.m. Eastern by the U.S. Energy Information Administration within the Weekly Petroleum Status Report, track the total stock and weekly net change of U.S. commercial crude oil held in storage (excluding the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, SPR) as of the prior Friday. It is the most authoritative high-frequency supply-and-demand balance sheet for the global oil market. Beginners often assume any crude inventory draw means booming consumer gasoline demand, or confuse Tuesday afternoon's private API industry estimate with Wednesday's official EIA government report.

Start with an everyday comparison

It is like checking the level of raw crude oil tanks at refineries and storage hubs every Wednesday: a drop in the raw crude tank (a draw) can mean refineries are busy turning crude into gasoline for eager drivers, or it can simply mean fog closed the shipping channel so foreign oil tankers could not unload that week.

What does this number measure?

Table 1 of the EIA report lists total stocks in millions (or thousands) of barrels, while headlines focus on the Weekly Net Change in millions of barrels (e.g., -2.5M means a 2.5-million-barrel draw; +2.5M means a build). Always separate four key rows in the report: (1) Commercial Crude Oil Inventories excluding SPR (the headline number); (2) the government's Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR); (3) downstream refined products—Total Motor Gasoline and Distillate Fuel Oil (diesel and heating oil); and (4) Refinery Utilization Rate (%) and Net Crude Imports.

Why can markets react?

Transmission to broad equity and crypto markets runs through inflation and growth expectations: surprises in crude and product inventories → move WTI and Brent crude oil prices → directly impact Energy sector earnings while simultaneously moving retail gasoline prices, headline CPI inflation expectations, and consumer discretionary budgets → influence Treasury yields and Federal Reserve rate-cut expectations → filter into broader equities and crypto. Consequently, while a supply-driven oil spike helps energy stocks, it often acts as an inflation and interest-rate headwind for tech stocks and crypto.

Read the release step by step

  1. Check the reference Friday and Commercial Crude (ex-SPR) line: open Wednesday's 10:30 a.m. EIA Table 1 and read the weekly change in Commercial Crude stocks.
  2. Compare actual against consensus (and Tuesday's API): note the million-barrel surprise relative to analyst forecasts.
  3. Inspect Gasoline, Distillate, and Refinery Runs: check whether a crude draw simply moved into a large gasoline or diesel build as refineries ramped up utilization.
  4. Check Net Imports and SPR adjustments: verify whether temporary port/hurricane disruptions skewed net crude imports or whether SPR releases added supply.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

For broad equities and crypto: if healthy crude supply and moderate builds keep oil and gasoline prices contained while product demand stays steady (pointing to lower headline inflation pressure without a recession), easing bond yields can support broad risk assets (even as oil futures and energy stocks soften).

When a fall is plausible

If supply shocks trigger steep inventory draws and send oil prices surging, fears of rebounding headline inflation and delayed rate cuts can push Treasury yields up and pressure broad equities and crypto; conversely, massive builds across both crude and products driven by collapsing fuel demand can spark recession fears.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If a large headline crude draw is offset barrel-for-barrel by builds in gasoline and distillate inventories—or stems from a one-week tanker import timing quirk—oil and macro markets often whip around 10:30 a.m. before settling back into a range.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose consensus expects Commercial Crude inventories to fall by -2.0M barrels (prior +1.0M), and Wednesday's EIA report prints a -5.0M barrel crude draw (a 3.0M barrel larger draw than expected). However, reading further down Table 1: Motor Gasoline inventories jump by +4.0M barrels (expected 0.0M) and Distillate (diesel) inventories rise by +1.5M barrels as Refinery Utilization climbs from 88% to 92%. Combining the three gives -5.0M (crude) + 4.0M (gasoline) + 1.5M (distillate) = +0.5M barrels net increase across crude and major products. Refineries simply processed crude into gasoline that sat unsold in storage, so the -5.0M crude headline does not prove strong end-user demand.

History and origin

EIA was established in 1977; the weekly reporting system and historical series evolved separately, so agency formation is not every component’s first release.

Where and how to find the data

U.S. Energy Information Administration EIA · Weekly · Usually Wednesday at 10:30 a.m. U.S. Eastern; holidays may delay it.

U.S. Energy Information Administration EIA official data

  1. Open Weekly Petroleum Status Report.
  2. Find U.S. commercial crude oil inventories excluding SPR and the weekly change.
  3. Read gasoline and distillate stocks, refinery runs, trade flows and adjustments before judging demand.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Trading the single Commercial Crude headline line while ignoring offsetting builds in Gasoline and Distillate stocks or changes in refinery utilization.
  2. Confusing Tuesday afternoon's private American Petroleum Institute (API) voluntary sample with Wednesday morning's mandatory official EIA government report.
  3. Assuming 'crude draw = bullish for oil = bullish for S&P 500 and Bitcoin', forgetting that surging oil prices act as a tax on consumers and an inflation headwind for rate-sensitive assets.
