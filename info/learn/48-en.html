MBA Weekly Mortgage Applications

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The MBA Weekly Mortgage Applications Survey, published every Wednesday morning by the Mortgage Bankers Association, tracks weekly changes in the volume of single-family mortgage loan applications submitted to U.S. commercial banks, thrifts, and mortgage bankers during the week ended the prior Friday. As the highest-frequency (weekly) leading gauge of U.S. housing activity, it splits applications into home Purchase applications and Refinance applications. Beginners often see a +10% jump in the headline composite index and assume homebuying demand jumped 10%, when the move was actually driven by existing homeowners refinancing after a small dip in rates.

Start with an everyday comparison

It counts how many people lined up at lender desks to hand in loan application forms last week: one line consists of shoppers applying to buy a home (Purchase), while the other line consists of current homeowners swapping an old loan for a slightly lower rate (Refinance); handing in an application form is neither final loan approval nor a closed home sale.

What does this number measure?

Reported in Index Points benchmarked to the week of March 16, 1990 = 100 alongside Seasonally Adjusted weekly percentage changes (w/w %). Three core indexes are reported: the Market Composite Index (total applications), the Purchase Index (applications specifically to buy a home—the true gauge of housing transaction demand), and the Refinance Index (applications to replace an existing mortgage, which is hyper-sensitive to small weekly rate moves). Note that the survey counts mortgage applications only and captures zero all-cash home purchases.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect: weekly moves in Treasury yields and mortgage rates → immediately alter household refinancing and home-purchase loan applications → lead Pending Home Sales (Lesson 45) and New Home Sales (Lesson 44) by several weeks while refinancing savings free up monthly household cash flow → inform market views on how tightly interest rates are biting housing → filter into bond yields, housing equities, and broad stock/crypto risk sentiment. Because weekly numbers are noisy and holiday-sensitive, single-week prints rarely move macro markets unless they form a multi-week trend.

Read the release step by step

  1. Check the reference week and contract mortgage rate: open Wednesday's MBA release, note the Friday cutoff date, and check how many basis points the accompanying 30-year mortgage rate moved.
  2. Separate Purchase from Refinance immediately: read the seasonally adjusted Purchase Index w/w % change separately from the Refinance Index w/w % change rather than relying on the Market Composite.
  3. Use a 4-week average to smooth holiday noise: because three-day U.S. holiday weeks heavily distort weekly seasonal adjustments, track 4-week trends.
  4. Cross-check against monthly home sales: compare multi-week Purchase Index trends against subsequent Pending and New Home Sales reports.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If the Purchase Index climbs steadily over several weeks while employment holds firm, it suggests buyers are absorbing current mortgage rates and housing demand is stabilizing, which can support cyclical equities and risk sentiment if bond yields stay calm.

When a fall is plausible

If the Purchase Index slides to multi-month lows over several consecutive weeks alongside rising mortgage rates, it warns that financing costs are choking off buyer demand ahead of next month's sales reports; however, a Composite drop driven solely by falling Refinance activity carries much less weight for home sales.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If the headline Composite jumps solely because a 15-bp rate dip sparked a Refinance burst while the Purchase Index stayed flat or fell—or if a federal holiday skewed the weekly adjustment—broad equity and crypto markets typically ignore the print.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose the 30-year mortgage rate dips 15 bps from 6.80% to 6.65% and the MBA Market Composite Index jumps +10.4% w/w. Splitting the components: suppose the Refinance Index (40% share of total applications) surges from 400 to 510 ([510 - 400] / 400 = +27.5% w/w), while the Purchase Index (60% share) slips from 150.0 to 148.5 ([148.5 - 150.0] / 150.0 = -1.0% w/w). Weighted check: (0.40 × +27.5%) + (0.60 × -1.0%) = +11.0% - 0.6% = +10.4%. The entire +10.4% headline surge came from refinancing existing loans, while actual home-buying applications fell 1.0%.

History and origin

MBA launched the weekly survey in 1990; unadjusted indexes use the week of March 16, 1990 as 100.

Where and how to find the data

Mortgage Bankers Association MBA · Weekly · Usually Wednesday at 7:00 a.m. U.S. Eastern; holidays may change timing.

Mortgage Bankers Association MBA official data

  1. Open the latest Weekly Applications Survey release or MBA Newslink.
  2. Find Market Composite, Purchase and Refinance indexes.
  3. Compare adjusted weekly changes and a multiweek trend; a refinancing jump is not a purchase jump.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading a +10% jump in the headline Market Composite Index as a 10% surge in homebuyers without checking whether Refinance or Purchase drove the move.
  2. Equating loan applications with closed loans—one buyer may submit applications to multiple lenders to shop rates, or later be denied underwriting approval.
  3. Overreacting to sharp single-week swings around U.S. holidays (such as Thanksgiving, Memorial Day, or July 4th) when seasonal factors often misfire.
