Cboe Volatility Index (VIX)

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Often dubbed the 'Fear Gauge', the VIX is not an opinion poll—it is a mathematical index derived directly from real-time S&P 500 option prices measuring expected 30-day volatility. Understanding how it translates annualized volatility into monthly price ranges prevents costly misinterpretations.

Start with an everyday comparison

The VIX is like a coastal hurricane insurance premium index: spiking premiums show institutions rushing to buy storm protection, but you cannot store the index itself.

What does this number measure?

Cboe calculates the VIX by aggregating the weighted prices of a wide strip of out-of-the-money S&P 500 Index (SPX) call and put options expiring in 23 to 37 days. Quoted in annualized percentage points, it converts to an expected 30-day one-standard-deviation move via: 30-Day Expected Move % = VIX / sqrt(12) (approx. VIX / 3.464). Unlike Historical Volatility (which measures past realized moves), VIX is strictly forward-looking.

Why can markets react?

Because institutional portfolios are heavily net-long equities, sudden market selloffs prompt urgent buying of SPX put options for downside protection, bidding up option premiums and sending the VIX sharply higher. Spiking VIX levels also force volatility-targeting funds to mechanically de-lever equity and risk exposure.

Read the release step by step

  1. Convert annualized VIX to a 30-day or daily range: divide VIX by 3.464 (sqrt of 12) for the expected 30-day 1-standard-deviation move, or by 15.87 (sqrt of 252 trading days) for the implied daily move.
  2. Contextualize the regime: readings of 12–20 reflect calm option pricing, whereas spikes above 30 signal acute hedging demand; however, low VIX merely means cheap options, not zero risk.
  3. Remember VIX spot cannot be bought directly: tradable VIX futures and ETPs suffer severe long-term contango roll decay and are designed only for short-term tactical hedging.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

When VIX spikes sharply higher: signals urgent institutional demand for option protection and heightened market stress, typically coinciding with sharp equity drawdowns.

When a fall is plausible

When VIX retreats from elevated peaks: indicates subsiding panic and implied volatility compression (IV crush), often accompanying market stabilization.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When VIX lingers at ultra-low levels (e.g., 12–14): reflects market complacency and inexpensive option protection, though crowded complacency can precede abrupt volatility shocks.

Work through a made-up example

Illustration only: Suppose the VIX reads 34.64. This does not mean the S&P 500 will drop 34.64% next month; it means expected annualized volatility is 34.64%. Dividing by sqrt(12) (approx. 3.464) yields 34.64% / 3.464 = 10.00%, implying the options market prices a roughly 68% probability (1 standard deviation) that the S&P 500 will stay within +/-10.00% over the next 30 days. These figures are hypothetical.

History and origin

Introduced by Cboe in 1993 based on Professor Robert E. Whaley's research (originally tracking S&P 100 options), the VIX methodology was updated in 2003 alongside Goldman Sachs to use a wide strip of S&P 500 Index (SPX) call and put options.

Where and how to find the data

Cboe Global Markets (Cboe Exchange, Inc.) · Continuous real-time updates every 15 seconds during Cboe trading hours · Calculated and disseminated in real time every 15 seconds across Cboe global trading hours and regular U.S. market hours (9:30 AM – 4:15 PM ET), subject to Cboe official schedules.

Cboe Official VIX Product Page and FAQs

  1. Visit the official Cboe VIX FAQ and data page to view live index levels and methodology.
  2. Interpret the unit: VIX quotes expected 30-day S&P 500 volatility as an annualized percentage (e.g., VIX = 20 implies 20% annualized standard deviation).
  3. Check the VIX futures term structure to see if near-term contracts trade below (contango) or above (backwardation) longer-dated contracts.

FRED: Cboe Volatility Index Historical Close Series (VIXCLS)

  1. Open the FRED VIXCLS page to examine daily historical closing levels dating back to 1990.
  2. Compare normal regime ranges (typically 12–20) against crisis spikes (exceeding 80 in 2008 and March 2020).
  3. Observe VIX's long-term mean-reverting behavior following acute liquidity shocks.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Attempting to buy and hold VIX ETFs long-term waiting for a crash: you cannot buy spot VIX, and VIX futures ETPs suffer relentless contango roll losses during calm markets.
  2. Mistaking a low VIX reading for guaranteed market safety: a low VIX only shows that protection is currently cheap, not that a sudden shock cannot occur.
