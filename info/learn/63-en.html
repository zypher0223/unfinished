Monthly Treasury Budget Balance

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The Monthly Treasury Budget Balance (from the Monthly Treasury Statement, MTS) measures the difference between total federal government outlays (spending) and total receipts (primarily individual and corporate income taxes and social insurance taxes) over a given month or fiscal year: outlays exceeding receipts produce a budget deficit, while receipts exceeding outlays produce a budget surplus. Crucially, the budget balance is a period flow rather than the cumulative stock of outstanding U.S. national debt.

Start with an everyday comparison

Think of the monthly budget balance as a household's monthly income-and-expense statement: earning $100 and spending $120 in a month creates a $20 monthly cash shortfall (a flow), which is distinct from the family's cumulative mortgage and credit card debt built up over decades (a stock).

What does this number measure?

Published monthly by the U.S. Department of the Treasury in millions or billions of dollars, the MTS requires attention to two structural rules: first, the U.S. federal fiscal year runs from October 1 through September 30 of the following calendar year (so October is Month 1 of the new fiscal year), meaning Fiscal Year-to-Date (FYTD) totals must never be confused with calendar-year totals; second, federal receipts and outlays are unadjusted for seasonality (NSA), and when the 1st of a month falls on a weekend or holiday, major benefit payments shift into the end of the prior month, distorting single-month headlines.

Why can markets react?

Fiscal deficits exert two competing forces on markets: from a macro demand standpoint, deficit spending injects income into households and revenues into corporations, supporting earnings during slowdowns; from a financial supply standpoint, large and growing structural deficits require heavier Treasury debt issuance, which can lift inflation expectations, term premia, and long-term Treasury yields, tightening valuation multiples for equities and crypto. Market direction depends on whether growth support or rate pressure dominates.

Read the release step by step

  1. Open the official Monthly Treasury Statement (MTS) and verify the reference month, publication date, million/billion-dollar units, and the unadjusted (NSA) nature of the series.
  2. Read monthly total receipts, total outlays, the monthly surplus or deficit, and the Fiscal Year-to-Date (FYTD, starting October 1) cumulative balance, checking the report notes for calendar timing shifts when the 1st of a month fell on a weekend or holiday.
  3. Compare the monthly print and FYTD trend against pre-release consensus forecasts, the same month a year earlier (YoY), and the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) monthly budget review, inspecting drivers such as net interest outlays, entitlement spending, and tax collections.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

When fiscal outlays support steady corporate revenue growth with orderly bond market absorption, or when stronger tax receipts narrow the FYTD deficit and ease long-term bond supply fears, equities and crypto can benefit.

When a fall is plausible

When non-recessionary deficits and net interest costs widen sharply above expectations, stoking Treasury supply concerns and pushing 10-year and 30-year yields higher, equity valuation multiples and crypto often face pressure.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When a large monthly deficit or surplus reflects routine seasonal tax deadlines (such as April tax inflows) or weekend benefit-payment shifts that wash out over two months, markets typically look through the headline noise.

Work through a made-up example

Suppose in a given month federal receipts are $300 billion and baseline outlays are $400 billion, implying a $400B - $300B = $100 billion baseline deficit; however, because the 1st of the following month falls on a Saturday, $50 billion of scheduled benefit payments are paid on the last business day of the current month, lifting reported outlays to $450 billion and the headline deficit to $450B - $300B = $150 billion (while reducing next month's reported deficit by $50 billion). The two-month combined deficit is unchanged, so analysts must adjust for the $50 billion calendar shift rather than claiming the deficit structurally jumped 50%. Illustration only.

History and origin

The statement reflects longstanding government accounting. A database start is neither the first U.S. deficit nor necessarily the first publication.

Where and how to find the data

U.S. Treasury Fiscal Service · Monthly · MTS normally around the eighth business day after month-end at 2:00 p.m. U.S. Eastern; confirm the calendar.

U.S. Treasury Fiscal Service official data

  1. Open the Monthly Treasury Statement dataset or latest report.
  2. Choose Summary of Receipts, Outlays, and the Deficit/Surplus.
  3. Compare monthly and fiscal-year-to-date totals and check tax or payment-calendar shifts.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Do not confuse a budget deficit with a sovereign default, do not mistake an April tax-season monthly surplus for permanent fiscal consolidation, and remember that TGA cash buffers prevent a one-for-one monthly link between the deficit and debt issuance.
  2. Markets trade marginal new information relative to expectations. Because the CBO usually releases an accurate monthly estimate days before the official MTS, check whether the print surprises relative to expectations and confirm with long-term Treasury yields, the dollar, and broad indices.
