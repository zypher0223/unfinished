Net Marketable Treasury Borrowing

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Net Marketable Treasury Borrowing measures the net new funding the U.S. Treasury raises from the private market over a given period by taking gross issuance of marketable Treasury securities and subtracting the principal of maturing securities repaid during the same window. While gross Treasury issuance runs into tens of trillions of dollars annually, the vast majority simply refinances (rolls over) maturing debt; only net borrowing represents incremental new supply that investors must absorb.

Start with an everyday comparison

Think of net Treasury borrowing like rolling over a monthly credit card balance: if you charge $100 in new purchases while paying off $80 of maturing old balances, your net additional borrowing from the lender is $20, not the $100 gross swipe volume.

What does this number measure?

Reported typically in billions of dollars in the Treasury's Quarterly Refunding borrowing estimates and monthly settlement tables, this metric requires separating initial quarterly estimates, revised mid-quarter estimates, and settled actuals, while focusing on 'Privately-Held Net Marketable Borrowing' (which excludes rollovers held in the Federal Reserve's SOMA portfolio unless redeemed).

Why can markets react?

Both the size and maturity mix (short-term Treasury Bills vs. longer-dated Coupon Notes and Bonds) of net issuance shape bond yields and market liquidity: heavy surprises in long-dated coupon supply can push up term premia and long-term Treasury yields, pressuring equity valuations and crypto leverage; conversely, net issuance skewed toward short-term T-Bills can often be absorbed by money market funds rotating cash out of the Fed's ON RRP facility without directly draining commercial bank reserves.

Read the release step by step

  1. Open the Treasury's quarterly Marketable Borrowing Estimates release and subsequent Quarterly Refunding Statement, confirming the calendar quarter, billion-dollar units, and the Privately-Held Net Marketable Borrowing figure.
  2. Check the accompanying Assumed End-of-Quarter TGA Cash Balance in the Monday borrowing release, then inspect the Wednesday refunding breakdown between short-term T-Bills and medium-to-long-term coupon auctions.
  3. Compare the announced net borrowing estimate against pre-release primary-dealer consensus forecasts and the prior quarter's estimate, verifying whether revisions stem from wider fiscal deficits or simply a change in the target TGA cash balance.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

Lower-than-expected quarterly net borrowing estimates or an issuance mix tilted toward T-Bills rather than long-dated coupons that allows long-term yields to ease can relieve term-premium pressure and support equities and crypto.

When a fall is plausible

An upward surprise in net borrowing paired with larger-than-expected 10-year and 30-year coupon auction sizes that drives long-term yields higher often weighs on growth stocks and crypto valuations.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

Net borrowing and maturity splits that match primary-dealer consensus, or bill-heavy supply smoothly absorbed out of ON RRP balances, typically leave broad markets trading within existing ranges.

Work through a made-up example

Suppose in a given quarter the U.S. Treasury issues $1,000 billion ($1.0 trillion) in gross marketable securities while repaying $800 billion of maturing principal; net marketable borrowing is $1,000B - $800B = $200 billion. If consensus expected $250 billion in net borrowing, the $200 billion print is actually $250B - $200B = $50 billion below expectations—a marginal supply relief—and one must never treat the $1,000 billion gross issuance as a $1 trillion net liquidity drain. Illustration only.

History and origin

Net borrowing is an accounting measure within Treasury’s evolving financing process; no single trader created it and no unverified first release date is asserted.

Where and how to find the data

U.S. Department of the Treasury · Quarterly plans and periodic actuals · Quarterly financing documents are issued in stages; planned borrowing differs from settled issuance.

U.S. Department of the Treasury official data

  1. Open Most Recent Documents under Quarterly Refunding.
  2. Choose Marketable Borrowing Estimates and the quarterly refunding statement.
  3. Find privately-held net marketable borrowing, assumed cash balances and the bill/coupon issuance mix.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Never confuse gross issuance with net borrowing, always distinguish the auction date from the actual cash settlement date, and remember that net borrowing equals the fiscal deficit plus any planned change in the TGA cash balance.
  2. Markets trade marginal new information relative to expectations. Evaluate both Monday's aggregate borrowing estimate and Wednesday's maturity composition alongside 10-year yields, term premia, the dollar, and broad index confirmation.
