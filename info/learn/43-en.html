Existing-Home Sales

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Existing-Home Sales, published monthly by the National Association of Realtors (NAR), count the number of previously owned U.S. homes (single-family homes, townhomes, condominiums, and co-ops) where sales transactions officially closed (title transferred) during the reference month, alongside active inventory and median sales prices. Accounting for the vast majority (roughly 80%–90%) of total U.S. residential transactions, it measures completed closings rather than newly signed contracts (Lessons 44 and 45). Beginners often mistake a rise in the median sales price for uniform appreciation across identical homes.

Start with an everyday comparison

If buying a home is like ordering custom furniture, signing the purchase contract (measured by New Home Sales and Pending Home Sales) is placing the order online, whereas Existing-Home Sales is the day the delivery truck arrives and you sign the final receipt—reflecting shopping decisions made one to two months earlier.

What does this number measure?

Sales volume is reported at a Seasonally Adjusted Annual Rate, while Inventory is the unadjusted month-end count of homes available for sale, also expressed as Months' Supply (Inventory divided by the monthly sales pace, where 4 to 6 months is traditionally considered balanced). Price is reported as the Median Sales Price (the middle transaction price). Because the median does not hold home quality constant, a shift toward more luxury sales and fewer starter-home sales can push the median price up even if individual home values are flat.

Rate SAARin millions of units, e.g., 4.00M

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect: existing-home turnover → drives real-estate commissions, mortgage originations, moving services, and home-improvement/appliance spending, while showing how mortgage rates from 30–60 days ago constrained household mobility → shapes expectations for consumer spending and economic resilience → influences Treasury yields and risk appetite across equities and crypto. After sharp rate-hiking cycles, the report often highlights the 'mortgage rate lock-in effect', where homeowners with 3% mortgages refuse to list their homes and take on a 7% rate, freezing both supply and sales volume.

Read the release step by step

  1. Check the reference month and closing lag: open the NAR release and remember that closed sales reflect contracts signed 1 to 2 months earlier.
  2. Compare SAAR sales against consensus and prior: read the headline annualized sales pace and monthly percentage change against forecasts.
  3. Inspect Inventory and Months' Supply: check active listings and months' supply to see whether low sales stem from weak buyer demand or a shortage of listings.
  4. Separate Median Price from repeat-sales prices: compare the median transaction price against repeat-sales indices like Case-Shiller (Lesson 46) to filter out mix distortions.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If Existing-Home Sales beat expectations alongside a gradual thaw in listings and balanced months' supply, it signals improving household mobility and housing-related retail demand, which can support equities and crypto if mortgage rates and bond yields remain stable.

When a fall is plausible

If sales fall while active inventory piles up past 6 months' supply and prices drop, it points to genuine demand weakness and potential wealth-effect drag; however, low sales paired with tight 3-month supply and firm prices reflect seller lock-in rather than a housing crash.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If sales miss slightly while tight inventory keeps prices firm—or if the move was already telegraphed a month earlier by Pending Home Sales (Lesson 45)—broad equity and crypto markets often show muted reaction.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose consensus expects Existing-Home Sales at 4.00M SAAR (prior 3.96M), and actual sales print at 3.84M (-0.16M below consensus, or [3.84 - 3.96] / 3.96 ≈ -3.03% m/m). However, month-end inventory stands at 1.152M units (-10% m/m); divided by the monthly sales pace of 3.84M / 12 = 0.32M per month, Months' Supply is 1.152M / 0.32M = 3.6 months (well below a 6-month glut). Meanwhile, a shortage of starter homes and a higher share of $1M+ sales push the Median Price up from $390k to $405k (+3.85%). Low volume here reflects tight supply rather than collapsing prices.

History and origin

NAR’s research guide states that its monthly Existing Home Sales report began in 1968; this is publication history, not the origin of home resale transactions.

Where and how to find the data

National Association of Realtors NAR · Monthly · Usually 10:00 a.m. U.S. Eastern; confirm the NAR calendar.

National Association of Realtors NAR official data

  1. Open the latest Existing-Home Sales release.
  2. Read the seasonally adjusted sales pace, inventory and months supply.
  3. Separate sales volume from median price, which can change with the mix of homes sold.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Comparing closed Existing-Home Sales directly with contract-based New Home Sales (Lesson 44) in the same month without accounting for the 1-to-2-month closing lag.
  2. Interpreting a +3.8% rise in the Median Price as proof that every house appreciated 3.8%, overlooking mix shifts toward larger or higher-end homes.
  3. Equating low transaction volume with a 2008-style housing crash without checking whether Months' Supply is tight (lock-in shortage) or flooded (distressed supply).
