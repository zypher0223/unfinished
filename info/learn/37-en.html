Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index

The Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index, published monthly by The Conference Board, surveys U.S. households on their assessment of current business and job conditions as well as their expectations for business, employment, and income over the next six months. It measures subjective consumer attitudes and labor-market perceptions rather than actual dollar spending recorded at cash registers. Beginners often confuse it with the University of Michigan survey or misread an index reading of 105 as meaning 105% of consumers are optimistic.

Start with an everyday comparison

It is like polling neighborhood residents about how secure their jobs feel and whether they plan to dine out next month before opening a restaurant: upbeat answers are encouraging, but survey intentions are not paid dinner bills.

What does this number measure?

Expressed in index points benchmarked to 1985 = 100 (not percentages). The headline index combines two sub-indexes: the Present Situation Index (roughly 40% weight) and the Expectations Index (roughly 60% weight). Unlike the University of Michigan survey, which emphasizes personal finances and durable-goods buying conditions, the Conference Board survey places heavy weight on labor-market questions ('jobs plentiful' vs. 'jobs hard to get'), making it especially sensitive to employment trends.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect: shifts in household job and income confidence → alter expectations for discretionary spending and labor-market slack → reshape market probabilities of a soft landing versus a recession → move Federal Reserve rate expectations and Treasury yields, which in turn affect equity valuations and crypto risk appetite. Traders closely watch the survey's 'Labor Differential' as an early monthly clue ahead of the official unemployment report.

Read the release step by step

  1. Confirm the survey and reference month: open The Conference Board release, verify the 1985=100 benchmark, and note the survey cutoff date.
  2. Compare actual, consensus, and revised prior: check how many index points the headline beat or missed forecasts and check prior-month revisions.
  3. Separate Present Situation from Expectations: check whether both sub-indexes moved together or diverged between current comfort and future worry.
  4. Calculate the Labor Differential: subtract the percentage saying 'jobs hard to get' from 'jobs plentiful' and cross-check against retail sales and payroll data.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If both the headline and Expectations indexes beat forecasts while job perceptions stay firm without stoking wage-inflation fears, recession worries can ease and support equities and crypto, provided Treasury yields remain stable.

When a fall is plausible

If confidence drops sharply alongside a shrinking Labor Differential (more respondents saying jobs are hard to get), growth fears can weigh on risk assets; conversely, an overheating print that pushes bond yields higher can also pressure valuations, though hard spending data must be checked too.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If the headline beats forecasts solely on Present Situation strength while the forward-looking Expectations index dips (or the print matches consensus), conflicting signals often leave equities and crypto range-bound awaiting hard economic data.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose consensus expects 100.0, the prior month is revised from 101.0 to 102.0, and the actual headline prints at 105.0 (+5.0 above consensus and +3.0 above the revised prior). However, the Present Situation Index jumps from 140 to 148 (+8), while the Expectations Index slips from 76 to 75 (-1); meanwhile, 40% say jobs are plentiful and 15% say jobs are hard to get (Labor Differential = 40% - 15% = +25 percentage points). Current job conditions look solid, but forward expectations did not improve.

History and origin

The survey began in 1967; the index uses 1985 as its reference value of 100. This is survey history, not the invention of a trading rule.

Where and how to find the data

The Conference Board · Monthly · Usually the last Tuesday of the month at 10:00 a.m. U.S. Eastern; confirm the calendar.

The Conference Board official data

  1. Open the latest Consumer Confidence news release.
  2. Locate Consumer Confidence Index, Present Situation Index and Expectations Index separately.
  3. Open Technical Notes to check the reference base, survey dates and prior-month revision.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Misreading 105 index points as 105% optimism, or comparing the Conference Board's ~100 level directly against the University of Michigan's ~70 level.
  2. Treating soft survey sentiment as identical to hard retail spending receipts when consumers often spend differently than they poll.
  3. Treating a single threshold (such as Expectations below 80) as an automatic, immediate recession timer.
