New Residential Sales

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

New Residential Sales (commonly called New Home Sales), published monthly by the U.S. Census Bureau and HUD, count the number of newly built privately owned single-family homes sold at the stage of signing a sales contract or accepting a buyer deposit, along with for-sale inventory and sale prices. Although new homes represent only roughly 10%–15% of total U.S. residential transactions, counting sales at contract signing makes this series 1 to 2 months timelier than closing-based Existing-Home Sales (Lesson 43). Beginners often overlook that it covers only single-family houses (excluding condos) and carries wide monthly sampling error.

Start with an everyday comparison

Whereas Existing-Home Sales counts buyers who have already closed and moved into older homes, New Home Sales counts buyers who just signed an order and put down a deposit at a builder's sales office; showroom orders react quickly to mortgage rates, but the sample is small and buyers can still cancel later.

What does this number measure?

Sales are reported in thousands at a Seasonally Adjusted Annual Rate, accompanied by seasonally adjusted month-end Inventory (Houses for Sale—including completed homes, homes under construction, and permitted units not yet started) and Months' Supply. Both Median and Average sales prices are reported. Keep two rules in mind: first, the survey covers only new single-family houses; second, Census does not revise prior monthly sales downward if a buyer later cancels the contract.

Rate SAARe.g., 660k = 660,000 annualized units

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect: shifts in new-home contract sales and builder inventory → directly determine homebuilder cash flow, land/construction plans, and whether builders must offer price cuts or mortgage-rate buydowns to move inventory → drive future Housing Starts (Lesson 41), construction jobs, and materials demand → shape GDP growth and rate expectations → influence equities and crypto risk appetite. When existing homeowners stay locked into old low-rate mortgages, builders often use rate buydowns to capture buyer demand.

Read the release step by step

  1. Verify the reference month and contract timing: open the Census New Residential Sales release and note that it tracks single-family contract signings at a SAAR pace.
  2. Check actual, consensus, revisions, and confidence intervals: compare the headline against forecasts and revisions to the prior three months, noting the wide ±12% to ±18% 90% confidence interval.
  3. Inspect Inventory stage and Months' Supply: check total Houses for Sale, Months' Supply, and how many unsold homes are already completed versus not yet started.
  4. Evaluate Median Price and builder incentives: check whether higher unit sales came at the cost of falling median prices or heavy mortgage-rate subsidies.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If New Home Sales trend higher over several months while Months' Supply declines and median prices hold firm without heavy discounting, it signals resilient housing demand and supports builder earnings and broader risk sentiment, provided bond yields stay stable.

When a fall is plausible

If New Home Sales drop while completed unsold inventory piles up past 8–9 months' supply and prices fall, it warns that high rates are choking off buyer demand and builders will likely cut future starts and hiring, weighing on cyclical equities and risk appetite.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If a headline monthly jump falls well inside the wide ±15% sampling margin of error, offsets a downward revision to the prior month, or relies on steep price cuts that squeeze builder margins, markets typically trade sideways.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose consensus expects New Home Sales at 630k SAAR, the prior month is revised down from 620k to 600k, and the actual prints at 660k (+30k above consensus, and [660 - 600] / 600 = +10.0% m/m, with a reported 90% confidence interval of ±14.5%, meaning the gain is not statistically significant). Meanwhile, month-end inventory rises from 450k to 495k (+45k); at a monthly sales pace of 660k / 12 = 55k per month, Months' Supply is 495k / 55k = 9.0 months, and the median price is down 4% y/y. Rising backlog and price cuts temper the headline beat.

History and origin

Census historical new single-family sales extend to 1963; the data start should not be confused with an independently verified first release.

Where and how to find the data

U.S. Census Bureau / HUD · Monthly · Usually 10:00 a.m. U.S. Eastern; confirm the Census calendar.

U.S. Census Bureau / HUD official data

  1. Open Latest New Residential Sales Report.
  2. Read New Houses Sold and Houses for Sale.
  3. Check confidence intervals, revisions and the price mix before treating a monthly change as a trend.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Reacting to a single month's +8% or -8% headline move without checking the massive ±12%–18% sampling error margin and the revisions to the prior three months.
  2. Forgetting that Census does not subtract subsequent contract cancellations, which can make official New Home Sales look stronger than homebuilders' net orders when mortgage rates spike.
  3. Assuming New Residential Sales includes new condominiums when it covers 100% single-family houses.
