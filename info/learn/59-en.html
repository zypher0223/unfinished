Secured Overnight Financing Rate SOFR

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR), published jointly by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York and the Office of Financial Research (OFR), is a broad benchmark interest rate measuring the cost of borrowing cash overnight collateralized by U.S. Treasury securities in the repurchase agreement (repo) market. Because it is grounded in over a trillion dollars of actual daily Treasury repo transactions rather than survey quotes, SOFR serves as the primary thermometer for U.S. dollar overnight funding conditions.

Start with an everyday comparison

Think of SOFR as the actual volume-weighted median annualized interest rate paid to borrow cash for one night when pledging high-grade U.S. Treasury securities as collateral—reflecting real transaction tickets rather than verbal estimates.

What does this number measure?

SOFR is quoted as an annualized percentage (so a 5.00% reading is an annualized rate, not a 5% overnight charge on principal). Daily SOFR is calculated as a volume-weighted median—ranking all eligible repo transactions from lowest to highest rate and identifying the rate at which cumulative transaction volume reaches 50% of total volume—rather than a simple arithmetic mean. Readers must also distinguish daily SOFR from 30-, 90-, and 180-day compounded SOFR Averages and the SOFR Index.

Why can markets react?

SOFR underpins trillions of dollars in floating-rate corporate loans, money market instruments, and derivatives: under normal conditions it trades smoothly inside the Federal Reserve's administered rate corridor (near the ON RRP and IORB rates), but when heavy Treasury settlements, scarce bank reserves, or quarter-end dealer balance-sheet constraints create a cash squeeze, unusual SOFR spikes can force leveraged funds and market makers to deleverage, pressuring equities and crypto.

Read the release step by step

  1. Open the New York Fed SOFR page and record the annualized SOFR rate, the underlying transaction date (normally the business day prior to publication), total repo transaction volume in billions of dollars, and the 1st, 25th, 75th, and 99th percentile rates.
  2. Compare daily SOFR and its upper percentiles (75th and 99th) against the Fed's administered rates (ON RRP and IORB) and the prior day's reading to separate routine policy-rate adjustments from genuine repo funding stress.
  3. Because daily SOFR has no standardized calendar consensus forecast, evaluate moves alongside month-end/quarter-end dates, corporate or individual tax deadlines, and large Treasury settlement dates, and use any official next-day revision if published.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

When an abnormal repo funding spread subsides back inside the policy rate corridor and upper-percentile tails narrow, short-term funding stress eases and can help restore leverage and risk appetite across equities and crypto.

When a fall is plausible

When SOFR and its 99th-percentile tail spike persistently above administered policy rates without a Fed hike, signaling scarce repo cash or collateral congestion, forced deleveraging can drag down stocks and crypto assets.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

Brief, predictable a-few-basis-point bumps on quarter-end reporting dates that quickly normalize, or orderly moves matching a fully priced FOMC decision, typically have little lasting impact on broad market trends.

Work through a made-up example

Suppose the Fed's ON RRP rate is 4.00% and IORB is 4.15%, with daily SOFR normally near 4.05%, but one morning SOFR prints at 4.85%—an 80-basis-point jump (4.85% - 4.05% = 0.80%, or 80 bps; an annualized 4.85% rate equals roughly 4.85% / 360 ≈ 0.0135% for one day). That does not mean the Fed secretly hiked policy rates by 80 bps; check whether quarter-end balance-sheet limits, tax payments, or Treasury settlements caused a temporary cash squeeze and whether the spread persists. History and origin

The New York Fed began publishing SOFR in April 2018 using Treasury repo transactions; it is not simply a renamed LIBOR or a policy target.

Where and how to find the data

Federal Reserve Bank of New York · Every U.S. business day · Normally 8:00 a.m. U.S. Eastern for previous-business-day transactions; eligible revisions may follow.

Federal Reserve Bank of New York official data

  1. Open the official SOFR page.
  2. Read Rate, transaction date, Volume and Percentiles.
  3. Compare the daily rate with policy-administered rates and previous business days; read FAQs for compounded averages.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Do not confuse secured SOFR with the unsecured Effective Federal Funds Rate (EFFR), remember that daily SOFR is a volume-weighted median rather than a simple arithmetic mean, and do not extrapolate a one-day quarter-end spike into an immediate recession.
  2. Markets react to marginal new information relative to expectations. Track SOFR spreads relative to IORB and ON RRP alongside repo volumes, Treasury yields, the dollar, and broad index confirmation before acting.
