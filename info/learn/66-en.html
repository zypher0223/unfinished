Federal Reserve FOMC Rate Decision

The Federal Reserve FOMC Rate Decision, announced eight times a year on scheduled Wednesdays at 2:00 p.m. Eastern followed by the Chair's press conference at 2:30 p.m. Eastern (with the Summary of Economic Projections and Dot Plot released at the March, June, September, and December meetings), sets the target range for the overnight federal funds rate and balance-sheet (QT/QE) policy. It acts as the master pricing anchor for global U.S. dollar liquidity and risk assets. Beginners often assume any rate cut automatically sends stocks and Bitcoin higher, or that an unchanged rate means nothing happened.

Start with an everyday comparison

Think of the FOMC as the master valve operator for the dollar system: every six weeks the operator not only clicks the valve open or shut by a notch today, but also posts a penciled-in schedule for the next two years (the Dot Plot and forward guidance); because markets usually guess today's click in advance, the biggest market swings come from changes to the future schedule.

What does this number measure?

The policy rate is set as a 25-basis-point (0.25 percentage-point) target range (such as 5.00%–5.25%). Distinguish three related rates: the FOMC's Target Range, the market-traded Effective Federal Funds Rate (EFFR), and the two administered rates the Fed uses to enforce the range—Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB) near the top and the ON RRP rate near the bottom. At quarterly meetings, the Dot Plot shows the anonymous median projections of all 19 FOMC participants (7 Board Governors and 12 regional Fed Presidents, of whom 12 vote in a given year) for year-end policy rates.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is powerful and multi-channel: FOMC changes to the current target range, QT pace, Dot Plot, and statement wording → immediately reprice fed funds futures and 2-year / 10-year Treasury yields → alter corporate borrowing costs, mortgage rates, and the discount rate used to value future equity cash flows → shift interest-rate differentials that drive the U.S. Dollar Index (DXY) → flow through to equities (especially rate-sensitive growth stocks) and crypto leverage/risk appetite. Markets trade the surprise relative to pre-meeting expectations priced into rate futures, not the headline action in isolation.

Read the release step by step

  1. Check the 2:00 p.m. Statement and Implementation Note: read the target range change (in bps), the vote split (any dissents), and word-for-word edits to the inflation and labor-market paragraphs.
  2. Read the quarterly SEP and Dot Plot (Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec): check how many basis points the median dot shifted for this year and next year, alongside GDP, unemployment, and Core PCE forecasts.
  3. Listen to the 2:30 p.m. Press Conference Q&A: price action between 2:00 and 2:30 p.m. frequently reverses once the Chair answers questions about the bar for future cuts or hikes.
  4. Compare against pre-meeting pricing and bond yields: compare the full package against pre-meeting FedWatch probabilities and check whether 2-year Treasury yields and the dollar confirm the move.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If the statement, Dot Plot, or press conference delivers a more accommodative (dovish) path than priced in—and the easing reflects cooling inflation rather than an economic collapse—falling 2-year and 10-year Treasury yields and a softer dollar can support equities and crypto.

When a fall is plausible

If the FOMC delivers a hawkish surprise (such as projecting fewer future cuts in the Dot Plot or emphasizing sticky inflation) that drives Treasury yields and the dollar higher, equity valuations and crypto can come under pressure; conversely, an emergency rate cut during a crisis can also coincide with selling if recession fear dominates.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If the rate decision matches consensus 100% or pairs an expected 25-bp cut with a hawkish upward shift in next year's dots ('a hawkish cut'), stocks and crypto often whip back and forth between 2:00 and 3:00 p.m. before settling into a range.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose ahead of a September FOMC meeting, futures price a 100% chance of a 25-bp cut (from 5.25%–5.50% to 5.00%–5.25%) and expect the Dot Plot to show 4 more 25-bp cuts next year (100 bps total, putting next year's median dot at 4.125%). At 2:00 p.m., the Fed cuts 25 bps to 5.00%–5.25% as expected (0-bp surprise on today's move); however, the new Dot Plot puts next year's median dot at 4.625% (implying only 2 cuts of 25 bps = 50 bps total, 2 fewer cuts or +50 bps higher than priced in). Two-year Treasury yields jump 12 bps—a classic 'hawkish cut' where the headline says 'Fed cuts rates' yet the forward path is tighter than expected.

History and origin

The FOMC framework developed through the 1933 and 1935 banking legislation; immediate policy announcements began in 1994, not policy decisions themselves.

Where and how to find the data

Federal Reserve Board · Usually eight scheduled meetings yearly · Usually statement at 2:00 p.m. and press conference at 2:30 p.m. U.S. Eastern; unscheduled meetings differ.

Federal Reserve Board official data

  1. Choose the latest meeting in the calendar.
  2. Open Statement for the federal funds target range, then Implementation Note.
  3. If available, read Projection Materials, the dot plot and the complete Press Conference questions and answers.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Trading off a headline alert ('Fed cuts 25 bps') without checking that the cut was already 100% priced in while the Dot Plot actually removed future cuts.
  2. Treating the quarterly Dot Plot as a binding FOMC promise rather than conditional individual projections from 19 officials (including 7 non-voters that year).
  3. Chasing the initial 2:01 p.m. candle spike before the 2:30 p.m. press conference Q&A begins, or forgetting U.S. Daylight Saving Time shifts.

What the author says

The author argues that textbook claims such as ‘rate hikes make stocks fall’ play out tortuously: in a strong economy stocks can keep rising after hikes, hitting new highs before a sharp drop, as money shifts to bonds only gradually. His response is to buy only what he can exit at any moment. This lesson likewise compares the decision with expectations rather than applying a single cause and effect.
