Credit Spreads (High-Yield OAS)

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Credit spreads represent the extra default insurance premium that corporate borrowers must pay above risk-free U.S. Treasury yields. Because bondholders prioritize principal survival over upside narratives, credit spreads serve as a vital reality check on equity market rallies.

Start with an everyday comparison

A credit spread is like the extra interest rate surcharge a risky borrower pays over a government-backed loan; when banks fear defaults, that surcharge spikes.

What does this number measure?

Credit Spread = Corporate Bond Yield - Comparable-Maturity Risk-Free Treasury Yield. Because many corporate bonds contain embedded call options (allowing the issuer to repay early if rates fall), raw yield differences can be distorted. Industry standard therefore uses the Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS)—which mathematically strips out embedded option value to isolate pure default and liquidity risk, quoted in percentage points (%) or basis points (1% = 100 bps). The benchmark gauge is the ICE BofA US High Yield OAS (below BBB- rating). Note that ICE BofA indices are proprietary and subject to copyright licensing.

Why can markets react?

While equity traders focus on growth stories, corporate bondholders focus on cash flow solvency. When financial conditions tighten or recession risks mount, institutions dump junk bonds and buy safe-haven Treasuries, causing credit spreads to 'widen' (rise). Widening spreads raise corporate refinancing costs, directly pressuring indebted small-cap stocks and signaling a broader retreat from risk assets.

Read the release step by step

  1. Distinguish 'widening' from 'tightening': a rising number means spreads are widening (rising default fear, risk-off); a falling number means spreads are tightening (benign credit, risk-on).
  2. Decompose the two legs: check whether spreads widened because corporate yields surged (credit selling) or because Treasury yields plunged (safe-haven buying).
  3. Cross-check equity trends: if stocks hit new highs while High-Yield OAS quietly widens for weeks, treat the divergence as a warning that underlying credit conditions are deteriorating.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

When credit spreads widen sharply (reading spikes higher): signals mounting corporate default fears and tightening liquidity, typically pressuring equities, small caps, and crypto.

When a fall is plausible

When credit spreads tighten (reading trends lower): indicates strong confidence in corporate balance sheets and risk appetite, providing a supportive backdrop for risk assets.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When credit spreads trade flat at historically tight levels: reflects calm credit conditions, though ultra-tight spreads offer thin compensation against unexpected macro shocks.

Work through a made-up example

Illustration only: Suppose the 5-Year Treasury yield is 4.00% and the option-adjusted yield on the High-Yield corporate bond index is 7.20%. The High-Yield OAS is 7.20% - 4.00% = 3.20% (320 bps). Next month, recession fears push junk bond yields up to 8.50% while safe-haven buying pushes the 5-Year Treasury yield down to 3.70%. The credit spread widens sharply to 8.50% - 3.70% = 4.80% (480 bps), an increase of 160 bps. These figures are hypothetical.

History and origin

Merrill Lynch built its high-yield bond index family in the 1980s; it later passed to Bank of America and is now run by ICE as the ICE BofA US High Yield Index. FRED carries the daily option-adjusted spread (BAMLH0A0HYM2), but since April 2026 it only shows the latest 3 years; longer history requires the source.

Where and how to find the data

ICE Data Indices, LLC · Each trading day (updated daily after market close) · Corporate bond prices move continuously during trading hours; ICE calculates end-of-day index values after the U.S. close, and FRED typically updates the prior day's Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) the following morning.

LLC ICE BofA Indicespublished via St. Louis Fed FRED

FRED: ICE BofA US High Yield Index Option-Adjusted Spread (BAMLH0A0HYM2)

  1. Open the FRED BAMLH0A0HYM2 series page to check the latest daily OAS reading (quoted in percentage points, where 3.50% = 350 bps).
  2. FRED shows only the latest 3 years; compare recent calm and stress periods there, and use the source or research reports for older crisis peaks (above 20% in 2008, above 10% in March 2020).
  3. Overlay equity indices to see whether widening credit spreads confirm or diverge from stock market moves.

FRED: ICE BofA US Corporate Index Option-Adjusted Spread (BAMLC0A0CM)

  1. Open the FRED BAMLC0A0CM series to inspect Option-Adjusted Spreads for Investment Grade (BBB and above) corporate bonds.
  2. Compare High-Yield OAS against Investment-Grade OAS to see if credit stress is isolated to weak borrowers or spreading to blue-chip firms.
  3. Note the copyright notice: ICE BofA indices are proprietary benchmarks subject to redistribution restrictions.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Mistaking a rising ('widening') credit spread for a bullish signal: like the VIX, credit spreads are an inverted stress gauge—higher numbers mean higher default risk and tighter liquidity.
  2. Assuming ultra-low credit spreads are the safest time to buy high-yield bonds: historically tight spreads mean investors are receiving minimal compensation for default risk.
