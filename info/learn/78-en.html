Total Social Financing (TSF)

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Looking only at bank loans misses massive funding channels like government bonds, corporate bonds, and equity issuance. Total Social Financing (TSF) is China's comprehensive credit gauge, measuring total financing provided by the entire financial system to the real economy from the borrower's liability perspective.

Start with an everyday comparison

If the real economy is farmland, bank loans are just the main canal, whereas Total Social Financing (TSF) is the total irrigation water combining canals, government bond wells, and corporate bond streams.

What does this number measure?

Total Social Financing (TSF) measures the aggregate volume of funds obtained by the real economy (non-financial corporations, households, and government) from the financial system over a period (monthly flow increment) or at a point in time (end-of-month stock). Its four main pillars are: 1. On-balance-sheet loans (RMB and foreign currency loans); 2. Net Government Bond financing (central + local government general and special bonds); 3. Direct financing (corporate bonds + domestic equity issuance); and 4. Off-balance-sheet ('shadow banking') financing (entrusted loans, trust loans, and undiscounted bankers' acceptances). Core distinction vs. M2: TSF and M2 are two sides of the banking balance sheet—TSF tracks the 'asset/credit deployment side' (how much the real economy borrowed), while M2 tracks the 'liability/money supply side' (how much cash and deposits households and firms hold in banks).

Why can markets react?

China's macro cycle is heavily credit-driven, making TSF stock YoY growth and loan composition key barometers for Chinese equities, bonds, and industrial commodities. Strong TSF growth led by medium-to-long-term corporate and household loans signals genuine capex and housing recovery; conversely, TSF propped up solely by short-term bill discounting or government bond front-loading indicates sluggish private credit demand.

Read the release step by step

  1. Always compare Year-over-Year (YoY) rather than Month-over-Month (MoM): Chinese banks heavily front-load loan quotas in January and Q1 ('New Year credit surge') while April and October are seasonally tiny; always check whether monthly flow is 'up or down YoY'.
  2. Inspect credit quality composition: check how much came from 'Corporate Medium-to-Long-Term Loans' (real capex) and 'Household Medium-to-Long-Term Loans' (mortgages) versus 'Short-Term Bill Financing' (used by banks to artificially pad loan quotas).
  3. Compare the 'TSF YoY minus M2 YoY' gap: if M2 growth significantly outpaces TSF growth, liquidity is pooling as idle savings inside the banking system rather than flowing into real economic investment.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

When TSF beats expectations (strong YoY increase) with solid medium-to-long-term corporate and household loans: signals effective credit expansion and recovering demand, typically supporting A-share/HK cyclicals and commodities while pressuring government bond prices.

When a fall is plausible

When TSF misses expectations (shrinking YoY) or relies heavily on short-term bill discounting: reflects weak private borrowing appetite, fueling bond market rallies on rate-cut hopes while weighing on cyclical equities.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When TSF stock YoY growth moves sideways supported primarily by steady government bond issuance: indicates a policy-stabilized floor without strong private credit acceleration.

Work through a made-up example

Illustration only: Suppose China's new TSF flow prints at 6.50 trillion RMB in January, then drops to 1.50 trillion RMB in February. A beginner looking at the -77% MoM drop might panic; however, January is always the peak front-loading month while February is shortened by Lunar New Year. If last year's February TSF was 1.30 trillion RMB, this February actually achieved a +200 billion RMB YoY increase (1.50T - 1.30T), showing resilient credit flow. These figures are hypothetical.

History and origin

The PBOC began publishing the TSF flow in 2011 and later added the outstanding stock; local government special bonds were included from September 2018, and central government bonds and local general bonds from December 2019, forming today's 'government bonds' item.

Where and how to find the data

People's Bank of China (PBOC) · Monthly · Usually released mid-month (around the 10th–15th) together with or close to the financial statistics report (M2, new RMB loans); the exact day and time vary, so check the PBOC website.

People's Bank of China: Statistics and Analysis - TSF Reports

  1. Open the PBOC Statistics Department portal and select the monthly TSF Stock and TSF Increment reports.
  2. Check the two headline numbers: end-of-month TSF stock YoY growth (%) and monthly TSF flow increment (in RMB billions/trillions).
  3. Examine the structural breakdown: compare RMB loans to the real economy, net government bond financing, corporate bonds, and off-balance-sheet items against the same period last year.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Comparing monthly TSF flows sequentially (MoM): Chinese credit follows a rigid seasonal quota rhythm (huge in Jan/Mar/Jun, small in Apr/Jul/Oct); sequential comparisons are meaningless without YoY benchmarking.
  2. Focusing only on the headline TSF number while ignoring composition: a headline beat driven entirely by short-term bank bill discounting and government bond issuance masks weak private-sector capex and mortgage demand.
