Consumer Credit G.19

Consumer Credit (the G.19 statistical release), published monthly by the Federal Reserve Board about five weeks after the reference month, measures outstanding U.S. household debt that is not secured by real estate, split into Revolving credit (mainly credit cards) and Nonrevolving credit (such as auto loans and student loans). It tracks non-mortgage household borrowing balances and monthly net flows. Beginners often confuse seasonally adjusted annualized growth rates with single-month dollar increases, or assume G.19 includes home mortgages.

Start with an everyday comparison

It is like checking a household's non-mortgage tab: auto and student loans are fixed installment plans for big-ticket items (nonrevolving), while credit cards are a revolving tab for everyday expenses; however, a rising tab can reflect either confident big-ticket buying or stressed families leaning on credit cards to pay bills.

What does this number measure?

The release reports three distinct figures: total Consumer Credit Outstanding (a stock balance in billions of dollars), the monthly net dollar change (a monthly flow in billions of dollars), and the Seasonally Adjusted Annual Rate (SAAR %) of change. If a $5,000B balance grows by $10B in one month, the monthly flow is +$10B (+0.2% unannualized), while the annualized rate is ($10B × 12) / $5,000B = +2.4% SAAR. G.19 strictly excludes residential mortgages and home-equity lines of credit.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect and lagging: trends in consumer borrowing → reveal household willingness to finance purchases and bank willingness to extend credit → shape expectations for retail revenues and bank loan losses → feed into Federal Reserve assessments of financial conditions, bond yields, and equity/crypto risk appetite. Because G.19 arrives roughly five weeks after the month ends (well after Retail Sales and PCE), markets use it primarily to verify whether prior spending was backed by healthy income or stretched credit-card borrowing.

Read the release step by step

  1. Verify the reference month and units: open the Fed's G.19 release, note the five-week lag, and separate dollar balances (Billions) from annualized percentage rates (SAAR %).
  2. Split Revolving from Nonrevolving: compare credit-card borrowing (Revolving) against auto and student loan borrowing (Nonrevolving).
  3. Compare actual, consensus, and revisions: check the monthly net dollar change against consensus forecasts and prior-month revisions.
  4. Cross-check saving and delinquency rates: pair G.19 with the Personal Saving Rate (Lesson 36) and Loan Delinquency Rates (Lesson 40) to judge borrower health.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If both nonrevolving auto credit and revolving credit expand moderately alongside rising incomes and low delinquencies, it suggests healthy credit availability and durable consumer demand, provided borrowing rates are not spiking.

When a fall is plausible

If total credit contracts abruptly due to bank credit tightening, or if high-interest credit-card debt surges while the saving rate is depleted and delinquencies rise, it can raise concerns over consumer strain and bank credit losses, though you must check timelier spending indicators.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If the headline dollar change is close to consensus or rising card balances offset softer auto loans, equities and crypto typically show little immediate reaction given the report's five-week reporting lag.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose last month Revolving credit was $1,000B and Nonrevolving credit was $2,000B, for a total of $1,000B + $2,000B = $3,000B. This month Revolving rises to $1,020B (+$20B monthly flow, or [$20B × 12] / $1,000B = +24% SAAR), while Nonrevolving falls to $1,990B (-$10B monthly flow, or [-$10B × 12] / $2,000B = -6% SAAR), bringing total credit to $3,010B (+$10B net flow, or +4% SAAR). The +$10B headline masks falling big-ticket loans and rising credit-card reliance.

History and origin

G.19 evolved from longstanding Federal Reserve credit statistics; official archives describe three predecessor releases in 1942–1952. That is not necessarily the first date of the present release.

Where and how to find the data

Federal Reserve Board · Monthly · Usually about five weeks after the reference month, often at 3:00 p.m. U.S. Eastern; verify the official calendar.

Federal Reserve Board official data

  1. Open the latest G.19 Consumer Credit release.
  2. Read Consumer Credit Outstanding, then Revolving and Nonrevolving components.
  3. Check the annual-rate and billions-of-dollars headings before interpreting a monthly flow.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating G.19 as total household debt when it excludes residential mortgages (which make up the largest share of household liabilities).
  2. Misreading a +12% annualized growth rate (SAAR) as a 12% jump in a single month (when the one-month increase is roughly 1%).
  3. Assuming rising consumer credit is always bullish or always bearish without checking whether it comes from confident auto buying or distressed card borrowing.
