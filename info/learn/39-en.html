Consumer Credit G.19

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

G.19 measures consumer credit, including revolving credit cards and nonrevolving auto or student loans, generally excluding real-estate-secured debt.

Start with an everyday comparison

Credit lets a household buy today and repay principal and interest later.

What does this number measure?

Separate outstanding balances, monthly changes and annualized rates. An annual-rate flow is not the actual dollar increase in one month.

Why can markets react?

Credit can sustain spending and revenue, but excessive debt weakens household resilience. Stocks and crypto respond through earnings, financing and risk appetite.

Read the release step by step

  1. Read total credit and the table units.
  2. Separate revolving from nonrevolving credit.
  3. Compare revisions and expectations with income and delinquency.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

Moderate borrowing backed by income and low defaults may support consumption.

When a fall is plausible

A lending freeze or debt-funded spending with rising delinquency may depress risk assets.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

Expected totals and offsetting components may produce little broad-market response.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical billions: cards rise from 100 to 102, other credit falls from 200 to 199. Total rises only one; this does not show broad demand strength. Rising defaults would change the interpretation.

History and origin

G.19 evolved from longstanding Federal Reserve credit statistics; official archives describe three predecessor releases in 1942–1952. That is not necessarily the first date of the present release.

Where and how to find the data

Federal Reserve Board · Monthly · Usually about five weeks after the reference month, often at 3:00 p.m. U.S. Eastern; verify the official calendar.

Federal Reserve Board official data

  1. Open the latest G.19 Consumer Credit release.
  2. Read Consumer Credit Outstanding, then Revolving and Nonrevolving components.
  3. Check the annual-rate and billions-of-dollars headings before interpreting a monthly flow.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. This is not total household debt or mortgages.
  2. Less credit can mean voluntary repayment or inability to borrow.
