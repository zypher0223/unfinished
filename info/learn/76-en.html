China Consumer Price Index (CPI)

China's Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures retail price changes across a basket of goods and services purchased by urban and rural households. Because food carries a heavy weight in China's basket, supply swings in pork or vegetables often mask underlying domestic demand, making Core CPI essential.

Start with an everyday comparison

Reading China's headline CPI is like weighing yourself while holding a wet sponge (food prices); squeezing out the sponge (checking Core CPI) reveals true demand.

What does this number measure?

Compiled monthly by the NBS across eight major categories, CPI is tracked via three metrics: 1. Year-over-Year (YoY %), measuring annual change against the same month last year; 2. Month-over-Month (MoM %), measuring sequential change from the prior month; and 3. Core CPI, which strips out volatile food and energy prices. The primary structural difference versus U.S. CPI is that U.S. CPI is dominated by Shelter (over one-third of the basket), whereas China's CPI assigns a much higher weight to Food, Tobacco, and Liquor.

Why can markets react?

CPI gauges domestic consumption health and monetary policy room. Persistently near-zero or negative Core CPI signals sluggish household demand and corporate price-cutting pressure, increasing expectations for PBOC rate cuts and fiscal stimulus (bullish for Chinese government bonds). Conversely, a steady recovery in Core CPI indicates healing household demand and improving corporate profit margins.

Read the release step by step

  1. Isolate food and pork supply noise: check the NBS commentary to see how many percentage points pork and fresh vegetables contributed to the headline YoY figure.
  2. Focus on Core CPI and Services CPI: stripping out food and energy reveals whether underlying household consumption and service demand are genuinely expanding.
  3. Account for Lunar New Year calendar shifts: because Spring Festival shifts between January and February each year, single-month YoY prints in Jan/Feb are heavily distorted by base effects and should be averaged together.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

When China CPI (especially Core CPI) rebounds moderately from low levels: signals healing domestic demand and easing deflationary pressure, often supporting A-share and HK consumer/cyclical equities while weighing slightly on bond prices.

When a fall is plausible

When CPI and Core CPI print below expectations near zero: highlights weak aggregate demand, boosting expectations for monetary easing (LPR/RRR cuts) and supporting Chinese government bonds.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When CPI hovers steadily in a benign 1%–2% band: indicates price stability without constraining PBOC policy, leaving markets to focus on sector fundamentals.

Work through a made-up example

Illustration only: Suppose headline China CPI rises +0.4% YoY in a given month. However, the NBS breakdown shows surging pork and vegetable prices from bad weather contributed +0.5 percentage points to headline CPI, while Core CPI (excluding food and energy) actually fell -0.1% YoY. This reveals that positive headline inflation was purely supply-driven while underlying consumer demand remained soft. These figures are hypothetical.

History and origin

China's NBS compiled worker living-cost price indices in the 1950s and began a national consumer price index in the mid-1980s. From 2001 it adopted international practice with a fixed-base index (base year 2000), rotating the base and the weights of eight expenditure categories every five years.

Where and how to find the data

National Bureau of Statistics of China (NBS) · Monthly · Typically released around the 9th or 10th of each month at 9:30 AM Beijing Time (UTC+8) covering the prior month, subject to the official NBS release calendar.

National Bureau of Statistics of China: Press Releases & CPI Data

  1. Open the NBS 'Latest Releases' portal and select the monthly Consumer Price Index press release and official commentary.
  2. Check the headline figures first: Year-over-Year (YoY) and Month-over-Month (MoM) percentage changes.
  3. Inspect the category breakdown table: focus on food items (especially pork and fresh vegetables) and 'Core CPI excluding food and energy'.

National Bureau of Statistics: CPI Methodology & Knowledge Base

  1. Review the official NBS methodological guide detailing the eight major expenditure categories.
  2. Understand how base-year weight rotations and carryover effects influence year-over-year readings.
  3. Distinguish carryover base effects from new monthly price changes.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Overreacting to wild YoY swings in January or February: Lunar New Year timing shifts between Jan and Feb create massive base-effect distortions that reverse the following month.
  2. Assuming a pork-driven CPI spike will force the PBOC to hike interest rates: policymakers look through single-commodity supply shocks and focus on Core CPI and employment.
