China Official Manufacturing PMI

China's Official Manufacturing PMI is the earliest monthly macroeconomic indicator, released on the very last day of the current month. Rather than counting raw output tonnage, it surveys 3,200 purchasing managers on whether business conditions improved, stayed the same, or worsened compared to the prior month.

Start with an everyday comparison

The PMI is like a month-end show-of-hands poll among 3,200 factory purchasing bosses: if more than half report better conditions than last month, the index tops 50.

What does this number measure?

PMI is a Diffusion Index (0–100%): Sub-index = (% Reporting Improvement) + 0.5 * (% Reporting No Change). The 50.0% mark is the expansion/contraction threshold: readings above 50 indicate month-over-month expansion, while readings below 50 indicate contraction. Headline Manufacturing PMI is a weighted composite of five sub-indices: New Orders (30%), Production (25%), Employment (20%), Supplier Delivery Times (15%, inverted), and Raw Material Inventories (10%). Key distinction: the Official NBS PMI surveys 3,200 firms across China with heavy representation of large/medium state-owned and heavy industrial enterprises; the private S&P Global survey (branded Caixin until July 2025 and RatingDog since) sample ~650 firms tilted toward smaller, private, export-oriented coastal manufacturers.

Why can markets react?

Because hard data like Industrial Production and retail sales are not released until mid-next month, the month-end PMI sets the immediate market tone for China's growth momentum. A surprise rebound above 50—especially led by New Orders and Output Prices—typically supports Chinese equities, industrial metals (copper, iron ore), and the Yuan; weak readings below 50 fuel expectations for PBOC/fiscal stimulus and bond rallies.

Read the release step by step

  1. Remember it is a sequential (MoM) gauge: a reading of 50.4% down from 51.0% still means the sector grew compared to last month, just at a slower pace.
  2. Compare New Orders against Production: if Production exceeds 52% while New Orders sit below 49%, factories are producing faster than demand, risking inventory gluts.
  3. Compare Output Prices against Input Prices: if raw material input prices surge while factory-gate output prices lag, downstream manufacturing profit margins are being squeezed.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

When Official Manufacturing PMI beats expectations above 50.0% (led by New Orders): signals expanding industrial activity, typically supporting cyclical equities and industrial commodities while weighing on government bonds.

When a fall is plausible

When PMI drops below 50.0% and misses consensus: indicates sequential contraction in manufacturing, pressuring cyclical assets while strengthening expectations for counter-cyclical policy easing.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When PMI hovers tightly around 49.8%–50.2%: suggests a fragile equilibrium where investors focus on divergence between Large enterprises and Small/Medium enterprises.

Work through a made-up example

Illustration only: Suppose out of 3,200 surveyed firms, 35% report higher new orders than last month, 40% report unchanged orders, and 25% report lower orders. The New Orders diffusion index is 35% + 0.5 * 40% = 55.0% (expansion). If the Official PMI prints at 50.8% while the private Caixin PMI prints at 49.2%, this divergence reflects domestic infrastructure supporting large heavy industries (NBS sample) while smaller coastal exporters (private sample) face softer foreign demand. These figures are hypothetical.

History and origin

The NBS and CFLP have jointly published China's official manufacturing PMI since January 2005; the current manufacturing survey covers 3,200 firms across 31 industry divisions.

Where and how to find the data

NBS Service Survey Center and China Federation of Logistics & Purchasing (CFLP) · Monthly · Typically released on the last day of each month (30th or 31st) at 9:30 AM Beijing Time (UTC+8) covering the current month, making it the earliest monthly macro gauge.

National Bureau of Statistics of China: Monthly PMI Press Releases

  1. Open the NBS 'Latest Releases' portal on the final day of the month to view the official PMI report.
  2. Check whether the headline Manufacturing PMI is above or below the 50.0% threshold, and compare Large, Medium, and Small enterprise breakdowns.
  3. Inspect the sub-indices: focus on New Orders (demand), Production (supply), and Input vs. Output Prices (margin pressure).

China Federation of Logistics & Purchasing (CFLP): PMI Portal

  1. Visit the CFLP portal to review Manufacturing PMI, Non-Manufacturing Business Activity Index, and Composite PMI.
  2. Review sector-specific PMI reports (e.g., steel, chemicals) for upstream and downstream granularity.
  3. Compare the end-of-month Official PMI against the early-next-month private (Caixin/S&P Global) PMI.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Assuming divergence between the Official PMI and Caixin PMI means one is fabricated: their sample sizes (3,200 vs. 650), enterprise scales (large SOEs vs. small private exporters), and geographic weights differ by design.
  2. Over-interpreting Lunar New Year seasonal dips: despite seasonal adjustments, factory shutdowns during January/February and post-holiday reopenings in March cause sharp mechanical swings.
