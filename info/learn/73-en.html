U.S. Dollar Index (DXY)

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The U.S. Dollar Index (DXY) measures the strength of the U.S. dollar against a basket of six major foreign currencies. Because global commodities, cross-border debt, and major crypto stablecoins are denominated in USD, DXY shifts reflect global dollar liquidity conditions.

Start with an everyday comparison

DXY is like the calibration weight on a global scale; when the dollar unit itself gets heavier, assets priced in dollars often face downward pressure.

What does this number measure?

Indexed to March 1973 = 100.00, DXY uses a fixed geometric mean of six developed currencies: Euro (57.6%), Japanese Yen (13.6%), British Pound (11.9%), Canadian Dollar (9.1%), Swedish Krona (4.2%), and Swiss Franc (3.6%). By contrast, the Federal Reserve's Nominal Broad Dollar Index dynamically weights 26 currencies—including the Chinese Yuan and Mexican Peso—based on actual bilateral trade flows.

Why can markets react?

First, denominator effect: USD-priced commodities (gold, oil) and crypto become more expensive for non-U.S. buyers when DXY rises. Second, corporate earnings translation: S&P 500 multinationals suffer FX headwinds when converting foreign revenue back into a strong dollar. Third, emerging market capital flows: a surging dollar tightens offshore USD debt servicing and can draw capital away from emerging market equities.

Read the release step by step

  1. Decompose basket drivers: since EUR (57.6%) and JPY (13.6%) make up over 71% of DXY, check whether a DXY move stems from U.S. strength or European/Japanese weakness.
  2. Cross-check the Fed Broad Dollar Index (DTWEXBGS): when analyzing Asian or emerging markets, supplement DXY with the trade-weighted Broad Index that includes CNY and MXN.
  3. Watch for regime shifts in correlations: during geopolitical shocks, safe-haven flows can push both DXY and gold higher simultaneously, breaking their textbook inverse correlation.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

When DXY rallies sharply: typically reflects widening U.S. rate advantages or global risk-off 'dash for cash', which can create headwinds for commodities, non-U.S. equities, and crypto.

When a fall is plausible

When DXY trends lower: often signals Fed easing or stronger non-U.S. growth, easing global financial conditions and potentially supporting commodities and emerging market assets.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When DXY trades flat: currency translation and cross-border liquidity pressures subside, leaving individual markets to trade on domestic fundamentals.

Work through a made-up example

Illustration only: Suppose DXY sits at 100.00. If the European Central Bank cuts rates while the Fed holds steady, causing the Euro (57.6% weight) to depreciate by 5% against the USD while the other five currencies remain unchanged, DXY will rise by roughly 5% * 57.6% = 2.88% to around 102.88. These figures are hypothetical.

History and origin

Created in March 1973 with a base value of 100.00 following the end of the Bretton Woods system, DXY futures launched in November 1985; the basket was updated in 1999 upon the introduction of the Euro into its current six-currency fixed geometric weighting.

Where and how to find the data

Intercontinental Exchange (ICE, for DXY) and Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (for Broad Dollar Index) · Continuous intraday during global FX trading hours (FRED Broad Dollar Index updated daily/weekly) · DXY is calculated in real time 24 hours a day from Sunday evening to Friday 5:00 PM ET; the Federal Reserve's Nominal Broad U.S. Dollar Index (DTWEXBGS) updates daily with a reporting lag on FRED.

Intercontinental Exchange (ICE): U.S. Dollar Index Futures & Methodology

  1. Visit the ICE U.S. Dollar Index portal to view contract specifications and methodology.
  2. Verify the fixed currency weights: Euro (57.6%), Japanese Yen (13.6%), British Pound (11.9%), Canadian Dollar (9.1%), Swedish Krona (4.2%), and Swiss Franc (3.6%).
  3. Note that because the Euro accounts for 57.6% of the basket, DXY heavily mirrors EUR/USD movements.

FRED: Nominal Broad U.S. Dollar Index (DTWEXBGS)

  1. Open the FRED DTWEXBGS page to view the trade-weighted Nominal Broad Dollar Index covering 26 major trading partner currencies.
  2. Compare DTWEXBGS against DXY to see if dollar strength is broad-based across emerging markets or concentrated against the Euro and Yen.
  3. Overlay Federal Reserve policy rate cycles to evaluate multi-month dollar trends.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Assuming DXY measures the dollar against all world currencies: DXY excludes the Chinese Yuan, Mexican Peso, and Australian Dollar, acting largely as an inverse Euro index.
  2. Relying on a rigid 'strong dollar = falling stocks/gold' rule: correlations frequently flip during sovereign crises or U.S.-led technological booms.
