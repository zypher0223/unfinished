Reserve Balances of Depository Institutions

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Reserve Balances of Depository Institutions (commonly called Bank Reserves), published weekly in the Federal Reserve's H.4.1 release and monthly in the H.6 release, measure the total dollar balances that commercial banks and other depository institutions hold in their master accounts at Federal Reserve Banks. They represent the banking system's foundational high-powered settlement cash used for interbank clearing and liquidity buffers—NOT retail checking or savings deposits held by households and businesses at commercial banks. Beginners often confuse bank reserves with M2 retail deposits or assume an extra $100B in reserves automatically forces banks to lend $100B into stocks.

Start with an everyday comparison

If household checking deposits are store gift cards held by shoppers, Bank Reserves are the internal settlement accounts that individual store owners keep at the mall's central clearing office (the Fed) to settle payments with each other at the end of the day; shoppers never touch that central clearing account directly.

What does this number measure?

Reported in millions of dollars in H.4.1 Table 1 (where 3,300,000 equals $3.3 trillion), shown as both Wednesday levels and weekly averages. By the Fed's balance-sheet identity: Bank Reserves ≈ Total Fed Assets - Currency in Circulation - Treasury General Account (TGA) - Overnight Reverse Repo (ON RRP) - Other Liabilities. Consequently, even when Total Fed Assets stay completely flat, money flowing out of TGA (government spending) or out of ON RRP automatically increases Bank Reserves dollar-for-dollar, whereas tax payments into TGA drain Bank Reserves. Since 2008, the Fed pays Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB) under an 'ample reserves' regime.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect: the total level and distribution of bank reserves across large and small banks → govern cash availability in interbank and Treasury repo funding markets → if reserves drop from 'ample' toward 'scarce', banks hoard liquidity and overnight repo rates (like SOFR) spike above the Fed's IORB rate (as occurred in September 2019) → forcing leveraged funds and dealers to unwind positions and pressuring the Fed to halt QT → directly impacting bond markets, equities, and crypto liquidity.

Read the release step by step

  1. Locate Reserve Balances in H.4.1: open Table 1 of Thursday's H.4.1 release, choose Wednesday levels or weekly averages consistently, and convert millions into trillions.
  2. Decompose the balance-sheet identity: compare weekly changes in Reserves against changes in Fed Securities (QT), the TGA (Lesson 54), and ON RRP (Lesson 53) to see what drove the move.
  3. Account for calendar and tax dates: note major corporate/individual tax deadlines (such as mid-April, mid-June, mid-September) and Treasury settlement dates that temporarily pull reserves into the TGA.
  4. Verify scarcity using money-market spreads: always check the spread between SOFR (market repo rate) and IORB (Fed interest on reserves) to see whether a drop in reserves is causing actual funding stress.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If Bank Reserves rebound as Treasury spending (falling TGA) or ON RRP outflows replenish bank accounts while SOFR trades calmly near IORB, the thicker liquidity buffer eases funding-stress fears and supports equities and crypto.

When a fall is plausible

If ongoing QT and a depleted ON RRP buffer pull Bank Reserves down toward scarcity thresholds—accompanied by persistent spikes in SOFR above IORB—tightening dollar funding conditions can force deleveraging across bonds, stocks, and crypto.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If Bank Reserves dip by $50B–$100B on a routine tax date or mid-month Treasury settlement while remaining well within the ample range and leaving repo rates untouched, broad markets rarely react.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose in a given week Total Fed Assets stay flat at $7,000B and currency is unchanged. Because of the April 15 tax deadline, households and firms pay $150B in taxes from their bank accounts to the U.S. Treasury, lifting the TGA from $700B to $850B (+$150B); meanwhile, ON RRP declines from $400B to $350B (-$50B). By the balance-sheet identity, the weekly change in Bank Reserves is ΔAssets ($0B) - ΔTGA (+$150B) - ΔRRP (-$50B) = -$100B (reserves drop from $3,400B to $3,300B). If SOFR stays steady relative to IORB, this $100B drop is a normal tax-season transfer rather than a liquidity crisis.

History and origin

Reserves arise from the central bank settlement system. Interest on reserves began in 2008 and the ample-reserves framework changed implementation.

Where and how to find the data

Federal Reserve Board · Weekly and monthly · H.4.1 usually Thursday at 4:30 p.m. U.S. Eastern; additional monthly data are in H.6.

Federal Reserve Board official data

  1. Open H.4.1 Table 1.
  2. Find Reserve balances with Federal Reserve Banks.
  3. Compare Wednesday levels with weekly averages and inspect TGA and reverse-repo movements.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Adding commercial bank Reserves at the Fed to household M2 bank deposits as if they were the same pool of money, or thinking retail stock buying directly spends Fed reserves.
  2. Relying on outdated textbook 'money multiplier' math (Reserves × 10 = Loans) when reserve requirements in the U.S. are 0% and bank lending is constrained by capital ratios, credit standards, and loan demand.
  3. Focusing only on the aggregate national reserve number while ignoring how reserves are distributed between cash-rich money-center banks and smaller regional banks.
