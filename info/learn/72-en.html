U.S. Treasury Yields and the Yield Curve

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

U.S. Treasury yields act as the gravitational benchmark for global asset pricing. Bond prices and yields move inversely: when buyers bid up Treasury prices, yields fall; when sellers dump bonds, yields rise. Tracking the 2-year and 10-year yields and the curve shape is essential for reading macro liquidity.

Start with an everyday comparison

Treasury yields are the baseline rental cost of money; when baseline rent spikes, high-multiple stocks and crypto face steeper discounting pressure.

What does this number measure?

Yield to Maturity (expressed as an annualized percentage %, where 1 basis point = 0.01%) measures the annualized return of holding a bond to maturity. The 2-Year yield closely tracks Federal Reserve policy rate expectations, while the 10-Year yield reflects long-term growth expectations, inflation outlook, and term premium. The term spread is commonly measured as the 10-Year yield minus the 2-Year yield.

Why can markets react?

The 10-Year Treasury yield serves as the global risk-free discount rate denominator. Rapidly rising yields reduce the present value of future cash flows, often pressuring growth equities and non-yielding crypto assets. When the 2-Year yield exceeds the 10-Year yield (an inverted curve with a negative spread), the market is pricing tight near-term Fed policy that may eventually cool the economy and force future rate cuts.

Read the release step by step

  1. Check level and basis-point changes: compare daily moves in the 2-Year and 10-Year yields to see whether short-end policy expectations or long-end term premiums are driving action.
  2. Calculate the 10Y-2Y spread: subtract the 2-Year yield from the 10-Year yield; positive values indicate a normal upward slope, while negative values indicate inversion.
  3. Monitor post-inversion re-steepening: historically, economic recessions rarely start on the first day of inversion, but often emerge months later when the curve un-inverts as short rates plunge.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

When yields rise sharply (bond prices fall): often reflects sticky inflation, hawkish Fed repricing, or heavy Treasury supply, which may pressure equity valuations and support the U.S. dollar.

When a fall is plausible

When yields fall notably (bond prices rise): if driven by cooling inflation, it may support risk asset multiples; if driven by recession panic and safe-haven buying, equities may fall alongside yields.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When yields trade sideways in a narrow band: indicates temporary consensus around the rate trajectory, allowing equities and crypto to trade on idiosyncratic fundamentals.

Work through a made-up example

Illustration only: Suppose the 2-Year Treasury yield is 4.50% and the 10-Year Treasury yield is 4.10%. The 10Y-2Y spread is 4.10% - 4.50% = -0.40% (-40 bps), meaning the yield curve is inverted. If dovish guidance next month pushes the 2-Year yield down to 3.80% while the 10-Year edges to 4.00%, the spread turns to +0.20% (+20 bps), un-inverting the curve. These figures are hypothetical.

History and origin

The U.S. Treasury has systematically compiled Constant Maturity Treasury (CMT) yield data since 1962; economist Campbell Harvey formally documented in his 1986 dissertation how yield curve inversions historically preceded U.S. recessions.

Where and how to find the data

U.S. Department of the Treasury and Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED) · Each trading day (intraday continuous in secondary markets; official par yield curve published daily) · Fluctuates continuously during secondary market trading hours; the U.S. Treasury typically publishes official closing Daily Treasury Par Yield Curve Rates in the evening (US Eastern Time) each trading day, subject to official schedules.

U.S. Department of the Treasury: Daily Treasury Par Yield Curve Rates

  1. Open the U.S. Treasury Interest Rate Statistics portal and select 'Daily Treasury Par Yield Curve Rates'.
  2. Compare the daily yield percentages across the 1-Month, 2-Year, and 10-Year maturity columns.
  3. Check whether the 10-Year yield sits above or below the 2-Year yield to identify normal or inverted curve shapes.

FRED: 10-Year Minus 2-Year Treasury Constant Maturity Spread (T10Y2Y)

  1. Navigate to the FRED T10Y2Y series page displaying the 10-Year minus 2-Year Treasury spread.
  2. Use the 0.00% horizontal line as the threshold; values below zero indicate an inverted yield curve.
  3. Compare inversions and subsequent re-steepenings against NBER recession shading bars.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating a yield curve inversion as an immediate stock-market short signal: lead times between inversion and historical recessions have varied widely from 6 to 24 months, with occasional false positives.
  2. Confusing bond prices with bond yields: a 'Treasury rally' means bond prices rose, which mathematically means Treasury yields fell.
