Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey PMMS

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

The Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey (PMMS), published every Thursday at noon Eastern by Freddie Mac, reports the weekly U.S. national average interest rate on the benchmark 30-Year Fixed-Rate Mortgage (and 15-Year Fixed-Rate Mortgage). It serves as the primary public barometer of U.S. household home-borrowing costs, measuring long-term retail mortgage financing rates rather than the Federal Reserve's overnight policy rate. Beginners often assume that if the Fed cuts its overnight rate by 25 basis points, the 30-year mortgage rate automatically drops by 25 basis points the next day.

Start with an everyday comparison

If the Federal Reserve's federal funds rate is the overnight wholesale rate between banks, the 30-year mortgage rate is the 30-year retail installment rate charged to homebuyers; retail 30-year mortgage rates track the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield plus a mortgage risk/prepayment spread rather than copying the overnight rate.

What does this number measure?

Reported as an annual percentage rate (%, such as 6.80%), with weekly changes measured in basis points (bps, where 1.00 percentage point = 100 bps, so moving from 6.50% to 6.80% is +30 bps). Since November 2022, Freddie Mac calculates PMMS directly from thousands of actual purchase loan applications submitted through its Loan Product Advisor system rather than surveying lenders by phone. Note the sample criteria: it reflects conventional, conforming purchase loans for borrowers with strong credit and a 20% down payment (80% Loan-to-Value), not an individualized quote for every borrower.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect: inflation and monetary policy expectations move the 10-year Treasury yield and Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS) spreads → setting the 30-year mortgage rate → directly altering monthly principal-and-interest payments (housing affordability) and homeowner mobility → driving home sales, construction starts, and durable-goods spending → feeding back into GDP growth, bond yields, and equity/crypto risk appetite. A useful rule of thumb is: 30-Year Mortgage Rate ≈ 10-Year Treasury Yield + Mortgage Spread (typically 150–300 bps).

Read the release step by step

  1. Check the weekly 30-Year and 15-Year rates: open Thursday's Freddie Mac PMMS release and note the current week's rate, prior week's rate, and year-ago rate.
  2. Calculate the basis-point change alongside the 10-Year Treasury: compare the weekly mortgage rate change (in bps) with how the 10-year Treasury yield moved from the prior Thursday to Wednesday.
  3. Monitor the Mortgage Spread: subtract the 10-year Treasury yield from the 30-year mortgage rate to see whether the spread is near a normal ~170 bps or widened above 250 bps due to bond volatility.
  4. Cross-check housing demand: compare moves across key psychological levels (such as 6% or 7%) against the MBA Purchase Index (Lesson 48) and monthly home sales.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If the 30-year mortgage rate declines in an orderly fashion alongside cooling inflation and narrowing MBS spreads while jobs remain steady, improved monthly affordability can unlock housing turnover and support equities and crypto risk sentiment.

When a fall is plausible

If the 30-year mortgage rate surges to multi-month highs on sticky inflation or rising Treasury yields, monthly payment shock can freeze housing activity and pressure equity valuations; conversely, if mortgage rates plunge only because a severe recession panic drives a flight into Treasuries, recession fears can still pull stocks and crypto down.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If the weekly PMMS rate moves by only a few basis points—and bond traders already observed that move all week in live 10-year Treasury and MBS pricing—Thursday's noon release rarely causes fresh volatility in equities or crypto.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: consider a $375,000 home purchase with a 20% down payment ($75,000) and a $300,000 30-year fixed loan (360 monthly payments). At a 6.00% annual mortgage rate (0.50% monthly rate), standard amortization gives a monthly principal-and-interest payment of about $1,799. If the mortgage rate rises 1.00 percentage point (+100 bps) to 7.00%, the monthly principal-and-interest payment on the exact same $300,000 loan jumps to about $1,996 (excluding taxes and insurance). Even with zero change in the home's price, the +100 bp rate increase adds $1,996 - $1,799 = $197 per month (+10.95%, or $2,364 per year), squeezing household budgets.

History and origin

PMMS began in 1971. In 2022 it moved to application-based methodology; back-calculated data should not be confused with the original survey method.

Where and how to find the data

Freddie Mac · Weekly · Usually Thursday at noon U.S. Eastern; a Thursday holiday may move it earlier.

Freddie Mac official data

  1. Open the latest PMMS weekly release.
  2. Choose the 30-Year Fixed Rate and its historical chart.
  3. Open Methodology to check the observation week and borrower criteria; this is not an individualized quote.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Assuming a Federal Reserve rate cut automatically lowers 30-year mortgage rates by the same amount—if long-term 10-year Treasury yields rise after a Fed cut, mortgage rates can actually go up.
  2. Treating the headline PMMS average (based on 20%-down prime conforming loans) as the exact rate quoted to low-down-payment, jumbo, or lower-credit borrowers.
  3. Overlooking the November 2022 methodology change from lender surveys to automated loan-application data when comparing long-term historical charts.
