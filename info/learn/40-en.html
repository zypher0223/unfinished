Household Loan Delinquency Rates

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Household Loan Delinquency Rates, published quarterly by the Federal Reserve Board (from commercial bank regulatory filings) and supplemented by the New York Fed's Quarterly Report on Household Debt and Credit, measure the percentage of loan balances in credit cards, auto loans, consumer loans, and residential mortgages that are past due. They track the share of delinquent dollar balances within defined loan portfolios rather than the percentage of U.S. citizens filing for bankruptcy. Beginners often misread a 3% balance delinquency rate as meaning 3 out of 100 people defaulted, or mix incompatible definitions from the Board and the New York Fed.

Start with an everyday comparison

It is like checking how many dollars out of every $100 a bank lent out are currently past their due date ($3 overdue out of $100 is a 3% balance delinquency rate); however, that $3 could belong to one large borrower or ten small ones, and loans written off as uncollectible are moved into a separate charge-off bucket.

What does this number measure?

Reported as a percentage (%) equal to Delinquent Loan Balances divided by Total Loan Balances in that category. In the Federal Reserve Board's commercial bank series, 'delinquent' loans include those 30 days or more past due and still accruing interest plus loans in nonaccrual status, reported alongside a separate 'Charge-off Rate' (loans written off as losses). By contrast, the New York Fed report tracks 30+, 60+, and 90+ day delinquency stocks as well as quarterly 'Transition Rates' into delinquency; never mix these series without matching the exact definition.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect and medium-term: rising delinquency rates → signal mounting household debt servicing strain → force households to curb discretionary spending while prompting banks to raise loan-loss provisions and tighten lending standards → weigh on corporate earnings expectations and credit availability → pressure equity valuations (especially banks and consumer cyclicals) and broad crypto risk appetite. Because the data are quarterly and lagged, they serve as a credit-cycle confirmation gauge rather than a fast intraday trading trigger.

Read the release step by step

  1. Lock the source and loan category: on the Fed's Charge-Off and Delinquency page, select Seasonally Adjusted (SA) data and pick one category (Credit cards, Other consumer loans, or Residential real estate).
  2. Distinguish Delinquency from Charge-offs and Transitions: verify whether you are reading the stock Delinquency Rate, the Charge-off Rate, or the New York Fed's quarterly Transition Rate.
  3. Track multi-quarter trends and revisions: compare the trajectory across the past 4 to 8 quarters rather than reacting to a single quarter in isolation.
  4. Check numerator and denominator effects: inspect whether total loan balances (the denominator) expanded or contracted, and cross-check with unemployment and bank lending standards.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If delinquency rates across credit cards and consumer loans stabilize or edge down while employment and wage growth hold firm, credit-cycle fears can ease and support equities and crypto, provided bank lending standards are not tightening simultaneously.

When a fall is plausible

If credit-card and auto delinquencies climb for several consecutive quarters alongside rising charge-off rates and higher unemployment, fears of consumer retrenchment and tighter bank credit can weigh on risk assets, though you should also check whether prime residential mortgages remain stable.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If a small uptick is confined to one sub-category due to technical reporting shifts (such as student-loan reporting changes) while jobs and mortgage quality stay solid, markets typically show little reaction because quarterly bank earnings have already priced in the trend.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose in Q1 a bank's credit-card portfolio (denominator) is $1,000B and 30+ day overdue balances (numerator) are $30B, giving a Delinquency Rate of $30B / $1,000B = 3.0%. In Q2 total card balances grow to $1,100B (+10%) and overdue balances rise to $33B (+$3B), keeping the Delinquency Rate flat at $33B / $1,100B = 3.0%. In Q3 the bank writes off $5B of uncollectible overdue loans as Charge-offs, reducing remaining overdue balances to $33B - $5B = $28B against $1,095B total balances (~2.56% delinquency rate). The drop from 3.0% to 2.56% came from writing off $5B in bad debt, not from borrowers paying back their loans.

History and origin

These are supervisory credit-risk statistics rather than a trader invention. The Board series extends into the 1980s; the New York Fed household panel is a separate dataset.

Where and how to find the data

Federal Reserve Board; New York Fed as a separate supplementary source official data

  1. Under Delinquency rates select All → SA.
  2. Locate Credit cards, Other consumer loans or Residential real estate; keep one category for comparisons.
  3. Read About to confirm the overdue threshold; do not substitute charge-off rates or New York Fed transition rates.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Assuming a lower delinquency rate always means healthier borrowers when writing off bad loans into charge-offs (shrinking the numerator) or rapid new lending (growing the denominator) can artificially lower the ratio.
  2. Mixing the Federal Reserve Board's 30+ day bank delinquency stock rate with the New York Fed's 90+ day quarterly transition rate.
  3. Treating a lagged quarterly credit report as a sudden surprise when big banks already disclosed their card charge-offs weeks earlier in quarterly earnings.
