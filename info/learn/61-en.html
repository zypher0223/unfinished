U.S. Treasury Auction Results

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

U.S. Treasury Auction Results record the pricing and allotment details when the U.S. Department of the Treasury sells newly issued marketable debt (including Treasury Bills, Notes, Bonds, TIPS, and Floating Rate Notes) in the primary market. These releases directly reveal the yield and price global institutional and retail investors demand to absorb incoming federal debt supply.

Start with an everyday comparison

Think of a Treasury auction as a borrower inviting bids from lenders to fund a large loan: eager competition among lenders lets the borrower lock in a lower interest rate (yield), whereas lukewarm demand forces the borrower to offer a higher interest rate (pushing yields up and bond prices down) to clear the entire offering.

What does this number measure?

Key metrics include the High Yield (or High Discount Rate for bills, expressed in percent under a single-price Dutch auction format), the auction Tail or Stop-Through in basis points (the High Yield minus the contemporaneous When-Issued market yield right at the bidding deadline), the Bid-to-Cover ratio (total bids tendered divided by total bids accepted), and the percentage allotments awarded to Indirect Bidders (often including foreign central banks and global funds), Direct Bidders, and Primary Dealers (who backstop unallocated supply).

Why can markets react?

Treasury auctions test real-money appetite for risk-free duration: weak demand at a benchmark 10-year or 30-year auction (marked by a wide positive tail and heavy primary-dealer takedown) can push secondary Treasury yields higher, raising equity discount rates and tightening crypto funding conditions. Conversely, a well-absorbed auction can calm rate volatility—though analysts must check whether strong bond buying reflects healthy duration demand or a sudden flight to safety during an equity sell-off.

Read the release step by step

  1. Open the official TreasuryDirect or Treasury auction announcement and verify the security maturity (e.g., 2-year, 10-year, or 30-year), offering size, auction date, and settlement date.
  2. Read the High Yield and Bid-to-Cover ratio, then immediately compare the High Yield against the same-maturity When-Issued (WI) yield at the bidding deadline (typically 1:00 p.m. Eastern): a High Yield above the WI yield produces a positive 'tail' (weaker demand), while a High Yield below WI is a 'stop-through' (stronger demand).
  3. Examine the allotment shares taken by Indirect Bidders, Direct Bidders, and Primary Dealers, comparing them against the average of the past 6 to 10 auctions of the exact same maturity rather than comparing across different maturities.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

A stop-through (negative tail) with low primary-dealer awards and an orderly decline in secondary Treasury yields—absent a crisis-driven flight to safety—often supports equity valuations and crypto risk appetite.

When a fall is plausible

A multi-basis-point auction tail paired with a high primary-dealer takedown that triggers a sustained climb in long-term Treasury yields typically pressures growth equities and leveraged crypto positions.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

An on-the-screws auction (High Yield matching the When-Issued yield) or mixed internal metrics (e.g., a solid bid-to-cover offset by a mild tail) often leaves secondary yields and risk assets trading in place.

Work through a made-up example

Suppose the Treasury auctions $40 billion of 10-year notes and receives $100 billion in total tenders, producing a Bid-to-Cover ratio of $100B / $40B = 2.50x; if the When-Issued yield at the 1:00 p.m. deadline is 4.20% and the auction stops at a High Yield of 4.23%, the auction has a +3 basis point tail (4.23% - 4.20% = +0.03%, or 3 bps), indicating soft marginal demand. Comparing 4.23% solely against last month's 4.40% auction yield would be a mistake because the pre-auction secondary market had already shifted. Illustration only.

History and origin

Treasury auctions evolved over time; Treasury bills were introduced through auctions in 1929. Modern uniform-price rules developed later.

Where and how to find the data

U.S. Treasury / TreasuryDirect · Scheduled auctions · Timing differs for bills and coupon securities; read each auction announcement.

U.S. Treasury / TreasuryDirect official data

  1. Navigate to Auctions → Auction Results rather than Announcements alone.
  2. Filter by security type and auction date, and open the result table or PDF.
  3. Read High Yield/Rate, Bid-to-Cover and allotments; compare with the same-maturity when-issued yield immediately before the auction.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Never judge an auction by the Bid-to-Cover ratio or the raw High Yield level in isolation; always evaluate the tail/stop-through relative to the deadline When-Issued yield and check the share left with Primary Dealers.
  2. Markets trade marginal new information relative to expectations. Because auction results hit screens instantly after the bidding deadline, watch whether secondary Treasury yields, the U.S. dollar, and equity/crypto prices confirm the move before acting.
