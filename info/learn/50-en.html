NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index HMI

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index (HMI), published monthly (typically mid-month) by the National Association of Home Builders, surveys U.S. single-family homebuilders on their ratings of current new-home sales, sales expectations over the next six months, and traffic of prospective buyers. Because it releases in the middle of the reference month—weeks ahead of that month's Housing Starts (Lesson 41) and New Home Sales (Lesson 44)—it serves as the earliest monthly sentiment gauge from the homebuilding front lines. Beginners often misread a score of 55 as meaning 55% of homes sold or a 55% price gain, or forget that it surveys only single-family builders.

Start with an everyday comparison

It is like calling sales-office managers at new-home subdivisions across the country mid-month to ask three questions: 'Are sales good right now? Will they be good over the next six months? Are many shoppers walking through the model homes?'; upbeat answers give an early clue, but showroom sentiment is not a count of closed deeds.

What does this number measure?

Reported as a 0-to-100 Diffusion Index where 50 is the breakeven line between positive and negative views: a reading above 50 means more builders rate conditions as 'Good/High' than 'Poor/Low', while below 50 means negative responses outnumber positive ones. The headline HMI is a weighted average of three sub-indexes: Present Single-Family Sales (~59% weight), Single-Family Sales for the Next 6 Months (~14% weight), and Traffic of Prospective Buyers (~27% weight). The report also tracks the percentage of builders cutting prices or offering sales incentives, plus four regional scores.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect and leading: shifts in mortgage rates, construction costs, and model-home foot traffic → immediately reshape single-family builder confidence (captured in HMI) → drive builders' decisions to pull Building Permits (Lesson 42), break ground on Housing Starts (Lesson 41), and order materials/labor → alter market forecasts for residential GDP investment and cyclical momentum → influence Treasury yields, homebuilder/retail equities, and broad stock/crypto risk appetite.

Read the release step by step

  1. Verify the reference month and the 50 threshold: open the mid-month NAHB release and check whether the headline sits above or below the 50 breakeven mark.
  2. Compare actual, consensus, and prior: check how many index points HMI beat or missed forecasts and track its multi-month direction.
  3. Decompose the three weighted sub-indexes: inspect Present Sales (~59%), Expected Sales (~14%), and Buyer Traffic (~27%) to see whether actual foot traffic supports future optimism.
  4. Check price-cut and incentive shares: read the text for the share of builders cutting prices or offering mortgage rate buydowns, then cross-check against subsequent New Home Sales.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If the headline HMI and all three sub-indexes—including Buyer Traffic—climb above expectations and hold above 50 while fewer builders need to cut prices, it signals genuine front-line buyer demand and can support cyclical equities and risk assets if bond yields remain stable.

When a fall is plausible

If HMI drops deeper below 50 alongside weak Buyer Traffic and widespread builder price cuts, it warns that high mortgage rates are freezing showroom demand and foreshadows weaker Housing Starts and New Home Sales ahead, weighing on housing and cyclical risk sentiment.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If the headline beats consensus by a couple of points while remaining below the 50 breakeven line—or if builders express hope for the next six months while current Buyer Traffic stays depressed—markets typically wait for hard sales data to confirm.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose consensus expects HMI at 47 (prior 45) and the actual prints at 49 (+2 points above consensus, +4 points m/m). Breaking down the three sub-indexes: suppose Present Sales is 54 (59% weight), Expected Next-6-Month Sales is 60 (14% weight), and Buyer Traffic is 34 (27% weight). Weighted check: (54 × 0.59) + (60 × 0.14) + (34 × 0.27) = 31.86 + 8.40 + 9.18 = 49.44 ≈ 49. Meanwhile, 30% of builders report cutting prices by an average of 5%. Even though 49 beat expectations, it remains below 50 and foot traffic (34) is still weak.

History and origin

The inaugural HMI survey was January 1985, with a reading of 50; it surveys single-family builders.

Where and how to find the data

National Association of Home Builders NAHB · Monthly · Usually 10:00 a.m. U.S. Eastern; confirm the NAHB calendar.

National Association of Home Builders NAHB official data

  1. Open the latest HMI report.
  2. Read Present Sales, Expected Sales and Buyer Traffic.
  3. Check the 50 threshold, regional trends and actual new-home sales.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Interpreting a rise from 45 to 49 as 'widespread builder optimism' or a '4% increase in home sales' when any reading below 50 still means negative responses outnumber positive ones.
  2. Looking only at the headline near 50 while ignoring a deeply depressed Buyer Traffic sub-index (e.g., in the 30s) propped up only by costly builder mortgage subsidies.
  3. Assuming the NAHB HMI measures commercial real estate or high-rise apartment construction when it surveys exclusively single-family homebuilders.
