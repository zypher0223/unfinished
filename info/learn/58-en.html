Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey SLOOS

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

The Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey on Bank Lending Practices (SLOOS) is a quarterly qualitative survey conducted by the Federal Reserve that asks senior loan officers at domestic and U.S. branches of foreign commercial banks two distinct sets of questions over the past three months: did your bank tighten or ease credit approval standards, and did borrower demand for loans strengthen or weaken? Readers must strictly separate bank-side credit supply standards from customer-side borrowing demand.

Start with an everyday comparison

Think of SLOOS as a quarterly check-in with shopkeepers across the country that asks two separate questions: first, whether the shop raised the bar and collateral rules for extending store credit (supply standards), and second, whether more or fewer customers walked in asking to borrow on credit (borrower demand).

What does this number measure?

SLOOS headlines are reported as net percentages in percentage points. For lending standards, the net tightening percentage equals the share of banks reporting they tightened standards minus the share reporting they eased standards (banks reporting unchanged standards are excluded from the net difference). A net tightening reading of +30.0 percentage points does not mean loan interest rates are 30% or that total loan volume fell by 30%; it simply means the share of banks tightening exceeded the share easing by 30 percentage points.

Why can markets react?

SLOOS often leads actual loan growth and default cycles: when a broad majority of banks tighten standards and widen loan spreads, marginal businesses and households face harder refinancing hurdles, curbing future capital spending, hiring, and consumption; meanwhile, plunging loan demand reflects the drag of high interest rates or economic uncertainty. The survey is closely watched for gauging financial conditions and recession risk, transmitting to equities and crypto through credit spreads and risk appetite.

Read the release step by step

  1. Open the quarterly Federal Reserve SLOOS report and verify whether each specific question measures tightening standards, wider loan rate spreads, or stronger borrower demand before interpreting the positive or negative sign.
  2. Review breakdowns across commercial and industrial (C&I) loans to large/middle-market and small firms, commercial real estate (CRE) loans, residential mortgages, and consumer credit cards and auto loans to see if supply tightening and weak demand align.
  3. Because SLOOS rarely has a standardized consensus number, compare the net percentage against the prior quarter and multi-quarter history; remember that a lower but still positive net tightening reading means banks are still tightening further, not easing.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

A notable decline in net tightening (or a shift toward net easing) accompanied by stabilizing borrower demand and contained inflation can bolster soft-landing confidence and support equities and crypto.

When a fall is plausible

A sharp surge in net tightening across C&I and CRE loans paired with deeply negative borrower demand often fuels credit-crunch and recession fears, pressuring equity multiples and high-beta crypto assets.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When net tightening moderates from the prior quarter yet remains positive (meaning standards are still getting tighter at a slower pace), or when corporate and consumer categories diverge, markets may trade sideways.

Work through a made-up example

Suppose in Quarter 1, 40% of surveyed banks report tightening C&I loan standards, 10% report easing, and 50% report no change, producing a net tightening reading of 40% - 10% = +30 percentage points. If in Quarter 2, 28% report tightening and 8% report easing, net tightening falls to 28% - 8% = +20 percentage points; this shows less widespread tightening (+20 vs. +30), but because +20 is still positive, overall credit standards tightened further on top of Quarter 1 rather than easing. Illustration only.

History and origin

The survey began in 1964 with 120 banks and quarterly reporting; panels and questions evolved, with four surveys a year typical since 1992.

Where and how to find the data

Federal Reserve Board · Usually quarterly · Normally around the January/February, April/May, July/August and October/November policy cycles.

Federal Reserve Board official data

  1. Choose the latest dated SLOOS release.
  2. Read Standards and Demand separately for business, property and household lending.
  3. Check the definition and sign of net percentage; net tightening is not an interest rate.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Never misinterpret a drop in positive net tightening (e.g., from +30 to +20) as net easing—as long as net tightening is above zero, banks are tightening standards further—and do not confuse qualitative survey percentages with actual loan interest rates or dollar volumes.
  2. Markets react to marginal new information relative to expectations. Because SLOOS is released quarterly, cross-check high-yield credit spreads, Treasury yields, the dollar, and broad index reactions rather than relying on the survey alone for short-term timing.
