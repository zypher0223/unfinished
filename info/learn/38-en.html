University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Index

The University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Index, published twice a month (Preliminary mid-month and Final at month-end) by the University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers, gauges U.S. households' views on their personal finances, general business conditions, and big-ticket buying conditions, alongside closely watched 1-year and 5–10-year inflation expectations. It measures subjective household perceptions rather than actual spending totals. Beginners often treat the Preliminary and Final releases as two separate months or overlook the inflation-expectations lines in the same report.

Start with an everyday comparison

It is like calling households twice a month to ask both 'How comfortable is your family budget?' and 'How much faster do you think grocery and gas prices will rise next year?'; however, respondents reacting to last week's gas-pump prices do not automatically predict actual future inflation.

What does this number measure?

The Headline Sentiment, Current Economic Conditions, and Consumer Expectations series are reported in index points benchmarked to 1966 Q1 = 100, whereas the accompanying 1-year and 5–10-year inflation expectations are reported as annual percentages (%). The mid-month Preliminary print covers partial monthly interviews, and the month-end Final print incorporates the full sample. Unlike the job-heavy Conference Board index, Michigan weighs personal finances and price sensitivity more heavily.

Why can markets react?

Transmission runs through two distinct channels: first, household financial confidence shapes big-ticket spending intentions and corporate earnings expectations; second—and often more important for markets—household inflation expectations are monitored by the Federal Reserve to judge whether inflation psychology remains anchored. Even if sentiment improves, an unexpected jump in 5–10-year inflation expectations can push Treasury yields and the dollar higher, indirectly pressuring equity valuations and crypto risk appetite.

Read the release step by step

  1. Check Preliminary versus Final: confirm whether the release is the mid-month preliminary estimate or the month-end final revision for the same month.
  2. Read both sentiment and inflation expectations: check the three sentiment index-point lines first, then read the 1-year and 5–10-year inflation expectation percentages.
  3. Compare against consensus and prior values: evaluate both the sentiment point surprise and any basis-point shift in inflation expectations.
  4. Cross-check gasoline prices and hard data: compare survey shifts against retail gasoline prices and actual retail sales to judge durability.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If consumer sentiment beats expectations while 1-year and 5–10-year inflation expectations remain anchored or edge lower, it points to healthier household confidence without added inflation pressure, which can support equities and crypto if bond yields ease.

When a fall is plausible

If sentiment slumps while inflation expectations jump higher (a stagflationary mix of weaker confidence and higher price fears), rate-cut hopes can recede and higher Treasury yields can weigh on stocks and crypto, though you should check whether a brief gas-price spike skewed responses.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If the month-end Final print barely revises the mid-month Preliminary reading, or a sentiment beat is offset by a slight uptick in inflation expectations, markets often trade sideways while awaiting hard spending or CPI releases.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose consensus expects Preliminary Sentiment at 70.0 (prior 69.0) and the actual prints at 74.0 (+4.0 index points above consensus); however, in the same release 5–10-year inflation expectations rise from 3.0% to 3.4% (+0.4 percentage points, or +40 bps). Note that +4.0 index points and +0.4 percentage points are different units; bond yields may climb on the 3.4% inflation expectation, offsetting the 74.0 sentiment beat.

History and origin

The Surveys of Consumers began in 1946 under George Katona and evolved into the present measures. Individual subseries need not date to the original survey.

Where and how to find the data

University of Michigan · Monthly preliminary and final · Preliminary and final releases follow the official calendar, usually at 10:00 a.m. U.S. Eastern.

University of Michigan official data

  1. Open Reports and select the latest monthly report.
  2. Find Index of Consumer Sentiment, Current Economic Conditions and Consumer Expectations.
  3. Use Data / Survey Information to check preliminary versus final, fieldwork dates and publication dates.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Confusing index points with percentage points, or focusing solely on the sentiment headline while ignoring the market-moving 1-year and 5–10-year inflation expectations.
  2. Treating the mid-month Preliminary release and month-end Final release as two separate months rather than an initial and revised read of the same month.
  3. Assuming household inflation expectations are a precise econometric forecast when they are heavily influenced by recent gasoline prices and partisan views.
