Building Permits

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Building Permits, published monthly by the U.S. Census Bureau and HUD alongside Housing Starts in the New Residential Construction report, count the number of privately owned new housing units authorized by building permits in permit-issuing jurisdictions during the reference month. They measure pre-construction regulatory approvals and builder intentions—one step ahead of Housing Starts (Lesson 41)—rather than active excavations or finished homes. Beginners often assume a permit means construction has already begun or overlook that non-permit rural areas are excluded from permit counts.

Start with an everyday comparison

If building a house is like opening a restaurant, a Building Permit is getting the city blueprint approval at the counter before hiring the crew: rising approvals show builders plan to build, but if financing costs jump or sales stall, builders can leave those approved blueprints sitting in a drawer.

What does this number measure?

Reported in thousands of units at a Seasonally Adjusted Annual Rate (SAAR) alongside monthly percentage changes (%), split into Single-Family (1-unit) and Multifamily (5 units or more, plus 2–4 units). Two key distinctions separate Permits from Housing Starts: first, Permits lead Starts in the construction timeline; second, Permits cover only permit-issuing jurisdictions (~20,000 places), whereas Housing Starts also estimate construction in areas that do not require permits.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect and forward-looking: changes in building permits → foreshadow construction employment, materials orders, and housing investment 1 to 3 months ahead (making Building Permits one of the 10 components of the Conference Board's Leading Economic Index) → shift expectations for GDP growth and cyclical turning points → influence Treasury yields and Federal Reserve rate expectations → filter into equity valuations and crypto risk appetite. Because clerks can issue permits indoors even during winter blizzards that halt outdoor digging, economists often view Permits as a cleaner signal than weather-sensitive Starts.

Read the release step by step

  1. Check the reference month and SAAR units: locate Building Permits in Table 1 of the Census New Residential Construction release and note the annualized thousands unit.
  2. Compare actual, consensus, and revisions: compare the headline SAAR figure against consensus forecasts and prior revisions, noting that permit data have much smaller sampling error than starts.
  3. Separate Single-Family from Multifamily: compare 1-unit permits with 5-or-more-unit permits to see whether growth comes from single-family homes or lumpy apartment filings.
  4. Check 'Authorized, Not Started' backlogs: compare Permits against Housing Starts and the backlog of units authorized but not yet started to see if projects are stalling on paper.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If Building Permits—particularly Single-Family permits—climb above consensus and translate smoothly into actual starts without sparking bond-yield spikes, it points to a healthy forward construction pipeline and can support soft-landing sentiment across equities and crypto.

When a fall is plausible

If Building Permits trend lower for several months, it signals that builders are pulling back future supply plans in response to weak demand or high financing costs; conversely, an overheating permit surge during high inflation can push Treasury yields up and pressure risk valuations.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If Permits rise while actual Housing Starts stall and the 'Authorized, Not Started' backlog swells—or if a jump is driven by a one-off rush to file apartment permits ahead of a local zoning rule change—markets often stay range-bound awaiting real groundbreaking.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose consensus expects Building Permits at 1,420k SAAR (revised prior 1,400k) and the actual prints at 1,460k (+40k above consensus, and [1,460 - 1,400] / 1,400 ≈ +4.29% m/m), with Single-Family permits rising from 980k to 1,010k (+30k) and Multifamily rising from 420k to 450k (+30k). However, in the same report Housing Starts remain at 1,250k while 'Authorized, Not Started' units climb from 260k to 280k (+20k). Builders secured more approvals on paper, but actual groundbreaking lagged.

History and origin

Census documents monthly and annual permit data since 1959; this is the series history, not the origin of building regulation.

Where and how to find the data

U.S. Census Bureau / HUD · Monthly · Published with New Residential Construction, usually at 8:30 a.m. U.S. Eastern.

U.S. Census Bureau / HUD official data

  1. Open Latest New Residential Construction Report.
  2. Find Building Permits and single-family / five-or-more-unit rows.
  3. Compare authorized, not-yet-started units with starts; a permit is not completed construction.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating an approved Building Permit as a completed or actively started home when projects can sit unstarted for months or be canceled.
  2. Wondering why Housing Starts occasionally exceed Building Permits in certain regions, forgetting that non-permit rural jurisdictions count in Starts but not in Permits.
  3. Over-interpreting a single-month multifamily permit spike caused by developers rushing applications before a local building-code or tax deadline.
