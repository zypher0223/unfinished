Housing Starts

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Housing Starts, published monthly by the U.S. Census Bureau and the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) in the New Residential Construction report, count the number of privately owned new housing units where excavation (groundbreaking) actually began during the reference month. It measures physical construction starts rather than pre-construction permits or completed home sales. Beginners often misread a headline '1.40 million Seasonally Adjusted Annual Rate' as meaning 1.40 million homes broke ground in that single month.

Start with an everyday comparison

Think of building a home like opening a restaurant: a Building Permit is getting city approval on paper, a Housing Start is the excavator actually digging the foundation, and a Completion is opening the doors; breaking ground shows real money is being spent on construction today, even though the finished home is months away.

What does this number measure?

Reported in thousands of units at a Seasonally Adjusted Annual Rate (SAAR)—meaning the single month's seasonally adjusted pace is multiplied by 12 to show what a full year would look like at that pace (unadjusted monthly starts are roughly one-twelfth of the headline). The report splits total starts into Single-Family (1-unit) structures and Multifamily (5 units or more, plus 2–4 units). Because breaking ground on one apartment building registers hundreds of units at once, the multifamily line is far more volatile than the single-family line.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect: changes in housing starts → drive construction employment, lumber/copper demand, and future appliance purchases → feed directly into the Residential Fixed Investment component of GDP → influence how the Federal Reserve judges the bite of interest rates on the real economy → move Treasury yields and broad risk sentiment across equities and crypto. Because housing is highly rate-sensitive, starts often act as an early cyclical barometer.

Read the release step by step

  1. Verify the reference month and SAAR units: open the Census New Residential Construction release and note that figures are in thousands at an annual rate.
  2. Compare actual, consensus, and revisions: check the headline SAAR level and monthly percentage change against forecasts and prior-month revisions, noting wide confidence intervals.
  3. Separate Single-Family from Multifamily: compare 1-unit starts against 5-or-more-unit starts to see whether the move reflects core single-family demand or lumpy apartment buildings.
  4. Cross-check Permits and Under Construction: read Building Permits (Lesson 42) and units Under Construction in the same release to gauge the pipeline ahead.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If Housing Starts—especially Single-Family starts—recover steadily above consensus without reigniting broad inflation pressure, it signals resilient real-economy investment and supports soft-landing sentiment for equities and crypto, provided Treasury yields stay contained.

When a fall is plausible

If both single-family and multifamily starts drop sharply over several months alongside high mortgage rates, it warns that tight financing is freezing construction activity and jobs; conversely, an overheating print during high inflation can push bond yields up and pressure asset valuations.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If a headline beat is driven entirely by a volatile multifamily spike while single-family starts fall—or merely reflects a weather rebound after winter storms within the wide sampling error margin—markets typically stay range-bound.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose consensus expects total Housing Starts at 1,350k SAAR (1.35M), the prior month is revised from 1,320k to 1,300k, and the actual prints at 1,400k (+50k above consensus, and [1,400 - 1,300] / 1,300 ≈ +7.69% m/m). However, Single-Family (1-unit) starts drop from 1,000k to 950k (-50k, or -5.0%), while Multifamily starts jump from 300k to 450k (+150k, or +50.0%), summing to 950k + 450k = 1,400k. The headline beat came entirely from lumpy apartment starts while single-family activity cooled.

History and origin

Census took responsibility for construction statistics in 1959; the modern comparable series starts then, with predecessor estimates earlier.

Where and how to find the data

U.S. Census Bureau / HUD · Monthly · Usually 8:30 a.m. U.S. Eastern on the Census calendar; delays and holidays can change dates.

U.S. Census Bureau / HUD official data

  1. Open Latest New Residential Construction Report.
  2. Find Housing Starts, then single-family and multifamily rows.
  3. Compare starts with permits and completions, checking annualized units and revised months.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Mistaking a 1.40 million annualized rate (SAAR) for 1.40 million homes started in a single month (actual unadjusted monthly starts are roughly 100k–130k).
  2. Reacting to a headline jump without checking the Single-Family vs. Multifamily split, when a few large apartment complexes can swing the total.
  3. Over-interpreting small 1%–2% monthly moves when the Census Bureau's 90% confidence interval for monthly starts changes is often ±10% or wider.
