Loan Prime Rate (LPR)

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

When headlines report an 'interest rate cut' in China, they may refer to PBOC wholesale policy rates (like the 7-Day Reverse Repo) or the retail lending benchmark—the Loan Prime Rate (LPR). Understanding what the 1-Year and Over-5-Year LPR tenors control reveals who actually benefits from each cut.

Start with an everyday comparison

The PBOC's 7-Day Reverse Repo rate is like the wholesale flour price, while the LPR is the retail bread benchmark price quoted by 20 major banks after adding their baking and funding costs.

What does this number measure?

Authorized by the PBOC and published by CFETS on the 20th of each month, the LPR is calculated from quotes submitted by 20 designated commercial banks based on the rates they charge their most creditworthy clients, formed by adding a spread over open market operation rates (anchored primarily to the PBOC 7-Day Reverse Repo rate since mid-2024, replacing the older 1-Year MLF anchor), after trimming the highest and lowest quotes. It features two tenors: 1. 1-Year LPR, pricing corporate working-capital loans and consumer credit; and 2. Over-5-Year LPR, serving as the pricing anchor for residential mortgages and long-term capital expenditure loans (Mortgage Rate = Over-5-Year LPR +/- local bank spread).

Why can markets react?

LPR directly dictates debt-servicing costs for businesses and homeowners. Cutting the 1-Year LPR lowers corporate operating interest expenses; cutting the Over-5-Year LPR sends a targeted signal to support the property market and reduce household mortgage burdens (freeing up disposable income for consumption), though it simultaneously compresses commercial bank Net Interest Margins (NIM).

Read the release step by step

  1. Watch leading indicators before the 20th: check whether the PBOC has already cut the 7-Day Reverse Repo rate or whether major state banks have collectively lowered deposit rates (which lowers bank funding costs and creates room for LPR cuts).
  2. Analyze asymmetric cuts (1Y vs. 5Y+): a larger cut in the Over-5-Year LPR relative to the 1-Year LPR signals targeted support for housing mortgages and long-term infrastructure.
  3. Consider bank NIM constraints: when commercial bank net interest margins are near historical lows and deposit rates remain sticky, quoting banks have limited room to compress LPR spreads.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

When LPR is raised: occurs only during overheating or high-inflation regimes, directly increasing corporate and mortgage borrowing costs and tightening financial conditions.

When a fall is plausible

When LPR is cut (especially a larger-than-expected cut in the 5Y+ tenor): lowers real-economy financing and mortgage costs, often supporting property, consumer, and cyclical equities (though government bonds may see 'sell-the-news' profit-taking if the cut was already priced in).

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When LPR is held unchanged on the 20th: reflects policy patience, bank margin protection, or FX stability considerations against the U.S. dollar.

Work through a made-up example

Illustration only: Suppose on the 20th of the month, the Over-5-Year LPR is cut by 25 bps (0.25%) from 3.85% to 3.60%. A household holds a 1,000,000 RMB mortgage priced at 'Over-5-Year LPR minus 30 bps'. Once their loan reaches its contractual repricing date, their effective mortgage rate resets downward from 3.55% (3.85% - 0.30%) to 3.30% (3.60% - 0.30%), directly reducing annual interest payments. These figures are hypothetical.

History and origin

The PBOC introduced the centralized LPR mechanism in October 2013 and overhauled it on August 17, 2019, adding the 5-Year-Plus tenor as the anchor for mortgages; in mid-2024, the PBOC transitioned its primary policy rate anchor from the 1-Year MLF to the 7-Day Reverse Repo rate; from July 22, 2024 the release moved from 9:15 to 9:00, and the panel grew from 18 to 20 banks in January 2024.

Where and how to find the data

National Interbank Funding Center (CFETS), authorized by the People's Bank of China (PBOC) · Monthly (on the 20th of each month, or next business day if a holiday) · Published on the 20th of each month (postponed to the next business day if the 20th falls on a weekend or holiday) at 9:00 AM Beijing Time (UTC+8), subject to official CFETS announcements.

China Foreign Exchange Trade System (CFETS / Chinamoney): Official LPR Portal

  1. Open the official Chinamoney LPR portal at 9:00 AM Beijing Time on the 20th of the month.
  2. Check the two official tenors: 1-Year LPR and Over-5-Year LPR, noting any basis-point changes from the prior month.
  3. Review the historical LPR table and the list of 20 designated quoting panel banks.

People's Bank of China: Monetary Policy Department Portal

  1. Visit the PBOC Monetary Policy Department page to check the 7-Day Reverse Repo rate in daily open market operation notices.
  2. Consult the quarterly Monetary Policy Execution Report to track weighted average corporate and mortgage lending rates.
  3. Check whether commercial banks have lowered deposit rates ahead of LPR cuts to preserve net interest margins.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Expecting existing monthly mortgage payments to drop the very next day after a 5Y+ LPR cut: existing floating-rate mortgages only update to the latest LPR on their contractual 'repricing date' (typically every 3, 6, or 12 months).
  2. Relying on outdated pre-2024 rules that treated the 15th-of-the-month MLF rate as the sole LPR predictor: following the PBOC's mid-2024 reform, the 7-Day Reverse Repo is the primary policy rate anchor, and MLF operations were moved to the 25th (after the LPR release).
  3. Assuming every loan is priced off LPR: since 2026 some new loans use DR (the depository-institution repo rate) as their benchmark, creating an 'LPR + DR' dual-anchor setup, so check which benchmark your contract references.
