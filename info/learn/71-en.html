EIA Weekly Natural Gas Storage

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The report covers working gas in underground storage in the Lower 48 states; seasonal injections and withdrawals require seasonal comparisons.

Start with an everyday comparison

A winter pantry is expected to empty; the surprise is how fast relative to seasonal needs.

What does this number measure?

Bcf means billion cubic feet; +80 is an injection and −80 a withdrawal, not dollars or oil barrels.

Why can markets react?

Storage surprises can change gas prices, utilities and industrial costs and inflation, with indirect broad-equity and crypto effects.

Read the release step by step

  1. Check the official reference period, publication date, unit and seasonal adjustment.
  2. Read the named headline and its components; distinguish levels, changes and revised observations.
  3. Compare actual, independently sourced consensus and revised prior values; do not manufacture a consensus if none is available.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

Ample supply with healthy demand can reduce inflation pressure even if gas producers weaken.

When a fall is plausible

Shortages raising costs or large builds signaling a demand collapse can hurt risk assets.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

An expected seasonal result or offsetting regional effects may barely move broad risk assets.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical: expected injection 90 Bcf, actual 70, a 20 Bcf shortfall. Gas can react even with stocks above last year, but broad equity or crypto direction does not follow automatically.

History and origin

EIA began weekly underground storage estimates on May 9, 2002, following an industry reporting predecessor; storage itself is older.

Where and how to find the data

U.S. Energy Information Administration EIA · Weekly · Usually Thursday at 10:30 a.m. U.S. Eastern; verify holiday changes.

U.S. Energy Information Administration EIA official data

  1. Open Weekly Natural Gas Storage Report.
  2. Read Lower 48 working gas and Net change.
  3. Compare the five-year range, the same week last year and regional detail, then check weather and LNG demand.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Working gas differs from base gas; compare the same seasonal week and consider forward weather.
  2. Markets react to new information relative to expectations. Check yields, the dollar and price confirmation; the release cannot guarantee a trade.
