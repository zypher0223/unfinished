EIA Weekly Natural Gas Storage

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The EIA Weekly Natural Gas Storage Report, published every Thursday at 10:30 a.m. Eastern by the U.S. Energy Information Administration, measures the total volume and weekly net change of 'working natural gas' held in underground storage facilities across the Lower 48 U.S. states as of the prior Friday. It is the primary weekly supply-and-demand benchmark for North American natural gas prices (Henry Hub). Beginners often ignore its strong seasonal cycle—panicking over routine winter inventory withdrawals—or confuse the volume unit Bcf (billion cubic feet) with dollars or oil barrels.

Start with an everyday comparison

Think of it like a winter pantry stocked during harvest and drawn down during the cold months: from April through October (injection season) the pantry is supposed to fill up every week, and from November through March (withdrawal season) it is supposed to empty every week; thus a winter drop is completely normal—what matters is whether the family ate faster or slower than expected for that exact week of the year.

What does this number measure?

Reported in Billion Cubic Feet (Bcf). Two headline figures matter: (1) total Working Gas in Underground Storage across the Lower 48 states (e.g., 2,800 Bcf); and (2) the Weekly Net Change in Bcf. A positive Net Change (e.g., +80 Bcf) indicates a net Injection (build, typical from April to October), while a negative Net Change (e.g., -120 Bcf) indicates a net Withdrawal (draw, typical from November to March). The report compares current total storage against both the exact same week last year and the 5-Year Average for that week. Note that 'Working Gas' excludes permanent 'Base Gas' needed to maintain underground reservoir pressure.

Why can markets react?

Transmission to broad equity and crypto markets is indirect and localized: weather swings (summer air-conditioning power burn and winter heating), LNG export flows, and dry-gas production → drive weekly storage injections or withdrawals → move Henry Hub natural gas futures and input costs for utilities, power generators, and chemical manufacturers → filter into household utility bills and the energy services component of CPI → marginally influence inflation expectations. Because U.S. natural gas is more regionally landlocked than global crude oil, its direct impact on the S&P 500 and crypto is usually modest unless an extreme energy crunch hits.

Read the release step by step

  1. Confirm the reference week and seasonal regime: open Thursday's 10:30 a.m. EIA report and note whether the market is in the April–October injection season (+) or November–March withdrawal season (-).
  2. Compare the Net Change (Bcf) against consensus: check how many Bcf the weekly injection or withdrawal beat or missed analyst forecasts.
  3. Compare total storage with the 5-Year Average: check the Bcf surplus or deficit relative to the 5-year historical average for that exact calendar week.
  4. Cross-check regional breakdowns, weather models, and LNG feedgas: inspect the five regional storage tables (especially South Central salt caverns) alongside 14-day temperature forecasts.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

For broad equities and crypto: if comfortable storage surpluses relative to the 5-year average keep natural gas and electricity costs stable while industrial demand holds steady, contained utility bills help keep headline inflation in check and support general risk sentiment (even if natural-gas producer stocks lag).

When a fall is plausible

If extreme weather or supply disruptions create a deep storage deficit below the 5-year average and spike gas and electricity prices, higher utility and industrial costs can add to inflation worries; conversely, unseasonably large builds driven by slumping industrial power demand can hint at manufacturing softness.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If the weekly Bcf change matches consensus—or if a tight backward-looking storage print is immediately offset by a warm 14-day winter weather forecast—natural gas futures may whip around while U.S. equities and crypto barely move.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose during June (injection season), consensus expects a net injection of +90 Bcf (prior +95 Bcf), and Thursday's EIA report prints +70 Bcf (+20 Bcf smaller build than expected, perhaps due to hot weather boosting power-plant gas burn). Meanwhile, total Lower 48 working gas stands at 2,900 Bcf compared with the 5-year average for that week of 2,600 Bcf—leaving a storage surplus of 2,900 - 2,600 = +300 Bcf (+11.5% above the 5-year norm). The +70 Bcf weekly build was tighter than the +90 Bcf forecast, yet overall storage remains 11.5% above normal, and the print has no direct link to Bitcoin or broad tech stocks.

History and origin

EIA began weekly underground storage estimates on May 9, 2002, following an industry reporting predecessor; storage itself is older.

Where and how to find the data

U.S. Energy Information Administration EIA · Weekly · Usually Thursday at 10:30 a.m. U.S. Eastern; verify holiday changes.

U.S. Energy Information Administration EIA official data

  1. Open Weekly Natural Gas Storage Report.
  2. Read Lower 48 working gas and Net change.
  3. Compare the five-year range, the same week last year and regional detail, then check weather and LNG demand.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Seeing a negative number like '-150 Bcf' in January and thinking inventories crashed unexpectedly—withdrawals are normal all winter, and if consensus expected a -180 Bcf draw, a -150 Bcf draw actually means 30 Bcf LESS gas was used than expected (bearish for gas prices).
  2. Trading last week's storage number in isolation while ignoring 8-to-14-day weather forecasts (Heating Degree Days and Cooling Degree Days) and LNG export terminal outages.
  3. Confusing usable Working Gas with permanent Base Gas, or assuming domestic U.S. natural gas inventory swings drive crypto prices.
