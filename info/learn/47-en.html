FHFA House Price Index

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The FHFA House Price Index (FHFA HPI), published monthly and quarterly by the Federal Housing Finance Agency, uses a repeat-sales methodology to track single-family home price changes across mortgages acquired or assured by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. It measures constant-quality price changes specifically for homes financed with conforming enterprise mortgages, giving it a very different sample coverage from Case-Shiller (Lesson 46). Beginners often overlook that it excludes all-cash purchases and jumbo mortgages above conforming loan limits, or mix the monthly purchase-only series with quarterly expanded series.

Start with an everyday comparison

Whereas Case-Shiller pulls county deed records that include billionaires buying mansions with cash, the FHFA index pulls repeat mortgage files from the desks of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac; thus it acts as a thermometer specifically for middle-class, mortgage-financed single-family homes across all 50 states.

What does this number measure?

Reported in index points (the monthly Purchase-Only index is benchmarked to January 1991 = 100), alongside Seasonally Adjusted (SA) monthly (m/m %) and annual (y/y %) percentage changes. Distinguish its three main flavors: the market-moving Monthly Purchase-Only Index (which excludes refinancings), the quarterly All-Transactions Index (which includes refinance appraisals), and the quarterly Expanded-Data Index (which adds FHA and county recorder data). Compared with Case-Shiller, the monthly FHFA HPI differs in three ways: (1) it covers only conforming mortgages (no cash sales or jumbo loans); (2) it weights every home equally rather than weighting expensive homes more heavily by dollar value; and (3) it covers rural and small-town America alongside major cities.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect: price trends in conforming mortgage-backed homes → determine Loan-to-Value (LTV) cushions across Fannie/Freddie MBS pools and commercial banks while shaping middle-class household equity → influence consumer credit capacity and long-run shelter inflation expectations → feed into Federal Reserve policy assessments, MBS spreads, Treasury yields, and broad equity/crypto risk appetite. Because FHFA and Case-Shiller normally release at the exact same minute (9:00 a.m. Eastern), comparing them reveals whether price strength sits in middle-tier financed homes or high-end/cash metro markets.

Read the release step by step

  1. Confirm the monthly Purchase-Only SA series: open the FHFA HPI release, note the two-month reporting lag, and select the seasonally adjusted monthly purchase-only table.
  2. Compare actual, consensus, and prior revisions: evaluate the monthly m/m % change alongside any revision to the prior month's growth rate.
  3. Check the nine Census divisions: inspect regional performance across all nine U.S. Census divisions to see where prices are firming or cooling.
  4. Compare side by side with Case-Shiller: since both release at 9:00 a.m. Eastern on the same Tuesday, compare FHFA (equal-weighted, conforming mortgages, nationwide) with 20-City Case-Shiller (value-weighted, includes cash/jumbo, major metros).

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If the FHFA index rises steadily in line with household income growth, it signals healthy collateral values for middle-class mortgages and low credit stress in the MBS market, supporting equities and crypto as long as inflation and bond yields remain well-behaved.

When a fall is plausible

If the FHFA index posts consecutive monthly declines, it warns that even mainstream conforming-mortgage housing is losing value, which can dampen consumer confidence; conversely, a hot upside surprise alongside hot Case-Shiller numbers can revive sticky-inflation fears and push Treasury yields up, pressuring risk assets.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If a slight beat on the current month is offset by a downward revision to the prior month—or if FHFA and Case-Shiller point in opposite directions due to metro-versus-non-metro or cash-versus-mortgage divergences—markets typically show muted reaction.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose for the March report consensus expects FHFA m/m growth at +0.2%, the prior month (February) is revised down from +0.4% to +0.1% (-0.3 percentage points), and the actual March figure prints at +0.3% (+0.1 percentage point above consensus). Combining the two months: before the release, expected cumulative two-month growth was 0.4% + 0.2% = +0.6%; after the release, actual two-month growth is 0.1% + 0.3% = +0.4% (0.2 percentage points lower net). If 20-City Case-Shiller prints +0.6% m/m on the same morning, higher-priced or cash-heavy big-city homes outperformed middle-tier conforming homes.

History and origin

OFHEO published official HPI reports in 1996 with estimates back to 1975; publication history and back-series dates differ.

Where and how to find the data

Federal Housing Finance Agency FHFA · Monthly and quarterly · Follow the FHFA calendar, usually 9:00 a.m. U.S. Eastern.

Federal Housing Finance Agency FHFA official data

  1. Open the latest HPI Report.
  2. Select monthly purchase-only or quarterly expanded-data; do not join them without checking definitions.
  3. Read adjusted monthly and annual changes, geographic detail and mortgage coverage.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Forgetting that the monthly FHFA Purchase-Only index excludes all-cash buyers and jumbo mortgages above conforming limits.
  2. Overlooking the weighting difference: FHFA weights a $200k house and a $1M house 1-to-1 (equal weight), whereas Case-Shiller gives the $1M house 5 times the weight (value weight).
  3. Celebrating or fearing a +0.1% headline beat on the current month while ignoring a -0.3% downward revision to the previous month.
