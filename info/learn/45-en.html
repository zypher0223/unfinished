Pending Home Sales Index

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The Pending Home Sales Index (PHS), published monthly by the National Association of Realtors (NAR), tracks existing-home transactions where a purchase contract has been signed during the reference month but the deal has not yet closed. Because closing a U.S. existing-home purchase typically takes 1 to 2 months after signing the contract, PHS serves as a direct leading indicator for Existing-Home Sales (Lesson 43). Beginners often misread an index level of 84 as meaning 840,000 homes were sold or that home prices are 16% below 2001 levels.

Start with an everyday comparison

It is like counting dinner reservations booked on a restaurant's phone today: rising reservations give a 1-to-2-hour heads-up on how busy the dining room will be (leading completed checkouts), though some reservations will cancel before ever paying a bill.

What does this number measure?

Reported in Index Points alongside Seasonally Adjusted monthly (m/m %) and annual (y/y %) percentage changes, NOT unit counts. NAR benchmarks the average contract-signing volume in the year 2001 as 100 (2001 = 100): a reading of 80 means current contract-signing volume is running at 80% of its 2001 pace (it says nothing about home prices). The release reports the national composite alongside four regions: Northeast, Midwest, South, and West. Roughly 80%+ of pending contracts typically close within 1 to 2 months.

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect and leading: current mortgage rates and listing availability → immediately impact buyers' willingness to sign contracts (captured in Pending Home Sales) → foreshadow completed Existing-Home Sales, broker fees, mortgage originations, and home-related retail spending 1 to 2 months ahead → shape market views on consumer and housing resilience → influence Treasury yields and broad equity/crypto risk sentiment. Because closed Existing-Home Sales lag by up to two months, traders watch PHS to see how buyers reacted to the latest month's mortgage rates.

Read the release step by step

  1. Verify the reference month and 2001=100 base: open the NAR Pending Home Sales release and note that it measures contract volume activity, not home prices.
  2. Focus on the m/m % change versus consensus: compare the seasonally adjusted monthly percentage change against economic calendar forecasts and prior revisions.
  3. Check the four regional breakdowns: inspect the Northeast, Midwest, South, and West indexes to see whether contract momentum is broad-based or regional.
  4. Cross-check mortgage rates and subsequent closings: compare the month's PHS move against that month's 30-year mortgage rate (Lesson 49) to project the next 1–2 months of Existing-Home Sales.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If Pending Home Sales beat monthly percentage forecasts across multiple regions while contract cancellation rates remain low, it signals that buyers are stepping back in and completed sales should improve over the next two months, supporting risk sentiment if bond yields stay steady.

When a fall is plausible

If Pending Home Sales fall sharply below expectations for several months, it warns that high mortgage rates or affordability pressures are choking off housing turnover at the contract stage; however, check active listings to see whether lack of available homes is the main bottleneck.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If the monthly change prints near consensus, regional trends offset each other, or tighter mortgage underwriting causes more signed contracts to stall before closing, markets typically stay range-bound.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose consensus expects Pending Home Sales to rise +1.5% m/m, the prior month's index was 80.0 (prior m/m -1.0%), and the actual index climbs to 84.0. The actual monthly percentage change is (84.0 - 80.0) / 80.0 = +5.0% (+3.5 percentage points above the +1.5% consensus). Notice two things: first, a +4.0 index-point gain from 80.0 is +5.0% (not +4.0%); second, sitting at 84.0 (16 points below the 2001 = 100 benchmark) means contract count is 16% below 2001 volume—it does NOT mean home prices fell 16%.

History and origin

The index uses 2001 average contract activity as 100; the methodology identifies that as the first analyzed year, not necessarily its public launch.

Where and how to find the data

National Association of Realtors NAR · Monthly · Usually 10:00 a.m. U.S. Eastern; confirm the NAR calendar.

National Association of Realtors NAR official data

  1. Open the latest news release and Monthly Index.
  2. Read the contract index, monthly percentage change and regional components.
  3. Open Methodology to confirm 2001=100 and the lag from signing to closing.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Misreading an index level of 84 as 840,000 homes sold, or assuming an index below 100 means house prices have fallen below 2001 levels.
  2. Equating a 4-point index change (from 80 to 84) with a 4% monthly change instead of dividing by the starting base (4 / 80 = +5.0%).
  3. Assuming every signed contract closes on schedule, overlooking that spikes in mortgage rates, failed home inspections, or low appraisals can cause contracts to fall through.
