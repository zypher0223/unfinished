S&P Cotality Case-Shiller Home Price Indices

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The S&P Cotality (formerly CoreLogic) Case-Shiller Home Price Indices, published monthly by S&P Dow Jones Indices, use the repeat-sales methodology developed by Karl Case and Robert Shiller to track price changes across matched sales of the exact same single-family homes over time. By comparing each home only against its own prior sale price, the index strips out the mix shift caused by more or fewer luxury homes selling in a given month. Beginners often overlook its two-month publication lag and three-month moving-average smoothing, or confuse home purchase prices with the rent-based shelter component in CPI.

Start with an everyday comparison

Whereas the median existing-home sales price (Lesson 43) mixes cheap wooden chairs and luxury leather sofas sold today into one middle number, Case-Shiller tags each individual house and compares its new sale price strictly against what that exact same house sold for previously; however, waiting for county deed records and averaging three months means the report looks in the rearview mirror.

What does this number measure?

Reported in index points benchmarked to January 2000 = 100 (2000 Jan = 100), alongside both Seasonally Adjusted (SA) and Non-Seasonally Adjusted (NSA) monthly (m/m %) and annual (y/y %) percentage changes. The flagship series are the U.S. National Index, the 20-City Composite (the most widely forecast headline), and the 10-City Composite. Keep two timing rules in mind: first, the report released at the end of May covers deeds closed in March (a two-month lag); second, each monthly reading is a three-month moving average (so the March print averages closed sales from January, February, and March) covering arms-length single-family resales (excluding new construction and condos).

Why can markets react?

Transmission is indirect and medium-term: repeat-sales home price trends → alter household housing wealth (the wealth effect) and home-equity borrowing capacity while affecting mortgage collateral cushions → filter with a 12-to-18-month lag into housing rent inflation (Shelter / Owners' Equivalent Rent) inside CPI and PCE → shape Federal Reserve views on sticky service inflation and the path of interest rates → influence Treasury yields, equities, and crypto risk appetite. Because of its lag and three-month smoothing, traders use it to confirm broad price and inflation trends rather than sudden weekly turns.

Read the release step by step

  1. Note the two-month lag and three-month window: open the S&P release and check the reference month (e.g., the late-May release reports the Jan–Feb–Mar average for March).
  2. Separate National from 20-City Composite: locate both the U.S. National Index and the 20-City Composite and match the exact series used by your consensus calendar.
  3. Read Seasonally Adjusted (SA) monthly changes: because spring buying naturally lifts unadjusted prices, check the SA m/m % line to judge true monthly momentum.
  4. Inspect city breadth and base effects: check how many of the 20 metro areas rose on the month and compare annual y/y % trends against monthly SA m/m % turns.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

If home prices rise moderately alongside stable employment without re-accelerating sharply, it supports household balance-sheet health and mortgage collateral quality, which can aid equities and crypto if current CPI shelter inflation continues to cool and bond yields stay calm.

When a fall is plausible

If repeat-sales prices fall across most of the 20 cities for consecutive months, concerns over negative wealth effects and housing weakness can grow; conversely, if home prices re-accelerate hot during an inflation fight, fears of sticky future shelter inflation can push Treasury yields up and pressure risk assets.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

If annual y/y growth cools solely due to a high base a year ago while monthly SA prices tick up slightly—or if markets have already priced in the two-month-old deed data—equities and crypto typically show limited immediate reaction.

Work through a made-up example

Hypothetical numbers for illustration only: suppose the March 20-City Composite (released in late May, averaging Jan/Feb/Mar) stood at 300.0 a year ago, 314.0 in February (revised), and prints at 315.0 in March against a +4.8% y/y consensus. The annual y/y change is (315.0 - 300.0) / 300.0 = 15.0 / 300.0 = +5.0% (+0.2 percentage points above consensus), while the single-month change from February is (315.0 - 314.0) / 314.0 = 1.0 / 314.0 ≈ +0.32%. The +5.0% figure is the 12-month gain, not a 5% jump in March, and reflects contracts signed months earlier.

History and origin

The method draws on Karl Case and Robert Shiller repeat-sales research. S&P/Case-Shiller indices launched in 2006, with back-calculated histories earlier.

Where and how to find the data

S&P Dow Jones Indices · Monthly · Usually the last Tuesday of the month at 9:00 a.m. U.S. Eastern; observations are lagged.

S&P Dow Jones Indices official data

  1. Select U.S. National or 20-City within the index family.
  2. Open the latest release and factsheet; note the reference month.
  3. Read monthly and annual changes separately and check the seasonal-adjustment label.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Forgetting the triple lag (1–2 months from contract to closing, a 3-month moving average, and a 2-month publication delay) and treating the late-May release as live May pricing.
  2. Assuming a +5% Case-Shiller home-price print means CPI Shelter inflation will jump 5% next month, when home prices lead official CPI rent measures by roughly 12 to 18 months.
  3. Reading Non-Seasonally Adjusted (NSA) spring price gains as a fresh breakout without checking the Seasonally Adjusted (SA) monthly figure.
