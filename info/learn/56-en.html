Commercial Bank Deposits H.8

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Commercial Bank Deposits in the Federal Reserve's weekly H.8 release track total deposit liabilities owed to households and businesses across commercial banks in the United States, serving as a high-frequency gauge of banking-system funding stability. In accounting terms, while a deposit is a liquid asset to the customer, it is a liability that the bank is obligated to honor upon demand or maturity.

Start with an everyday comparison

Think of bank deposits like customer balances loaded onto a retail chain's prepaid store cards: the store gains working funds to deploy, yet simultaneously carries a binding obligation to honor withdrawals or redemptions whenever customers ask.

What does this number measure?

The weekly H.8 release typically reports deposit balances in billions of dollars; readers must distinguish seasonally adjusted (SA) from not seasonally adjusted (NSA) series, and separate system-wide net outflows from internal deposit migration between large domestically chartered banks (top 25) and smaller regional or community banks. Furthermore, deposits shifting into money market funds to buy Treasury bills or enter the Fed's ON RRP facility do not vanish from the broader dollar financial system.

Why can markets react?

Deposits represent banks' primary low-cost funding base: sustained deposit outflows can force banks to pay higher funding rates, tap costlier wholesale borrowing, or curtail lending, tightening credit conditions for the real economy and equities. For crypto markets, beyond macro credit transmission, deposit instability at crypto-friendly banks can also disrupt fiat on-ramps and stablecoin reserve confidence.

Read the release step by step

  1. Open the latest Federal Reserve H.8 release, locate the Deposits line under All Commercial Banks, and note the billion-dollar units, seasonal-adjustment status (SA vs. NSA), and the Wednesday as-of reference date.
  2. Compare deposit breakdowns across large domestically chartered banks, small domestically chartered banks, and foreign-related institutions to determine whether a weekly move reflects system-wide drainage or a flight-to-safety rotation between bank tiers.
  3. Because H.8 carries no standardized consensus forecast, evaluate multi-week trends, account for predictable tax-date outflows, and inspect the release footnotes for bank mergers, acquisitions, or accounting reclassifications.

Three possible market reactions

These are conditional explanations, not fixed trading rules. The same release can be interpreted differently when inflation, recession or funding stress dominates.

When a rise is plausible

Stabilizing or rebounding deposits across both aggregate and regional bank cohorts alongside narrower wholesale funding spreads can ease banking stress fears and support equities and risk assets.

When a fall is plausible

Persistent multi-week deposit flight forcing banks into expensive wholesale funding, emergency borrowing, or asset sales can spark credit-crunch concerns and weigh heavily on stocks and crypto.

When a mixed or sideways response is plausible

When a fall in small-bank deposits is largely matched by an inflow into large banks, or reflects routine tax-payment and quarter-end calendar effects, aggregate credit conditions may stay stable and leave markets range-bound.

Work through a made-up example

Suppose a weekly SA H.8 report shows small-bank deposits falling by $50 billion while large-bank deposits rise by $45 billion over the same week, leaving total commercial bank deposits down by only $50B - $45B = $5 billion. Analysts must recognize that $45 billion was an intra-banking migration and only $5 billion left commercial bank deposit accounts (often into money funds), rather than claiming $50 billion vanished from the U.S. financial system. History and origin

H.8 builds on longstanding weekly banking data whose collection traces to 1917; today’s deposit definitions have evolved.

Where and how to find the data

Federal Reserve Board · Weekly · H.8 normally Friday at 4:15 p.m. U.S. Eastern; holidays can shift publication.

Federal Reserve Board official data

  1. Open the latest H.8 release.
  2. Select All Commercial Banks and find Deposits, then large and small bank categories.
  3. Read adjusted weekly levels and footnotes for mergers, reclassifications and other breaks.

Check the current official release calendar. Time zones, daylight saving, holidays and revisions can change what you see; never assume a displayed local time is New York time.

Common mistakes

  1. Do not declare a systemic bank run from a single week's unadjusted deposit drop without checking tax payment dates, quarter-end window dressing, and H.8 merger/reclassification footnotes, and do not confuse shifts into money market funds with destroyed money.
  2. Markets react to marginal new information relative to expectations. Because H.8 reports lagged Wednesday snapshots, check whether regional bank equities and funding spreads already priced the move, and verify confirmation across Treasury yields, the dollar, and broad indices.
