Seasonality & Calendar Effects

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Market folklore is filled with calendar adages like the 'January Effect' or 'Sell in May and Go Away'. While some stem from tax harvesting, institutional rebalancing, or agricultural cycles, many are data-mined flukes that fade once widely publicized.

DefinitionSeasonality and Calendar Effects refer to empirical market anomalies where asset returns exhibit historical statistical deviations during specific months, quarters, days of the week, or holiday windows. Historical Period Mean Return = Sum of Period Returns across N Years / N. They represent long-run sample averages rather than deterministic causal rules.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Seasonality is like historical climate averages: October is cooler on average, yet any single October day can still bring a heatwave.

A made-up example

Illustration only: Over a 20-year sample, an equity index rose in 13 Januaries and fell in 7, yielding a mean January return of +1.5%. In one year of that sample, rate-hike fears drove an 8.5% January drop. A trader who went heavily long on the '+1.5% January average' without risk controls lost 8.5% in a single month. A positive long-run average does not rule out a sharp loss in any given year. These figures are hypothetical.

Who developed or popularized it?

Sidney B. Wachtel and Financial Anomaly Research

Sidney B. Wachtel's 1942 Journal of Business article 'Certain Observations on Seasonal Movements in Stock Prices' was an early record of the year-start seasonal rise later called the January Effect; Donald Keim (1983) showed it was concentrated in small caps, and Sven Bouman & Ben Jacobsen (2002) studied 'Sell in May' (the Halloween indicator).

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Identify whether a structural mechanism backs the pattern, such as year-end tax-loss harvesting, quarter-end window dressing, or physical commodity cycles.
  2. Inspect multi-decade return distributions across both mean and median metrics to ensure a few extreme outlier years are not skewing the average.
  3. Compare recent 10-year sub-samples against older decades to check if front-running arbitrage has arbitraged the anomaly away.
  4. Use seasonality strictly as a secondary environmental filter alongside current macro liquidity and price action, never as a standalone trigger.
  5. Beware of data-mined calendar curiosities (e.g., 'the third Tuesday of odd months') that lack economic causality.

The mistake to avoid

Treating a slight multi-decade statistical average as a guaranteed annual event, ignoring that a single macro regime shift can inflict heavy losses.
