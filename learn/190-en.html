Backtesting & Overfitting

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Backtesting tests trading ideas against history, yet pristine equity curves frequently hide statistical traps. Tweaking parameters to maximize past returns mostly memorizes random noise. Separating sample sets and deducting real friction prevents costly self-deception.

DefinitionBacktesting is the quantitative simulation of a codified trading rule across historical market data; Net Backtest Return = Gross Simulated Profit - Cumulative Commissions - Cumulative Slippage. Overfitting (curve-fitting) occurs when a model's parameters are excessively tailored to historical In-Sample noise, resulting in severe performance degradation on Out-of-Sample data and live markets.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Overfitting is like memorizing last year's answer key letter-by-letter for this year's exam; any new question causes failure.

A made-up example

Illustration only: An intraday strategy simulates 100 trades in 2023 with a gross profit of $50,000. However, round-trip commission and slippage cost $450 per trade, totaling $45,000 (100 * $450) in friction and leaving only $5,000 net profit. After adding five indicator filters to boost historical win rates to 85%, the strategy loses $12,000 on 2024 out-of-sample data. Ignoring friction and curve-fitting parameters creates illusory returns.

Who developed or popularized it?

Quantitative Finance Academia and the Basel Committee

Computerized strategy backtesting expanded in the 1970s; the Basel Committee mandated risk-model backtesting in 1996, and researchers like Marcos López de Prado later formalized the Deflated Sharpe Ratio to quantify backtest overfitting.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Split historical data strictly into In-Sample (for development) and Out-of-Sample (for blind validation) segments.
  2. Configure realistic round-trip commissions, exchange fees, and execution slippage in the simulation engine.
  3. Audit code for look-ahead bias (using future unclosed prices) and survivorship bias (excluding delisted bankrupt tickers).
  4. Minimize free parameters and test parameter sensitivity to confirm a broad stability plateau rather than a fragile spike.
  5. Verify sufficient sample size across diverse market regimes so results are statistically meaningful rather than lucky outliers.

The mistake to avoid

Repeatedly tweaking indicator parameters across the entire dataset until the equity curve looks flawless, mistaking curve-fitted historical noise for predictive edge.
