Order Book Depth & Liquidity Heatmaps

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Order book depth shows how much is resting at each price right now. A heatmap draws this as bright bands that look like walls. But orders can be pulled at any moment, so the wall may be gone when price arrives; only executed volume shows real trading there.

DefinitionOrder book depth lists the resting buy and sell orders at each price; a liquidity heatmap plots those resting orders over time as colours, brighter where more size rests. Orders can be cancelled or modified at any time; a heatmap shows what was resting at the time, not what traded, and is not the same as support or resistance.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like a “we buy in bulk” sign in a shop window: it can be taken down at any time, and the sign is not a trade.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a heatmap shows a bright bid band of about 300 BTC at 59,500. When price falls to 59,600 the band disappears and price passes straight through 59,500: the orders were pulled and formed no support.

Who developed or popularized it?

A basic market-microstructure concept; heatmaps popularised by tools such as Bookmap

The order book is the basis of exchange matching; plotting order-book history as a heatmap was popularised by professional tools such as Bookmap, and its originator is unverified.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Confirm which exchange and pair the book belongs to; one exchange is only part of the market.
  2. Find prices where resting size clusters and note the size and when it appeared.
  3. As price approaches, watch whether the orders are filled or pulled.
  4. Treat only executed trades and price reaction as evidence; resting orders are just clues.

The mistake to avoid

Treating bright heatmap bands as guaranteed support or resistance is the most common misconception; orders can be cancelled and some are placed to mislead.

From the book: cis Stock Trading

Source: cis, cis Stock Trading (Chinese edition, translated by Zhu Jia, China Youth Press, 2020, ISBN 978-7-5153-6031-7; Japanese original published by KADOKAWA, 2018)

What the author says

In the 2005 J-Com fat-finger incident, a sell order for about 40 times the company’s shares outstanding hit the book. The author checked the prospectus to confirm it was an error, bought in small manual orders, then sold everything at the first limit-up in case trades were cancelled. It shows that an abnormal order should first be checked for plausibility, and that rule risk exists in extreme events.

This is our English summary of the author’s view; the Chinese page of this lesson carries the original excerpt. The amounts and events are the author’s own experience, mostly in Japanese markets, and nothing here is trading advice.
