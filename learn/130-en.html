Pin Bars

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

DefinitionA pin bar is a single candle whose main shadow is more than twice its body, with the body near one end of the range and a short or absent opposite shadow; a bullish pin bar has a long lower shadow and a body near the top, while a bearish pin bar has a long upper shadow and a body near the bottom.

A pin bar looks like a needle pushed back: price probes far in one direction and closes near the other end, showing that the probed level was not held in that bar.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like reaching far for something and pulling your hand back; reaching far does not mean you held it.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a pin bar opens at 99.06, closes at 99.36, with a high of 99.46 and a low of 96.36, so its 0.30 body has a 2.70 lower shadow, more than twice the body, and a 0.10 upper shadow, fitting the bullish pin bar shape.

Who developed or popularized it?

No single inventor verified; Pin Bar is a generic price-action term

Pin Bar is often explained as short for Pinocchio Bar, but accounts vary, so this lesson uses only the checkable shape definition and does not present a circulated story as a verified origin.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Measure three parts first: main shadow, body and opposite shadow, rather than only noting a long shadow.
  2. Note which end the body sits at: a long lower shadow with a top body is bullish in shape, a long upper shadow with a bottom body is bearish in shape.
  3. Note the location: near a key level, in a trend pullback, or in the middle of a range.
  4. Write down the invalidation point: a bullish shape fails when price closes below the shadow low, and a bearish shape fails when price closes above the shadow high.

The mistake to avoid

A common mistake is to call every long shadow a pin bar. Shadow length, small body, closing location and location must hold together, and a long shadow in the middle of a range carries less weight.
