Options Basics

Options are time-decaying derivative contracts, not simple stock proxies. Purchasing options exposes traders to constant time decay (Theta); even if directional bias is correct, slow movement will cause the entire premium to expire worthless.

DefinitionAn option is a standardized derivative contract conveying the right, but not the obligation, to buy (Call) or sell (Put) an underlying asset at a designated strike price on or before a specified expiration date. Option Premium = Intrinsic Value + Time Value. An option buyer's loss is capped at the premium paid, whereas the option seller assumes substantial or theoretically uncapped asymmetric risk.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Buying options is like buying car insurance: the buyer pays a premium that decays to zero if no disaster occurs, but pays out if a crash happens.

A made-up example

Illustration only: A stock trades at $100. A trader buys a 1-month $105 strike Call option for a $3.00 premium. If the stock reaches $112 at expiration, the intrinsic value is $7 ($112 - $105), generating a net profit of $4 ($7 - $3, +133%). However, if the stock only reaches $103, the option expires out-of-the-money with zero intrinsic value, resulting in a total loss of the $3.00 premium (-100%).

Who developed or popularized it?

Chicago Board Options Exchange (Cboe) and the Black-Scholes Model

The Chicago Board Options Exchange (Cboe) launched the first standardized exchange-traded options in 1973. That same year, Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published their landmark pricing model, formalizing quantitative options valuation.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Open the standardized Option Chain to review strike prices, bid-ask spreads, and expiration cycles.
  2. Deconstruct the option premium into Intrinsic Value (moneyness) and Time Value (extrinsic uncertainty).
  3. Differentiate Historical Volatility (past realized standard deviation) from Implied Volatility (market-priced forward expectations).
  4. Keep long option position sizes modest to withstand inevitable time decay (Theta attrition).
  5. Never sell uncovered/naked short options without strict collateral, avoiding catastrophic open-ended liabilities.

The mistake to avoid

Assuming that being directionally right guarantees a profit, overlooking that failing to outpace Theta decay or an IV crush can still wipe out 100% of the option premium.
