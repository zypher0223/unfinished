Reading Financial Statements

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Financial statements reflect a company's operational health. Beginners often assume positive net income guarantees safety, unaware that paper profits can be inflated with unpaid receivables while survival depends entirely on real cash.

DefinitionThe three financial statements are the standardized reporting framework: the Income Statement (Net Income = Revenue - Expenses - Taxes) measures operational profitability over a period; the Balance Sheet (Assets = Liabilities + Equity) captures financial condition at a point in time; and the Cash Flow Statement (Ending Cash = Beginning Cash + Operating + Investing + Financing Cash Flows) tracks actual cash inflows and outflows.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

The income statement is a school report card, the balance sheet is a net worth snapshot, and the cash flow statement is the circulatory bloodstream.

A made-up example

Illustration only: A business sells $100,000 worth of goods on credit with payment due next year, booking $100,000 in accounts receivable. It pays $70,000 in cash production expenses during the quarter. The income statement reports a net profit of $30,000 ($100,000 - $70,000), but operating cash flow is -$70,000. Despite positive accounting profit, the company experienced an actual net cash drain.

Who developed or popularized it?

Luca Pacioli and the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)

Italian mathematician Luca Pacioli codified double-entry bookkeeping in 1494. After the Great Depression, the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 required public companies to disclose audited annual financial statements, and the 1934 Act created the SEC. Quarterly 10-Q reports only became mandatory in 1970, and they are typically reviewed rather than fully audited.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Search the issuer's latest 10-K or 10-Q filing on official platforms like SEC EDGAR, CNINFO, or HKEXnews.
  2. Review the Income Statement to check revenue trajectory, gross margins, and recurring net earnings.
  3. Inspect the Cash Flow Statement to verify whether operating cash flow matches net income and remains reliably positive.
  4. Examine the Balance Sheet for debt maturity schedules, cash reserves, and short-term obligations.
  5. Cross-check notes for anomalies; if reported profit climbs while operating cash flow turns sharply negative, evaluate receivable aging risks.

The mistake to avoid

Confusing accrual accounting net income with liquid cash in the bank, overlooking that uncollected receivables or obsolete inventories can trigger insolvency.
