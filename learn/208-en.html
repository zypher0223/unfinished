Greeks I: Delta & Gamma

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

After learning calls, puts, and intrinsic versus time value (see the "Options Basics" lesson on this site), a natural question arises: if the underlying stock moves by $1, how much will my option price change? Delta gives that instantaneous conversion ratio, yet Delta itself is a moving target. Gamma is the force that makes Delta accelerate or decelerate. Understanding how Delta and Gamma interact explains why option payoffs are nonlinear and why at-the-money contracts near expiration swing violently.

DefinitionDelta (Δ) measures how much an option's theoretical price changes for a 1-unit move in the underlying asset; Call Delta ranges from 0 to 1, while Put Delta ranges from -1 to 0. Traders also loosely read absolute Delta as a rough proxy for the probability of expiring in-the-money, though it is not a true statistical probability. Gamma (Γ) measures how much Delta itself changes for a 1-unit move in the underlying price; at-the-money (ATM) options approaching expiration carry the highest Gamma.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

If the underlying asset's price move is the distance traveled by a car, Delta is the car's current speed (how much the option price moves per dollar of stock movement), while Gamma is the acceleration pedal (how fast that speed itself changes).

A made-up example

Illustration only: suppose a stock trades at $100, and an at-the-money $100 strike Call is priced at $3.00 with a Delta of 0.50 and a Gamma of 0.08. If the stock rises $1 from $100 to $101, the option price increases by roughly the initial Delta plus half of Gamma: $3.00 + $0.50 + 0.5 × $0.08 = $3.54 (often approximated in quick mental math as ~$3.50), while the new Delta rises from 0.50 to 0.50 + 0.08 = 0.58. If the stock rises another $1 from $101 to $102, the option now gains roughly $0.58 + 0.5 × $0.08 = $0.62, reaching about $4.16.

Who developed or popularized it?

Derived from partial-derivative sensitivity analysis in the 1973 Black–Scholes–Merton option pricing framework

In 1973, Fischer Black, Myron Scholes, and Robert C. Merton published the option pricing framework built on dynamic Delta hedging. Market makers and risk managers standardized the first and second partial derivatives of option value with respect to the underlying price as Delta and Gamma across Cboe and global derivatives desks.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Locate the Delta and Gamma columns in the option chain and verify the sign convention (positive Delta for Calls, negative Delta for Puts).
  2. Use Delta to estimate option price sensitivity for small underlying moves, noting that a single snapshot cannot capture large gap moves.
  3. Check moneyness via Delta magnitude: deep out-of-the-money options sit near 0, at-the-money options sit near 0.50, and deep in-the-money options approach 1.00.
  4. Monitor Gamma as expiration approaches: near-expiry at-the-money options exhibit steep Gamma, causing Delta to whip rapidly between 0 and 1 as the underlying crosses the strike.

The mistake to avoid

Treating entry Delta as fixed over the life of the trade, or equating a 0.30 Delta with a literal 30% real-world chance of making money; Delta does not predict true return distributions and ignores Gamma swings during large price jumps.
