FX Quotes: Currency Pairs, Direction & Spread

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

CME Group’s course explains that the first currency in a pair is the base and the second the quote currency, and trading acts on the base: buying EUR/USD at 1.1250 means paying 1.1250 dollars for 1 euro. Institutions also write rates differently: the Federal Reserve’s weekly H.10 table shows most currencies as units per US dollar, but the euro, pound, Australian dollar and New Zealand dollar as US dollars per unit; the European Central Bank’s reference rates use the euro as the base for every currency. Quotes usually have two prices, the bid and the ask, and the gap between them is the spread, the direct cost of trading. Leveraged FX trading is risky: the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) warns that the forex market is volatile and carries substantial risks, and that trading on margin can lose more than you deposited.

DefinitionCurrencies are quoted in pairs: EUR/USD = 1.1250 means 1 euro buys 1.1250 US dollars. The currency before the slash is the base currency and the one after is the quote (or terms) currency; buying EUR/USD means buying euros and paying dollars. Some pairs put the dollar second (GBP/USD), others first (USD/JPY, USD/CHF), so check the direction before reading a quote.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like the rate board at a bank’s exchange counter: the bank’s buying and selling prices differ, and the gap is the counter’s income; first check whether the board shows “one unit of foreign currency in local money” or the reverse.

A made-up example

Illustration only: EUR/USD is quoted 1.1248 / 1.1250 (bid first, ask second). You buy 10,000 euros at 1.1250, paying 10,000 × 1.1250 = USD 11,250; selling straight back at 1.1248 returns only 10,000 × 1.1248 = USD 11,248, and the 11,250 − 11,248 = USD 2 gap is the spread cost. Trading apps often call 0.0001 in the fourth decimal place here one “pip”, so this spread is 2 pips; yen pairs use different decimals, so check your platform. Now USD/JPY = 150 means 1 dollar buys 150 yen; when it becomes 135, a dollar buys fewer yen, meaning the yen has strengthened and the dollar weakened.

Who developed or popularized it?

Currency-pair quoting is standard FX convention; this lesson follows CME Group, the Federal Reserve’s H.10, the ECB and the CFTC

CME Group’s “Understanding FX Quote Conventions” covers base and quote currencies and American versus European terms; the Fed’s H.10 notes most rates are in currency units per dollar, with starred currencies (including the euro and pound) in dollars per unit; the ECB’s reference rates use the euro as base; the CFTC’s forex fraud advisory stresses high risk and margin losses.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Find the base currency first: the quote tells you how much quote currency one unit of base currency is worth.
  2. Separate bid from ask; the spread is the direct cost of every trade.
  3. Read the direction: USD/JPY rising means a stronger dollar; EUR/USD rising means a stronger euro.
  4. Leveraged FX can lose more than your deposit; be wary of FX platforms promising high returns (see the CFTC fraud advisory).

The mistake to avoid

Reading a rise in USD/JPY as a stronger yen, or using high leverage because the spread is small. Reading the direction backwards flips your whole judgement; a small spread does not mean small risk, since leverage decides how fast losses grow.
