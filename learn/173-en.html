Trading Psychology & Biases

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Many losses come not from misreading charts but from perfectly human reactions: fear of losing, rushing to bank gains, feeling that “after such a rise it must fall.” You cannot remove these reactions, but you can write rules before trading and check your records afterwards to see which bias keeps catching you.

DefinitionCommon biases in trading include the gambler’s fallacy (thinking a run of gains means a fall is “due,” or a run of losses means a win is due), loss aversion (a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases), the disposition effect (selling winners too early and holding losers too long), anchoring (judging cheap or expensive against a past price) and hindsight bias (treating explanations written afterwards as if they were foreseeable). This lesson aims to recognize them and reduce their effect on decisions with rules written in advance.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like not keeping snacks at home when dieting: not because you believe you have no appetite, but because you admit you do and arrange things in advance.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a stock rises from 20 to 30 and you skip it because it is “50% dearer than before”; that anchors on 20. Another falls from 30 to 20 and you buy because it looks “cheap”; that is the same anchor. The useful question is the current structure and risk, not whether price is above or below some past level.

Who developed or popularized it?

Behavioral finance: Kahneman and Tversky, Shefrin and Statman, and others

Kahneman and Tversky’s 1979 prospect theory described loss aversion, and they explained the gambler’s fallacy through the representativeness heuristic. Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman coined the term “disposition effect” in a 1985 paper. cis draws on his own losses to describe the illusion that wins and losses must even out, the rush to lock in gains, and judging price against the past.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Before trading, write the reason, the invalidation and the target, so you cannot invent reasons afterwards.
  2. After each trade, note your emotion (fear of missing out, fear of loss, urge to win it back) and review weekly.
  3. Check against a bias list: shorting because of a long rise, sizing up after losses, selling early because of an open gain.
  4. Turn any recurring bias into a concrete rule, such as “never average down a loser” or “exit winners by trailing the stop, not by mood.”

The mistake to avoid

Believing that knowing about biases is enough to avoid them; what works is rules set beforehand and records kept afterwards, not willpower in the moment.

From the book: cis Stock Trading

Source: cis, cis Stock Trading (Chinese edition, translated by Zhu Jia, China Youth Press, 2020, ISBN 978-7-5153-6031-7; Japanese original published by KADOKAWA, 2018)

What the author says

The book returns repeatedly to how human nature gets in the way: fear of losses, rushing to lock in gains, anchoring to past prices (‘expensive compared with what?’), trusting media explanations written after the fact, and assuming a widely shared tactic still works (the first loach is delicious, the second still fine, the third optional). He also lost heavily early on by buying ‘cheap’ stocks on his own valuation. This lesson organizes these into common biases with self-check questions.

This is our English summary of the author’s view; the Chinese page of this lesson carries the original excerpt. The amounts and events are the author’s own experience, mostly in Japanese markets, and nothing here is trading advice.
