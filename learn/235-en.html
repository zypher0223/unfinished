NFT Basics

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Many people think buying an NFT means buying an image. In fact the chain usually records only the token ID and its holder; the image may be on-chain, on IPFS (distributed storage addressed by content) or on the project’s own server, and if that server stops, the image may fail to load or be swapped. Whether you may use it commercially depends on the licence the project publishes. Royalties (a share paid to the creator on each resale) have a standard, ERC-2981, which is also Final, but it only defines how to look up royalty information, and its text states that payment is voluntary, so marketplaces may not enforce it. Also, the “floor price” is the lowest asking price among listings, not a price you can sell at; when bids are thin, selling means accepting less.

DefinitionAn NFT (non-fungible token) is a token where every ID is unique. Common standards are ERC-721 (each token has its own ID) and ERC-1155 (one contract can manage both fungible and non-fungible tokens); both are Final in the official Ethereum repository, so they are formal standards. On-chain, an NFT records which address holds which ID; images and other content usually sit wherever the metadata (information describing the token) points. Owning an NFT is not the same as owning the copyright.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

An NFT is like a numbered collector’s certificate: you hold the certificate, but where the painting hangs and whether you may print copies depends on the terms written on the back.

A made-up example

Illustration only: Chen holds 10 NFTs from a collection with a floor (lowest listing) of 0.5 ETH, so the paper value is 10 × 0.5 = 5 ETH. But the bids are 3 at 0.45 ETH and the next 7 at 0.3 ETH. Selling all now brings 3 × 0.45 + 7 × 0.3 = 1.35 + 2.1 = 3.45 ETH, which is 5 − 3.45 = 1.55 ETH below paper value, before fees and royalties.

Who developed or popularized it?

ERC-721 was proposed in 2018, ERC-1155 the same year, and ERC-2981 in 2020

All three are Final in the official Ethereum EIP repository. ERC-721 defines an interface for individually numbered tokens; ERC-1155 lets one contract manage many token types; ERC-2981 only defines how to query royalties and does not force payment.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Check the contract address: copycat collections reuse names and images, so confirm the contract and token ID on an explorer or the project’s official channels.
  2. See where the content lives: on-chain, on IPFS, or on the project’s server, which may stop serving it.
  3. Read the licence: whether holders may use or adapt the work commercially depends on the project’s stated terms.
  4. Look at real sales and bid depth rather than the floor alone; volume can also be inflated by the same people trading back and forth.

The mistake to avoid

Believing an NFT gives you the copyright, or valuing your holdings at the floor price. Copyright depends on the licence; the floor is what sellers ask, not what you can get.
