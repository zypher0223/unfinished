Greeks II: Theta & Vega

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

DefinitionTheta (Θ) measures the daily decline in an option's theoretical value due purely to the passage of one calendar day, holding other inputs constant (typically negative for option buyers). Vega (ν) measures how much an option's theoretical price changes for a 1-percentage-point (1%) change in Implied Volatility (IV); option buyers hold positive Vega while option sellers hold negative Vega across both Calls and Puts.

Why can an option buyer guess the stock's direction correctly and still lose money? Beyond underlying price moves (Delta and Gamma), the passage of time and shifts in expected volatility constantly reshape an option's time value (see the "Options Basics" lesson on this site). Theta measures the daily rent eroded by the calendar, while Vega measures how rising or falling implied volatility inflates or deflates option premiums. Buyers and sellers sit on opposite sides of both forces.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Think of an option's time value as an ice cube sitting outdoors: Theta is the steady drip of daily melting that speeds up as the deadline nears, while Vega is the outside weather—rising implied volatility refreezes and thickens the ice, whereas falling implied volatility is a sudden heatwave that shrinks it rapidly.

A made-up example

Illustration only: suppose a stock trades at $100, and an at-the-money Call with 20 days to expiration costs $4.00, with Theta of -0.08, Vega of 0.15, and Implied Volatility (IV) at 30%. If one day passes with the stock still at $100 while calming sentiment drops IV from 30% to 26% (-4 percentage points), the option loses $0.08 from Theta and 4 × $0.15 = $0.60 from Vega, bringing its theoretical price to $4.00 - $0.08 - $0.60 = $3.32—a $0.68 one-day loss for the buyer and a $0.68 gain for the seller.

Who developed or popularized it?

Theta comes from time sensitivity in the Black–Scholes–Merton framework; "Vega" is not a Greek letter but an industry convention

In the Black–Scholes–Merton framework, the partial derivative of option value with respect to time is called Theta (Θ). Sensitivity to volatility (σ) is called "Vega" by practitioners; it is not a letter of the Greek alphabet and is commonly explained as borrowing the V of Volatility, sometimes written with the symbol ν.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Read Theta and Vega on the option chain, noting that Theta is quoted per calendar day while Vega reflects a 1-percentage-point move in IV.
  2. Compare decay curves across expirations: at-the-money Theta decay is nonlinear and accelerates sharply during the final 30 days to 1 week before expiry.
  3. Compare Vega across maturities: longer-dated options carry higher dollar Vega sensitivity to a 1-point shift in implied volatility than near-term options.
  4. Audit your position side: long option holders benefit from rising IV (+Vega) while paying daily time decay (-Theta); short option sellers collect daily Theta but face losses if IV spikes (-Vega).

The mistake to avoid

Treating short-option Theta collection as effortless rent or assuming an option only changes by Theta when the underlying stays flat; Theta alone cannot protect a seller against a sudden volatility spike (Vega shock) that wipes out weeks of time decay.
