Triple Screen

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Triple Screen is a rule-based version of multi-timeframe analysis: the long timeframe decides which direction is allowed, the intermediate waits for a pullback, and the short one picks the entry. All three must pass in order; if any fails, do nothing.

DefinitionTriple Screen assigns jobs to three timeframes about five times apart: screen one (long, e.g. weekly) uses a trend indicator to set the only direction to trade; screen two (intermediate, e.g. daily) uses an oscillator to find pullbacks against that trend, such as an oversold reading in an uptrend; screen three (short or intraday) uses price itself for entry, such as a buy stop above the prior bar’s high.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like three security gates: first you are allowed into the building, then onto the floor, and only then can you open the door.

Who developed or popularized it?

Alexander Elder

Alexander Elder introduced Triple Screen in the 1980s and described it in “Trading for a Living” (1993); later books updated his indicator choices.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. On the long timeframe, set direction with a trend indicator (such as the slope of the MACD histogram).
  2. On the intermediate timeframe, wait for an oscillator reading against that trend.
  3. On the short timeframe, let price decide the entry, for example a break above the prior bar’s high.
  4. If any screen’s condition disappears (for example the long-term trend turns), stop trading in the original direction.

The mistake to avoid

Using the screens out of order, or acting on intermediate signals while the long timeframe points the other way, removes the filtering that is the system’s point.
