Exchanges, Brokers & Trading Rules

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

“Buying a stock” works differently depending on the market and the hour: whether orders can fill, the size of one lot, and whether you can sell the same day all vary. Learning the hours, minimum unit, price limits and fees before opening an account avoids many needless traps.

DefinitionAn exchange matches buyers and sellers (for example the NYSE, Nasdaq, and the Shanghai and Shenzhen exchanges); ordinary investors open an account with a broker to place orders. Rules differ by market: A-shares use T+1 (shares bought today can be sold from the next trading day) and most main-board stocks have a 10% daily price limit; US regular trading runs 9:30–16:00 Eastern Time with no uniform per-stock limit, though circuit breakers exist; crypto exchanges mostly trade around the clock.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like driving in different countries: it is still driving, but which side of the road and the speed limit depend on local rules.

A made-up example

Illustration only: buying 100 A-shares (one lot) at 10.00 costs 1,000. A 0.025% commission is about 0.25, but many brokers charge a 5 minimum per trade, so you pay 5; selling adds stamp duty of 0.05% of the amount. Actual rates depend on your broker.

Who developed or popularized it?

Rules set by each exchange and regulator; no single originator

The NYSE traces its roots to the Buttonwood Agreement of 1792; the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges opened in 1990. A-share stamp duty on sales was halved to 0.05% from 28 August 2023, and US stocks moved to T+1 settlement on 28 May 2024.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Choose the market: A-shares, Hong Kong, US stocks or crypto, each with different hours.
  2. Check the minimum trading unit and whether you can sell the same day (for example A-shares are T+1 with 100-share lots).
  3. Learn the rules on daily limits, circuit breakers or trading halts, and whether pre-market and after-hours trading is allowed.
  4. Write down commissions, stamp duty and platform fees; minimum charges matter most for small trades.

The mistake to avoid

Assuming every market lets you buy and sell the same day at any time, or ignoring minimum commissions and trading tiny amounts so often that fees eat most of the gains.
