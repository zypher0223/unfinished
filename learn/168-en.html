Moving Average Alignment

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Bullish alignment means several moving averages are stacked short, medium, long from top to bottom and all rising, showing recent average prices have stayed above earlier ones. It describes a trend that has already happened; when it turns, the alignment usually breaks only after price has moved a fair way.

DefinitionBullish alignment: the short moving average is above the medium, and the medium above the long (such as MA5 > MA10 > MA20), with all three rising; bearish alignment is the reverse. The alignment breaks when the short average crosses below the medium or long one. Moving averages are computed from past prices, so alignment always lags price.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like three people climbing a hill in a line, the fastest in front, all heading up.

A made-up example

Illustration only: at one bar MA5 = 103.2, MA10 = 102.6 and MA20 = 101.8, and all three are higher than five bars earlier: a bullish alignment. When MA5 later drops below MA10, the alignment breaks.

Who developed or popularized it?

No single inventor verified; common in moving-average teaching

Watching how several averages stack is long-established in moving-average analysis (Joseph Granville proposed moving-average rules in the 1960s); “bullish/bearish alignment” is common wording in Chinese teaching.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Add three moving averages of different lengths, such as 5, 10 and 20.
  2. Check the order: short on top, long at the bottom, all rising means bullish alignment.
  3. Watch for the short average crossing below the medium as the sign the alignment has broken.
  4. Remember the lag: by the time it breaks, price has usually fallen some way from its high.

The mistake to avoid

Assuming a bullish alignment means prices will keep rising, and chasing long after it formed, is a common misconception; alignment describes the past, not the future.
