Bond Prices & Yields

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

FINRA’s investor education reminds readers that bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions: when rates rise, bond prices generally fall; coupons are generally paid semiannually. US Treasury notes come in 2, 3, 5, 7 and 10-year terms and pay a fixed rate every six months. Keep two numbers apart: the coupon rate is printed on the bond and fixed; the yield is the actual return based on the price you pay, so the lower the price, the higher the yield. The longer the term, the more rate changes move the price, which FINRA calls duration risk. Held to maturity without default, a bond repays face value; sold early, it fetches the market price of the day, which may be a gain or a loss. The Treasury yield curve in information lesson 72 is simply yields across maturities joined into a line.

DefinitionA bond is an IOU from a borrower (a government or company) to investors, promising periodic interest (the coupon) and repayment of principal (face value) at maturity. An existing bond’s coupon is fixed; when market rates rise, new bonds pay more interest, so old bonds must fall in price to sell, which is why bond prices and interest rates (yields) move in opposite directions. The further a bond is from maturity, the more its price reacts to rate changes.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like a seesaw: when the interest-rate end goes up, the bond-price end goes down, and the further from the pivot (the longer the term), the bigger the swing.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a bond maturing in 1 year with face value 100 and a 5% coupon pays 100 + 5 = 105 at maturity. If comparable bonds yield 5%, its price is 105 ÷ 1.05 = 100. If market rates rise to 6%, buyers want a 6% return and the price becomes 105 ÷ 1.06 ≈ 99.06, down about 0.94; if rates fall to 4%, the price is 105 ÷ 1.04 ≈ 100.96. Now a 10-year zero-coupon bond paying 100 at maturity: at 5% its price is 100 ÷ 1.05^10 ≈ 61.39, and at 6% it is 100 ÷ 1.06^10 ≈ 55.84, a fall of (61.39 − 55.84) ÷ 61.39 ≈ 9.0%. The same one-point rise in rates hits the 10-year bond far harder.

Who developed or popularized it?

The inverse price–yield link is basic bond pricing; this lesson follows FINRA and the US Treasury’s TreasuryDirect

FINRA’s bond page states that prices and rates move in opposite directions, that coupons are generally paid semiannually, and explains interest-rate and duration risk; TreasuryDirect says Treasury notes run 2, 3, 5, 7 or 10 years and pay interest every six months.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Identify the three basics: face value, coupon rate and maturity date.
  2. Separate the coupon rate from the yield: the coupon is fixed, the yield changes with the price you pay.
  3. If rates may rise, longer-dated bonds fall more.
  4. Held to maturity without default, you get face value back; sold early, you get the market price, possibly at a loss.

The mistake to avoid

Assuming bonds are “safe” so their prices cannot fall. Selling early after rates rise means a loss, larger for longer terms, and a default can cost even the principal.
