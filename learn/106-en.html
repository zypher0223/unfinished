Percent Above MA

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Percent Above MA (a market-breadth indicator calculating the percentage of stocks in an index or exchange that are currently trading above their own N-day moving average) asks what share of the market's individual stocks are holding above their own 20-, 50-, or 200-day trend baselines. It plots between 0% and 100% in a lower pane. Beginners often assume a low reading like 15% ensures an immediate market bottom.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Instead of checking only the class's overall average score, it counts what percentage of students beat their own personal two-month average today: 85% means broad participation, whereas 25% while the class average stays high means a handful of top students are carrying the rest.

A made-up example

Illustration only: Percent Above MA = (Number of Stocks with Close > Individual N-day MA / Total Stocks in Universe) × 100%. In a 500-stock index, if 350 stocks close above their own 200-day SMA, the reading is (350 / 500) × 100% = 70%. If broad pullbacks leave only 200 stocks above their 200-day SMA (150 fewer stocks), the reading drops to (200 / 500) × 100% = 40%, meaning 60% (300 stocks) are now below their 200-day lines even if mega-cap leaders keep the headline index near its highs. Breadth narrowed, though mega-caps need not fall immediately.

First: why look at this indicator?

Percent of Stocks Above Moving Average measures what percentage of stocks inside a defined universe are currently trading above their own individual N-day moving average (for example, each stock's own 200-day simple moving average). Think of it as counting what percentage of students in a class scored above their own personal 200-day average—rather than averaging the whole class together. This breadth indicator answers how large the group of stocks in an uptrend relative to their own average is; it does not tell you what price to pay for a specific stock.

When it is most useful

Helpful for checking whether an index advance is backed by a growing majority of stocks holding above their own moving averages, or whether participation has been improving over recent weeks. Fix the stock universe and moving-average length (e.g., 20-day, 50-day, or 200-day) first: a 20-day breadth percentage swings much faster than a 200-day breadth percentage, so never mix the two lengths and mistake their difference for a data conflict.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

The percentage curve and 0–100 right-hand scale in the breadth pane
A reading of 70 means 70% of the constituent stocks in that universe closed above their own chosen moving average—not that the index rose 70%, and not that your individual stock sits 70% above its moving average. Check the ticker symbol to confirm both the universe and MA length.
The 50% horizontal reference line alongside the matching market index above
50% simply marks the threshold where half the stocks sit above their own MA and half sit below—it is a headcount dividing line, not a magic buy/sell trigger. The main chart shows the index price level while the lower pane shows the percentage of participating members.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: The percentage climbs steadily from 30% to 60%

What it suggests: A growing share of constituent stocks has reclaimed its own moving average, showing broadening participation.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean those stocks gained 30% in price, nor does it guarantee the improvement will continue.

You see: The market index prints a new high while the percentage of stocks above their MA declines

What it suggests: The index climbed while more individual members slipped back below their own moving averages, pointing to narrower concentration.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not assure the index will drop immediately—watch how long the divergence persists.

You see: The percentage drops to a very low reading (such as below 15%)

What it suggests: The vast majority of constituent stocks are trading below their own historical moving averages.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not automatically mean stocks are fundamentally cheap or that the bottom is in, as weak breadth can persist during bear markets.

The easiest mistake to make

Mixing up thresholds for the 20-day, 50-day, and 200-day versions when the 20-day ratio swings between 15% and 85% much more often than the slow 200-day ratio; shorting a strong bull market simply because 85% of stocks sit above their 50-day average; catching falling knives on the first low reading before price stabilizes.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Hypothetical 500-stock index universe evaluating each stock against its own 200-day SMA with unchanged constituents.

  1. On Monday, 350 out of the 500 stocks close above their own 200-day SMA, giving a reading of 350 ÷ 500 × 100% = 70%.
  2. By Friday, only 250 stocks remain above their own 200-day SMA, dropping the reading to 250 ÷ 500 × 100% = 50%—meaning 100 stocks slipped below their 200-day lines, NOT that prices fell by 20%.
  3. If the cap-weighted index rose from 100 to 102 (+2%) over that same Monday-to-Friday week, you observe a 2% index gain paired with narrowing internal breadth (from 70% down to 50%), often driven by mega-cap weights.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Separate the percentage of stocks above their moving average from the percentage price change of any single stock or index, using breadth to judge how widely participation is shared.

But do not jump to this conclusion: A 70% reading does not mean 70% of traders are profitable, 50% is not a future win probability, and index constituent rebalancings can shift historical comparisons.

One last question: did it click?

If the breadth pane reads 80%, does that mean the stock on your main chart is trading 80% above its 200-day moving average?

Think first, then open the answer

No. A reading of 80% means 80% of the stocks in the specified index universe are currently trading above their own respective 200-day moving averages (whether by 0.1% or 30%). To see how far a single stock sits above its own 200-day MA, read that stock's price and 200-day MA directly on the main chart.

Where to find it: TradingView & AICoin

These paths describe desktop web charts, checked against official help on 19 October 2026. App layouts and account/data permissions can differ.

TradingView

Search terms: Above moving averages / Market breadth / Percent above 200 day moving average

Use market-breadth data or an appropriate data symbol. Official fields include 20, 50 and 200 days; verify coverage.

  1. Open the TradingView chart using the button below. Choose a stock or coin at the upper left and check the exchange. Choose a chart interval, for example 1 hour or 1 day; one daily candle represents one day.
  2. Use Indicators in the upper chart toolbar, then enter one of the search terms below. Check built-in Technicals first. Community scripts with similar names may calculate different things.
  3. Select the matching result to add it, close the search, and look for a new line or lower pane. If there is no exact built-in match, read the availability note rather than substituting a different indicator.
  4. Hover over the added indicator name and open Settings. Inputs changes calculation settings such as length; start with the defaults. Style changes visual appearance. Follow this lesson’s interpretation steps.
  5. Review completed historical candles and explain what the indicator showed at that time. Adding it to a chart does not place a trade. Practise reading it before any simulated execution.

Search hints; stock-universe breadth availability unverified. A single MA line is not this percentage.

  1. Open the AICoin web chart below. Select an exchange and pair, such as BTC/USDT. Check whether you selected spot or a perpetual contract: their data can differ.

  3. Add only an exact matching result. Check whether it appears over price or in a separate pane. Similar names do not guarantee the same formula.
  4. If the implementation exposes parameters/settings, open them and start with the defaults, then follow the interpretation steps on this page. Check the official help if your interface differs.
  5. No result may mean the feature is unavailable in your version. Check existing Custom indicators (自定义指标) only if you can verify their formula. Otherwise learn it on a platform that supports it; do not assume every indicator here is built in.
