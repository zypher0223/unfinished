Futures Basics: Contract Months, Offsetting, Rolling & Delivery

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) explains that most futures contracts are liquidated before the delivery date, that some allow cash settlement instead of delivery, and that customer accounts are adjusted to each trading day’s market value at the close. In other words, futures gains and losses are settled every trading day, not when you close: losses come out of the account that day, and if margin falls short you must top it up. A small margin deposit controls the contract’s full notional value, so leverage is high. CME Group’s course notes that the expiration date is the last day the contract can trade, and that traders often watch volume in both months, rolling once volume builds in the next month by closing the current contract and opening the next at the same time. Crypto perpetual futures (lessons 182 and 108) have no expiry and use funding rates instead of rolling.

DefinitionA futures contract is a standardised agreement to buy or sell an asset (a commodity, a stock index, an interest rate and so on) on a future date under set terms. Each contract has a fixed expiry month. Before expiry a trader can offset (make an equal and opposite trade) or roll (close the expiring contract and open one further out); holding to expiry leads to settlement, by physical delivery or in cash depending on the contract. Most contracts are closed out before delivery.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like a purchase contract signed in advance: it says “delivery in September”; in August, if you do not want the goods, you can hand the contract to someone else (offset) or swap it for a new “December delivery” contract (roll).

A made-up example

Illustration only: a stock-index future is worth USD 50 per point. You buy 1 September contract at 5,000, a notional value of 5,000 × 50 = USD 250,000; with USD 12,500 of margin, leverage is about 250,000 ÷ 12,500 = 20 times. The next day it closes at 4,950, a loss of (5,000 − 4,950) × 50 = USD 2,500, taken from the account that day. Near expiry you sell the September contract at 4,980 and buy December at 5,030: the September leg realises a loss of (5,000 − 4,980) × 50 = USD 1,000, and the new position’s cost is 5,030, with later gains and losses measured from December’s price. The 50 points by which December exceeds September is not a loss; you have simply switched to a higher-priced contract.

Who developed or popularized it?

The offsetting, rolling and settlement material follows CFTC and CME Group investor education

The CFTC’s “Basics of Futures Trading” says most contracts are liquidated before delivery, some settle in cash, and accounts are adjusted to each day’s closing value; CME Group’s course lists three choices before expiry: offset, roll, or go to settlement (physical or cash, depending on the market).

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Check the contract specifications: underlying, value per point (multiplier), expiry months, last trading day, settlement method.
  2. Measure risk by notional value, not margin, and work out the leverage.
  3. Note the last trading day and decide in advance whether to close or roll; do not hold to expiry unless you want settlement.
  4. When rolling, record the near month’s realised result separately from the far month’s new entry price.

The mistake to avoid

Believing you can “wait until it comes back” if you never sell. Futures settle at the market price every day; losses are taken that day, margin shortfalls must be topped up, and failing to do so means a forced close. Some traders also forget the last trading day and drift into settlement.
