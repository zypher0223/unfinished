CVD

My learning & reviewBeginner route

CVD asks whether classified buy-side or sell-side volume has accumulated a persistent lead over time. It plots as a cumulative curve or candle series in a lower pane. Beginners often mistake a scheduled session reset to zero for a sudden market crash.

CVD Cumulative Volume Deltaan indicator that keeps a running total of single-bar Volume Deltas over a chosen anchor period or across the chart
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

If single-bar Volume Delta is today's net cash flow, CVD is the running ledger balance since the start of the period; when the ledger starts a fresh page at the beginning of a new session, resetting the starting number to zero is just bookkeeping, not lost money.

A made-up example

Illustration only: starting from 0 at the session open, CVD_t = CVD_{t-1} + Delta_t. If the first three bars print single-bar Deltas of +100, -40, and +60, then Bar 1 CVD = 0 + 100 = +100; Bar 2 CVD = 100 - 40 = +60; Bar 3 CVD = 60 + 60 = +120. Cumulative delta rose from +100 on Bar 1 to +120 on Bar 3 (+20 higher). If price on Bar 3 actually sits lower than on Bar 1, passive sellers absorbed the net +120 buying without letting price advance.

First: why look at this indicator?

Cumulative Volume Delta (CVD) keeps a running ledger of single-bar Volume Delta values over a chosen anchor period to show whether positive-classified or negative-classified volume has been more persistent over time. If single-bar Volume Delta is today's net cash-flow receipt, CVD is the running balance since the ledger started. It accumulates classified volume differences—not bank balances, net dollar inflows, or buyer headcount minus seller headcount. Always check whether the underlying delta comes from true tick-by-tick trades or lower-timeframe bar estimation.

When it is most useful

Use CVD when comparing multi-bar price highs and lows against cumulative volume-delta highs and lows within the same anchor period. Because the Anchor Period setting (such as Daily, Weekly, or Session) resets the running total back to zero at each boundary, never subtract yesterday's end-of-day CVD from today's opening zero reset; also keep the exchange feed, symbol, and lower-timeframe resolution identical.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

The cumulative CVD curve or CVD candles and right-hand scale in the lower pane
The vertical coordinate shows the running cumulative volume delta since the last reset (positive or negative); rising CVD means positive delta bars are adding to the total, while falling CVD means negative delta bars are subtracting. CVD candle open/high/low/close values track the running volume ledger—not the asset's dollar price.
The anchor reset boundaries alongside main-chart price bars
Locate where each anchor period begins. A drop back to 0 at the start of a new day or week is a scheduled ledger reset—not a market sell-off. Always align the exact same timestamp vertically with the main price chart.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: Within the same anchor period, both price and CVD reach new highs

What it suggests: Price advances and cumulative positive-classified volume are reinforcing each other over this period.

What it does not tell you yet: It is not a promise that price will keep rising afterward.

You see: Price prints a new high within the period while CVD fails to reach a new high

What it suggests: Cumulative classified volume delta has not kept pace with the price high, flagging a divergence clue.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not prove the top is in, as intrabar estimation quirks and passive limit-order absorption can both affect CVD.

You see: CVD drops abruptly from a large positive number back to 0 at the start of a new session

What it suggests: Check first whether the indicator just hit its daily or session anchor reset boundary.

What it does not tell you yet: Never misread a scheduled zero reset as sudden mass selling.

The easiest mistake to make

Panicking when a Session-anchored CVD line drops vertically back to zero at the start of a new day, which is just the scheduled clock reset; assuming a rising CVD line forces price to rise when large passive limit sellers can absorb aggressive market buys while pushing price down.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Hypothetical daily-reset CVD across three consecutive bars with single-bar Volume Deltas of +100, −40, and +60 units.

  1. Starting from 0, the running CVD ledger moves to +100 on Bar 1, then 100 − 40 = +60 on Bar 2, and 60 + 60 = +120 on Bar 3 (Bar 3 added +60, not +120).
  2. On Bar 4, price prints a fresh intraday high on the main chart while single-bar delta is −30, pulling CVD down from +120 to +90—creating a temporary divergence between the new price high and lower CVD.
  3. At the start of the next day's session, the ledger resets to 0—do not read the step from yesterday's +90 to today's 0 as 90 units of selling.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Compare CVD peaks and troughs strictly within the same un-reset anchor segment, and always rule out session reset boundaries first.

But do not jump to this conclusion: A CVD divergence does not guarantee a price reversal, and lower-timeframe estimated CVD is an approximation rather than a complete audit of every market order across all venues.

One last question: did it click?

If yesterday's final CVD closed at +1,000 and today's first bar starts near 0, can you conclude that 1,000 units of net selling hit the market at the open?

Think first, then open the answer

No. When CVD is set to a Daily anchor period, the running total automatically resets to zero at the start of each new trading day. Today's first bar simply starts a fresh daily ledger from zero—never subtract across a reset boundary.

TradingView

Search terms：Cumulative Volume Delta

Official built-in; Anchor period controls resets.

  1. Open the TradingView chart using the button below. Choose a stock or coin at the upper left and check the exchange. Choose a chart interval, for example 1 hour or 1 day; one daily candle represents one day.
  2. Use Indicators in the upper chart toolbar, then enter one of the search terms below. Check built-in Technicals first. Community scripts with similar names may calculate different things.
  3. Select the matching result to add it, close the search, and look for a new line or lower pane. If there is no exact built-in match, read the availability note rather than substituting a different indicator.
  4. Hover over the added indicator name and open Settings. Inputs changes calculation settings such as length; start with the defaults. Style changes visual appearance. Follow this lesson’s interpretation steps.
  5. Review completed historical candles and explain what the indicator showed at that time. Adding it to a chart does not place a trade. Practise reading it before any simulated execution.
