A beginner’s reading guide · Beginner

Volume

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Volume and Volume MA (a histogram counting total shares, coins, or contracts traded during each bar, paired with a moving average baseline) ask how active trading was during a candle and whether activity was above or below normal. They plot in a pane below the price chart. The most common beginner misconception is thinking a green volume bar means 'all buying and no selling'.

A small worked example, then three interpretations

Separate worked example: these numbers are for hand calculation, not the plotted data.

Last three volumes 120,240,180 give a 3-period average of 180 shares. Every trade has both a buyer and a seller.

  1. Higher volume: more participation; inspect price direction.
  2. Lower volume: less activity, not proof that selling is exhausted.
  3. High volume with little price movement: heavy two-sided activity.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

If price on the main chart is the price per kilogram of apples, volume is how many kilograms were sold today, and the Volume MA is the usual daily sales total; however, every single kilogram sold had both one buyer and one seller.

A made-up example

Illustration only: if the last 3 completed bars traded 100k, 200k, and 300k shares, the 3-bar Volume MA is (100k + 200k + 300k) / 3 = 600k / 3 = 200k shares. If Bar 4 finishes with 500k shares, it exceeds the 200k average by 500k - 200k = 300k shares (500k / 200k = 2.5x average volume). Trading was 2.5 times busier than the recent average, with 500k shares bought matched against 500k shares sold.

Who developed or popularized it?

Underlying market data; no single creator

Volume is recorded by markets; a volume average applies averaging to it. There is no single inventor of this underlying market data.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

First: why look at this indicator?

Trading Volume records how many shares, coins, or contracts changed hands inside a single bar, while a Volume Moving Average (Volume MA) shows the typical level over the last N bars. Price tells you where trades cleared; volume tells you how active trading was. Heavy volume means high turnover—not 'more buying than selling,' because every executed trade has both a buyer and a seller.

When it is most useful

Volume and its moving average help you judge whether a price move occurred during routine activity or unusually heavy participation. Always align the exchange feed, timeframe, and volume unit (shares/coins vs. dollar notional), and never compare a 5-minute bar that just opened against a full day's completed volume.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

The Volume bars and right-hand volume axis below the main price chart
Each bar aligns with the candlestick above it—taller bars mean heavier volume, where 'K' denotes thousands and 'M' denotes millions. Check whether the axis counts shares, base coins, contracts, or dollar value, as 1,000 coins and $1,000 are completely different.
The moving-average line overlaid on the volume bars and the bar coloring rule
For example, MA 20 averages the last 20 volume bars. Green and red volume bars are typically colored by whether the price bar closed up or down—not by splitting buyer volume and seller volume into separate bars. An unclosed bar's volume is still accumulating.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: Price rises on volume noticeably above the Volume MA

What it suggests: The advance was accompanied by heavier-than-usual turnover and active participation.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not prove buyers won permanently or that the next bar is bound to rise.

You see: Price falls on heavy volume

What it suggests: Trading was active as prices moved lower, involving both aggressive selling and passive bids absorbing supply.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean everyone is selling, nor does it automatically mark a bottom.

You see: Price moves a long distance on volume below the Volume MA

What it suggests: The price move occurred on relatively light turnover, making follow-through and liquidity worth monitoring.

What it does not tell you yet: Light volume does not guarantee an immediate reversal or prove market manipulation.

The easiest mistake to make

Thinking a tall green volume bar means buyers outnumbered sellers when every traded share has both a buyer and a seller and bar color only reflects Close versus Open; comparing an unfinished live candle's partial volume against the 20-bar average; treating one exchange's volume as the entire global market.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Hypothetical three completed bars with volumes of 100, 200, and 300 shares, giving a 3-period Volume MA of 200 shares. On the next completed bar, volume is 500 shares as the close rises from 10 to 10.5 yuan.

  1. 500 shares is 2.5 times the prior 200-share average, showing noticeably higher activity.
  2. The price bar above closed higher, meaning heavy volume accompanied a price advance—the volume bar itself did not create the upward direction.
  3. Once this 500-share bar enters the 3-period average, the new Volume MA becomes (200 + 300 + 500) ÷ 3 ≈ 333 shares; comparing 500 against the prior average (200) versus the updated inclusive average (333) yields different multiples.

Putting the observations together, you could say

State which average benchmark you are comparing against and confirm the bar is closed before describing volume-price alignment, avoiding the trap of reading a tall green bar as pure net buying.

But do not jump to this conclusion: 500 shares traded does not tell you how many participants traded, whose position grew, or where the stock goes tomorrow.

One last question: did it click?

Does a green volume bar of 1 million shares mean 1 million shares were bought and zero shares were sold?

Think first, then open the answer

No. Every 1 million shares traded requires 1 million shares bought and 1 million shares sold; green coloring simply reflects that the bar's price closed higher under the chart's coloring rule. It cannot be read as net cash inflow.

Once you can say “what I see → what it means → what is still uncertain”, practise with the platform walkthrough below.

TradingView

Search terms: Volume

Official built-in; check the Volume MA option in settings. Data sources vary by symbol. Practice setup: Add Volume and identify whether units are shares, coins, contracts or value.

  1. Open the TradingView chart using the button below. Choose a stock or coin at the upper left and check the exchange. Choose a chart interval, for example 1 hour or 1 day; one daily candle represents one day.
  2. Use Indicators in the upper chart toolbar, then enter one of the search terms below. Check built-in Technicals first. Community scripts with similar names may calculate different things.
  3. Select the matching result to add it, close the search, and look for a new line or lower pane. If there is no exact built-in match, read the availability note rather than substituting a different indicator.
  4. Hover over the added indicator name and open Settings. Inputs changes calculation settings such as length; start with the defaults. Style changes visual appearance. Follow this lesson’s interpretation steps.
  5. Review completed historical candles and explain what the indicator showed at that time. Adding it to a chart does not place a trade. Practise reading it before any simulated execution.
