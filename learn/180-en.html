Wallets & Exchange Custody

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Once you own a crypto asset, the first question is who holds the keys. Keeping it on an exchange is convenient for trading but hands the keys to the platform; your own wallet is more independent, but a lost or stolen recovery phrase usually cannot be recovered.

DefinitionCustodial: assets sit in an exchange account, the exchange holds the private keys, and you sign in with your credentials. Self-custody wallet: you hold the private keys yourself, usually backed up as a 12- or 24-word recovery phrase, and whoever has the phrase can move the assets. The risks differ: the first carries the risk of the platform failing, freezing or being hacked; the second the risk of losing or leaking your phrase.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Custody is like keeping cash in someone else’s safe-deposit box with the shop holding the key; self-custody is a safe at home, and if you lose the key no one can open it for you.

A made-up example

Illustration only: you move coins worth 1,000 from an exchange to your own wallet; the exchange charges a 1 withdrawal fee and 999 arrives after on-chain confirmation. A wrong network or address could make those 999 unrecoverable, so send 10 first as a test and move the rest after it arrives.

Who developed or popularized it?

Public-key cryptography and bitcoin’s key system; recovery phrases follow BIP-39

Bitcoin uses private-key signatures to prove control of assets; BIP-39, proposed in 2013, defined word lists for representing a seed and was adopted by most wallets. The collapses of exchanges such as Mt. Gox in 2014 and FTX in 2022 cost many users their custodied assets.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Decide which assets stay on the exchange (to trade) and which move to your own wallet (to hold).
  2. With a self-custody wallet, write the recovery phrase on paper and keep it offline: no photos, no cloud, never sent to anyone.
  3. Check the address and network before withdrawing, and test with a small amount the first time.
  4. Turn on two-factor authentication on exchange accounts and review signed-in devices and withdrawal whitelists regularly.
  5. Wallet assets are not lost only through leaked seed phrases: token approvals and off-chain signatures can also hand spending rights to others, so read every prompt before signing and revoke unused approvals regularly (see “Smart Contracts & Token Approvals”).

The mistake to avoid

Saving the recovery phrase as a screenshot in your photos or cloud drive, or trusting “support staff” who ask for your phrase or private key; legitimate platforms never ask.
