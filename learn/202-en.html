Bridges & Oracles

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Several of the largest hacks in crypto history hit bridges: they hold huge sums and depend on a few signers or complex code. Oracles are a smart contract’s eyes; blindfold them and the contract executes at the wrong price. Understanding both explains many “protocol hacked” headlines.

DefinitionA cross-chain bridge “moves” assets between chains, usually by locking them on the source chain and minting an equal amount of wrapped tokens on the destination; if the bridge’s lock contract or signers are compromised, the wrapped tokens can lose their backing. An oracle feeds off-chain data, such as prices, into smart contracts; if that price is manipulated or delayed, lending and derivatives protocols relying on it can liquidate wrongly or be exploited.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

A bridge is like depositing gold in a vault in city A and receiving a claim ticket in city B; if the vault is robbed, the ticket is worthless. An oracle is like a mall’s price board: if someone quietly changes the board, the till charges the wrong price.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a bridge locks 100,000 ETH on the source chain and has issued 100,000 wrapped ETH on the destination. A hacker obtains enough signing keys and drains the locked ETH, leaving the 100,000 wrapped ETH unbacked; their market price can quickly slide toward zero.

Who developed or popularized it?

Landmark events: the Wormhole (February 2022) and Ronin (March 2022) bridge hacks

In February 2022 a signature-verification flaw let an attacker mint about 120,000 wrapped ETH on Wormhole, a loss of about USD 320 million. In March 2022 five of the nine validator keys of Axie Infinity’s Ronin bridge were compromised and about 173,600 ETH plus 25.5 million USDC (about USD 625 million) were stolen; the US Treasury later attributed it to North Korea’s Lazarus group. The Chainlink oracle network’s white paper appeared in 2017.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Before using a bridge, check how many signers control it, whether it is audited and whether it has had incidents.
  2. Prefer official or long-proven bridges, use them only when needed and limit the amount.
  3. When holding wrapped assets, remember their value depends on the bridge behind them.
  4. When using lending or derivatives protocols, learn which oracle they use and how often prices update.

The mistake to avoid

Treating a wrapped asset as identical to the native one. Wrapped ETH is a claim backed by a bridge; when the bridge fails, the two prices diverge sharply.
