Trend Lines and Channels

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Trendlines and Channels (drawing tools that connect a sequence of rising swing lows or falling swing highs with a diagonal line and frame the opposite side with a parallel line) ask what slope a trend has been following and whether price remains inside that sloping corridor. They are drawn manually on the main price chart. Beginners often force a line right through the middle of prior candle bodies just to touch the latest bar.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

It outlines the left and right edges of a mountain trail to show whether the hiker is still walking up the same grade or stepping off the path; however, changing the map's vertical scale between linear and logarithmic shifts where a straight ruler lands.

A made-up example

Illustration only: on a linear daily chart, suppose Swing Low 1 is at 100 on Day 1, Swing Low 2 is at 105 on Day 6 (5 bars later), and the intervening Swing High on Day 4 is 112. Connecting Day 1 (100) and Day 6 (105) gives a rise of 105 - 100 = 5 over 5 bars, or +1 per day; extending 5 more bars to Day 11 places the lower trendline at 105 + 5(1) = 110. On Day 4 the lower line was at 100 + 3(1) = 103 beneath the 112 high, a vertical channel width of 112 - 103 = 9, placing the Day 11 upper channel line at 110 + 9 = 119. Breaking 110 shows the +1/day pace slowed, not a assured crash.

First: why look at this tool?

Trendlines and Channels connect a sequence of rising swing lows (or falling swing highs) with a straight diagonal line and a parallel opposite boundary, helping you answer what slope price has been following and whether that pace is holding, steepening, or breaking down. They summarize the geometry of past swings rather than locking price onto fixed rails.

When it is most useful

Useful when at least two clear directional swings have formed on a fixed timeframe so you can project the slope forward to watch a third test. They become unreliable in flat sideways chop or when you switch between linear and logarithmic chart scales without checking the anchor points.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

Primary diagonal Trend Line connecting swing lows or highs on the main chart
An rising trendline is drawn below price across higher lows; a falling trendline is drawn above price across lower highs.
Opposite Parallel Channel boundary and optional midline
Dragged from the main trendline to an opposing swing extreme to frame the width of the sloping corridor in price units.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: Price pulls back to a rising lower channel line for a third touch and turns back up

What it suggests: The prior pace of rising lows is still holding along the established slope.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not ensure the fourth or fifth touch will also hold.

You see: A candle closes decisively below a rising trendline and fails to reclaim it on a retest

What it suggests: The previous upward pace has broken, warning that the trend may be slowing into a range or reversing.

What it does not tell you yet: Breaking a steep trendline does not automatically start a crash; price often transitions into a shallower uptrend.

You see: A line is forced through the middle of multiple candle bodies just to touch the latest bar

What it suggests: The original trendline already broke earlier, and cutting through bodies distorts the boundary.

What it does not tell you yet: Redrawing lines to excuse every break does not mean the original trend remained intact.

The easiest mistake to make

Drawing an unsustainably steep 80-degree trendline and calling a major bear market the moment price pauses sideways and crosses it; switching between Linear and Log scales without rechecking your anchors; treating an unclosed intraday wick poke as a confirmed channel breakdown.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Illustration only: on a linear daily chart, Swing Low 1 is at 100 on Day 1, Swing Low 2 is at 105 on Day 6 (5 bars later), and an intervening Swing High sits at 112 on Day 4.

  1. Connecting Day 1 (100) and Day 6 (105) gives a rise of 105 - 100 = 5 over 5 bars, a slope of +1 per day.
  2. Extending +1 per day forward 5 more bars to Day 11 places the lower trendline at 105 + (5 × 1) = 110.
  3. On Day 4 the lower line was at 100 + (3 × 1) = 103 while the high was 112, giving a vertical channel width of 112 - 103 = 9; thus on Day 11 the upper parallel line sits at 110 + 9 = 119.

Putting the observations together, you could say

By Day 11 the projected channel spans from 110 (lower line) to 119 (upper line), giving clear reference levels to observe.

But do not jump to this conclusion: It does not ensure 110 will hold on Day 11, and switching to a logarithmic scale shifts the projected line because equal percentages replace equal dollar steps.

One last question: did it click?

You draw a long-term rising trendline connecting lows at 10 and 20 on a Linear scale, then toggle the chart to Logarithmic (Log) scale and see candles suddenly sitting below your line even though price has not moved. Did the market just break down?

Think first, then open the answer

No. Toggling between Linear (equal dollar spacing) and Log (equal percentage spacing) alters the geometry of diagonal lines across large price spans; price itself did not change, so you must keep scale settings consistent.
