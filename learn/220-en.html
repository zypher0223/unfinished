A Map of the Web3 Industry

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Beginners often equate Web3 with token trading, yet the industry operates across four distinct layers: first, base-layer infrastructure including Layer 1 blockchains (see the "Bitcoin vs Ethereum" lesson on this site), Layer 2 scaling networks (see the "Layer 2 & Rollups" lesson on this site), and node providers; second, middleware and security, covering bridges, oracles (see the "Bridges & Oracles" lesson on this site), data indexers, wallets, and smart contract auditing firms; third, the application layer, spanning exchanges, lending protocols, stablecoin payment rails, and consumer dApps; and fourth, research, media, legal compliance, and governance services. Across these layers, teams typically collaborate via remote-first, asynchronous workflows requiring both technical engineers and product, design, analytics, operations, and legal contributors.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Think of the Web3 industry as a newly built digital port city: Layer 1 and Layer 2 engineers build the roads and power grid, oracle and audit teams run the waterworks and safety inspections, DeFi and consumer apps operate the shops and banks on main street, and researchers, community leads, and legal counsel serve as the city's newspapers, guides, and law offices.

A made-up example

A single on-chain token swap involves multiple roles working together: frontend developers build the web interface, wallet engineers handle local transaction signing, smart contract developers write the AMM pool logic, security auditors test for exploits, oracle nodes feed reference prices, and data analysts and community managers track metrics and support users (for illustrative purposes only).

Who developed or popularized it?

Gavin Wood's Coining of Web 3.0 and the Multi-Layer Stack Evolution

In 2014, Ethereum co-founder Gavin Wood coined the term 'Web 3.0' to describe a decentralized, cryptographically verifiable internet stack. Following the rise of smart contracts in 2017, DeFi in 2020, and Layer 2 rollups after 2021, the industry evolved from small core-client teams into a multi-layered ecosystem spanning infrastructure, security auditing, data analytics, and global operations.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Map the four ecosystem layers: identify whether a project sits in base infrastructure, middleware/security, user applications, or research and compliance services.
  2. Analyze business sustainability: examine whether the team earns sustainable revenue from protocol fees, B2B infrastructure subscriptions, or audit services.
  3. Match your existing skillset: align technical skills with smart contract, full-stack, or data engineering, and non-technical skills with product, growth, research, or BD.
  4. Build verifiable public proof-of-work: compile open-source GitHub commits, on-chain analytics dashboards, or structured research reports.
  5. Screen for legal and operational legitimacy: verify code repository activity, real product usage, and local regulatory compliance before joining any team.

The mistake to avoid

Misconception 1: Believing only cryptography PhDs or Solidity coders can work in Web3—protocols rely equally on frontend/backend engineers, designers, researchers, and operations specialists. Misconception 2: Equating remote work with low accountability—cross-timezone teams demand strong written communication and self-discipline. Misconception 3: Joining unverified Ponzi or fraudulent token schemes without due diligence, risking severe legal liability (see the "Beginner Safety: Scams & Money Management" lesson on this site).
