Open Interest

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Open Interest asks whether total active contract positioning in the market is expanding or contracting. It plots in a lower pane on derivative charts. Beginners often confuse Open Interest with daily trading Volume or mistakenly think rising OI means 'more longs than shorts'.

Interest OIa derivatives statistic counting the total number of futures or perpetual contracts that remain open and unsettled at the end of each bar
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

If trading Volume counts everyone who walked in or out of a theater's doors today, Open Interest counts how many occupied seats currently hold an active ticket inside the hall; and every contract ticket requires one long partner and one short partner sitting together.

A made-up example

Illustration only: when a new buyer and a new seller open 1 contract together, OI rises by +1; when a new buyer takes over from an existing long closing out, OI changes by 0; when an existing long and existing short close against each other, OI drops by -1. Starting from OI = 0: Trade 1 matches A (new long 10) with B (new short 10) → Volume = 10, OI = 0 + 10 = 10 (not 20). Trade 2 matches C (new long 4) with A (closing 4 longs) → cumulative Volume = 10 + 4 = 14, OI = 10 - 4 + 4 = 10. Trade 3 matches A (closing 6 longs) with B (closing 6 shorts) → Volume = 14 + 6 = 20, OI = 10 - 6 = 4. OI measures active contracts, and longs always equal shorts.

First: why look at this tool?

Open Interest (OI) counts the total number of derivative contracts (futures or perpetuals) that remain open and unsettled at a given moment, helping you answer whether total market positioning is expanding (new positions opening) or contracting (existing positions closing). It is neither cumulative trading volume nor an excess of longs over shorts.

When it is most useful

Useful alongside price moves on futures or perpetual charts to distinguish moves accompanied by new contract creation from moves driven by position liquidations or short covering. Do not confuse USD-denominated OI rising purely from higher asset prices with an actual increase in contract count.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

Open Interest line or candles in the lower pane and its right-hand unit axis
Plotted below a derivatives chart; always check whether the axis unit is native coins/contracts or USD notional value.
Main contract price bars and the Volume pane
Compare price direction and bar volume with changes in OI on the same bar to distinguish position building from position unwinding.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: Price moves strongly while coin-denominated Open Interest rises steadily

What it suggests: More new contracts are being opened than closed as price moves, expanding total active positioning.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean the new contracts are 'all longs', because every new contract has both one long and one short.

You see: Price spikes sharply higher or lower while Open Interest drops sharply

What it suggests: Existing holders are closing out positions (such as short covering or long liquidations), reducing total open contracts.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not ensure that price will reverse immediately once the unwind slows.

You see: Coin-denominated OI stays flat while USD-denominated OI rises 10% alongside a 10% price rally

What it suggests: No net new coin contracts were added; the higher dollar figure simply reflects the higher price per coin.

What it does not tell you yet: Do not misread price appreciation in a USD-valued chart as fresh contract inflows.

The easiest mistake to make

Claiming that rising Open Interest proves 'more buyers than sellers entered', forgetting that every single open contract matches one long with one short 1-to-1; reading USD-denominated OI during a rally and mistaking price appreciation for fresh contract creation; confusing cumulative daily volume with open contract count.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Illustration only: start a new contract market at OI = 0 contracts and track Volume versus Open Interest across three trades.

  1. Trade 1: Trader A opens a new 10-contract long and Trader B opens a new 10-contract short; Volume is 10, and Open Interest rises from 0 to 0 + 10 = 10 contracts (not 20).
  2. Trade 2: Trader C opens a new 4-contract long by buying from Trader A, who closes 4 existing long contracts; Volume rises to 10 + 4 = 14, while OI stays 10 - 4 + 4 = 10.
  3. Trade 3: Trader A closes the remaining 6 longs against Trader B closing 6 shorts; Volume rises to 14 + 6 = 20, while OI falls to 10 - 6 = 4 contracts.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Total traded volume reached 20 contracts, but active Open Interest ended at 4 contracts; OI rises only when new openings exceed closings.

But do not jump to this conclusion: Seeing OI rise to 10 in Trade 1 does not prove longs outnumber shorts, as all 10 contracts have 10 long units matched with 10 short units.

One last question: did it click?

Today a perpetual contract rallies 5% and coin-denominated Open Interest grows from 100,000 coins to 120,000 coins. Does that prove buyers opened 20,000 more contracts than sellers?

Think first, then open the answer

No. Every open contract matches one long unit with one short unit; the 20,000-coin increase means 20,000 coins of new longs AND 20,000 coins of new shorts were added to total open contracts.

  1. Choose a supported futures or perpetual contract, then search Indicators for Open Interest. A spot instrument itself has no derivatives open interest.
  2. Place the cursor on the same bar to read price and OI together. Record whether OI is contracts, coins or notional value before comparing adjacent bars.
