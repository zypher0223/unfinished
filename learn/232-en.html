Phishing Signatures, Address Poisoning & Fake Airdrops

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Many people think “if I never send a transfer, I can’t lose money.” On-chain, an approval or a signature can hand over spending rights by itself. Permit, defined in ERC-2612 (a way to grant an allowance by signing a message instead of sending a transaction), has Final status in the official Ethereum repository. It is a normal feature, yet scammers often disguise it as a “login” or “verification”. Another scam needs no signature: the scammer generates an address whose first and last characters match one you use, plants a zero-value or fake-token transfer in your history, and waits for you to copy the wrong address next time.

DefinitionOn-chain phishing does not ask for your money directly. It tricks you into signing an approval or signature in your wallet, or into sending funds to an address that only looks right. Three common forms: phishing signatures (getting you to sign approve or an off-chain Permit that hands over spending rights), address poisoning (planting a transfer from a lookalike address in your history so you copy the wrong one later), and fake airdrops (sending an unknown token to your wallet to lure you to a fake site to “claim” it).
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

A phishing signature is like being handed a “sign-in sheet” that is really a power of attorney; address poisoning is like someone adding a contact to your phone with your friend’s name and a number that differs only in the middle digits; a fake airdrop is a “you’ve won” letter in your mailbox pointing to a website.

A made-up example

Illustration only: Lin often sends USDC to 0xA1b2…9F3e. One day his history shows a 0 USDC transfer from an address that also looks like 0xA1b2…9F3e, but the middle characters differ. The next day he copies the address from his history and sends 2,000 USDC to the fake one. Checking the full address character by character, or using a saved address book, would have prevented it. Another case: 5,000 unknown tokens appear, named “redeem for $500 at some site”; going there to claim 5,000 × 0.1 = $500 and connecting the wallet, the signature is in fact an approval, and the loss is the assets already in the wallet.

Who developed or popularized it?

No single inventor: these methods arose from how ERC-20 approvals, ERC-2612 permits and public transfer histories work

ERC-20 (proposed 2015) defines approve; ERC-2612 (proposed 2020 by Martin Lundfall) lets an approval be made with an off-chain signature. Both are Final in the official Ethereum repository. Phishing and address poisoning are scams that abuse these normal features, not techniques anyone proposed.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Before signing, read the wallet prompt: transfer, approval (approve), Permit signature, or just a login message. If you cannot tell, do not sign yet.
  2. Check the full address before sending, not just the first and last characters; save regular addresses in an address book instead of copying from history.
  3. Do not open unknown tokens or “claim your airdrop” links; treat such tokens in a block explorer as possible bait.
  4. Review and revoke unused approvals regularly, and keep large holdings in a wallet you do not use for daily interactions.

The mistake to avoid

Believing “I only signed, I didn’t pay” is safe, or checking only the first and last few characters before sending. The first hands spending rights to the other side; the second is exactly what address poisoning is designed to exploit.
