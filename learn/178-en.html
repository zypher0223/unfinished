Indexes & ETFs

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

When the news says “the market rose 1%,” it usually means an index. You cannot buy the index itself, but an ETF that tracks it buys the basket in one trade. Learn which stocks an index holds and how it weights them to know what it really represents.

DefinitionA stock index turns the prices of a basket of stocks into one number representing a market or sector, such as the S&P 500, the Nasdaq-100 or the CSI 300. Most indexes are weighted by market cap (large companies count more), while the Dow Jones Industrial Average and the Nikkei 225 are price-weighted. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) is a fund that trades on an exchange like a stock; many ETFs aim to track an index.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

An index is like a class’s average score; an ETF is like buying the whole class’s homework bundled as one.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a two-stock cap-weighted index has A worth 90 billion and B worth 10 billion, so weights are 90% and 10%. If A rises 1% and B falls 5%, the index moves about 0.9 × 1% + 0.1 × (−5%) = 0.4%; B’s big drop only trims the gain slightly.

Who developed or popularized it?

Charles Dow published the Dow Jones Industrial Average in 1896; ETFs spread in the US after SPY launched in 1993

The Dow Jones Industrial Average was first published in 1896; the S&P 500 has covered 500 stocks since 1957; the CSI 300 launched in 2005. The SPDR S&P 500 ETF (SPY), launched in 1993, is often cited as the first US ETF.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Check which stocks the index holds, how many, and how they are weighted.
  2. See what share the largest holdings make up, to judge whether a few companies dominate.
  3. When choosing an ETF, check the index it tracks, its expense ratio and how actively it trades.
  4. When comparing an ETF with its index, remember the units differ; compare percentage changes, not points.

The mistake to avoid

Expecting your own stock to rise because the index did, or comparing index points with an ETF’s price directly; the units differ.
