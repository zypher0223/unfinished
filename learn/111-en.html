Support and Resistance

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Support and Resistance (horizontal price zones marking areas where past declines repeatedly paused or bounced as support, and where past rallies repeatedly stalled or pulled back as resistance) ask where noticeable buying or selling reactions occurred previously on the chart. They are drawn manually on the main price chart using horizontal lines or rectangles. Beginners often mistake support and resistance for exact single-penny walls that can never break.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

They act like doorways where a crowd often pauses before moving on: people frequently stop there, yet a strong push can carry the crowd right through the doorway, after which that same doorway stands behind them as a barrier if they try to walk back.

A made-up example

Illustration only: over two months on a daily chart, three swing lows print wick lows at 99.2 (close 100.5), 99.8 (close 100.8), and 99.5 (close 100.4). The wick lows span 99.8 - 99.2 = 0.6 points, and framing from the lowest wick (99.2) to the highest turn close (100.8) defines a support zone 100.8 - 99.2 = 1.6 points wide rather than a single line at 100.00. If a fourth drop closes at 97.5 (1.7 points below 99.2), the support zone has broken and can act as overhead resistance on a retest.

First: why look at this tool?

Support and Resistance mark horizontal price zones where past declines repeatedly paused or bounced (support) and past advances repeatedly stalled or pulled back (resistance), helping you answer where buyers or sellers previously stepped in noticeably. They are price zones with width rather than razor-thin walls.

When it is most useful

Best drawn on higher timeframes (such as daily or 4-hour charts) across clear, repeated swing turns before price returns to test them. Avoid drawing a line at every minor 5-minute candle high and low, which clutters the chart so every price sits on a line.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

Horizontal zone (rectangle or twin lines) connecting repeated swing highs or lows on the main chart
Span the zone between the outer wicks and the candle bodies of prior turns; levels are read directly in price units on the right axis.
Current candle close relative to the marked zone
Distinguish an intraday wick poking into or through the zone from a completed candle body closing cleanly beyond the zone.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: Price pulls back into a prior 99–101 support zone, slows down, and prints lower wicks

What it suggests: Price is re-entering an area where buyers previously absorbed selling pressure.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not ensure a bounce; repeated tests can weaken a zone until it breaks.

You see: A strong candle closes above a 110–111 resistance zone and later holds 111 from above on a pullback

What it suggests: The former resistance zone has been cleared and is acting as potential new support (polarity flip).

What it does not tell you yet: It does not make 111 permanent; a close back below 110 warns of a failed breakout.

You see: Price chops midway between distant support and resistance

What it suggests: Price is in the middle of the range with no nearby historical reference boundary.

What it does not tell you yet: Do not invent an arbitrary horizontal line mid-range just to force a trade.

The easiest mistake to make

Treating support as a razor-thin number like 100.00 instead of a zone and getting whipsawed by a normal wick to 99.50; assuming that a level tested five times is invincible when repeated hits can exhaust resting orders; constantly dragging lines after the fact to fit every new candle.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Illustration only: over two months on a daily chart, three swing lows printed wick lows at 99.2 (close 100.5), 99.8 (close 100.8), and 99.5 (close 100.4).

  1. The three wick lows span 99.2 to 99.8 (a 99.8 - 99.2 = 0.6 range), while the lowest closes sit between 100.4 and 100.8.
  2. Drawing a zone from the lowest wick (99.2) to the highest turn close (100.8) creates a support band 100.8 - 99.2 = 1.6 wide rather than a single line at 100.00.
  3. On a fourth visit, price dips intraday to 99.6 and closes at 101.2, staying inside the 99.2–100.8 band and closing 0.4 above its top edge.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Treating 99.2–100.8 as a 1.6-point zone accounts for the 99.6 test without mislabeling a 0.4 dip below 100.00 as a breakdown.

But do not jump to this conclusion: Three prior holds do not prove a fifth test cannot close at 97.0 and break the zone.

One last question: did it click?

A resistance zone sits at 119.5–120.5. Today price spikes intraday to 121.0, then drops back to close at 118.8 with a long upper wick. Is this a confirmed breakout above the resistance zone?

Think first, then open the answer

No. Although price briefly traded at 121.0 intraday, it closed at 118.8 below the entire 119.5–120.5 zone, making it a rejected test (false breakout) rather than a confirmed close above resistance.
