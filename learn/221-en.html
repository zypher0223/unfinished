Everyday Tools & Industry Jargon

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Working or researching in Web3 relies on five foundational tool categories: first, block explorers (such as Etherscan or Solscan) to inspect transaction hashes, verified contract code, and wallet balances; second, data dashboards (such as DefiLlama or Dune) to track protocol Total Value Locked (TVL), fee revenue, and on-chain activity; third, self-custody wallets (such as MetaMask, Rabby, or Phantom; see the "Wallets & Exchange Custody" lesson on this site) to manage keys and sign transactions; fourth, multisig wallets (such as Safe) requiring M-of-N approvals to move team treasury funds; and fifth, testnet faucets (such as Sepolia Faucets) providing free test tokens for safe experimentation. Alongside these tools, newcomers encounter dense community jargon and must follow strict security rules: never connect a main vault wallet to unfamiliar websites, and never reveal your seed phrase to anyone (see the "Beginner Safety: Scams & Money Management" lesson on this site).

DefinitionThe five essential tool categories for daily Web3 research and operations (block explorers, data dashboards, self-custody wallets, multisigs, and testnet faucets) alongside core industry jargon.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Think of these five tools as a ship's navigation kit: a block explorer is sonar showing every underwater rock, a data dashboard is weather radar, a personal wallet is the captain's key, a multisig is a vault requiring three officers to turn their keys simultaneously, and a testnet faucet is free simulator fuel for practicing on dry land.

A made-up example

16 common Web3 jargon terms explained: (1) Gas: network computation fee; (2) Slippage: gap between expected and executed trade price; (3) TVL (Total Value Locked): assets deposited in a protocol; (4) DYOR: Do Your Own Research; (5) FOMO: Fear Of Missing Out; (6) FUD: Fear, Uncertainty, and Doubt; (7) Rug Pull: developers draining liquidity or abandoning a project with user funds; (8) Rekt: suffering heavy financial loss; (9) Whale: an address holding large capital; (10) Alpha: valuable early research insight; (11) HODL: holding long-term; (12) WAGMI / NGMI: 'We're All Gonna Make It' / 'Not Gonna Make It'; (13) Diamond / Paper Hands: holders who stay firm vs. panic-sell; (14) Mint: creating new tokens/NFTs via a contract; (15) Snapshot: recording account balances at a specific block height; (16) Sybil: operating many wallets to farm rewards (see the "Airdrops & Sybil Attacks" lesson on this site) (for illustrative purposes only).

Who developed or popularized it?

From the 2013 BitcoinTalk 'HODL' Typo to Mature On-Chain Tooling

Much of Web3 jargon originated in early forum culture—most famously in December 2013 when a BitcoinTalk user misspelled 'HOLD' as 'HODL' in a post title. Meanwhile, the launch of Etherscan (2015), MetaMask (2016), Gnosis Safe multisigs (2018), and analytics platforms like Dune and DefiLlama (2019–2021) established transparent on-chain tooling as the standard workflow.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Verify transactions on a block explorer: paste a TxHash or contract address to confirm execution status, gas paid, and whether source code is verified.
  2. Cross-check metrics on data dashboards: compare TVL, active users, and fee revenue to filter out marketing hype.
  3. Separate main vaults from burner wallets: use a segregated low-balance wallet for unfamiliar dApps and never connect your primary wallet to unknown sites.
  4. Practice on testnets via faucets: claim free testnet tokens to rehearse contract interactions before risking mainnet funds.
  5. Audit and revoke token approvals regularly: inspect and revoke stale allowances (see the "Smart Contracts & Token Approvals" lesson on this site).

The mistake to avoid

Misconception 1: Clicking sponsored search engine ads for wallets or explorers, which frequently lead to phishing clones that steal seed phrases. Misconception 2: Connecting a primary savings wallet to an unfamiliar mint or airdrop page. Misconception 3: Acting on community FOMO slogans without doing independent on-chain verification (DYOR).
