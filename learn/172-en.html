Expectancy & Win Rate

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Many people only count how often they win, but the result depends on how much you make when you win and how much you lose when you lose. A 30% win rate can be profitable and a 90% win rate can lose money. Rushing to bank every small gain while letting losers run pushes expectancy negative.

DefinitionExpectancy (average result per trade) = win rate × average win − (1 − win rate) × average loss, with the average loss taken as a positive number. Positive expectancy means that, if these statistics hold, the average trade makes money over time; it is not the same as a high win rate. The payoff ratio = average win ÷ average loss, and the break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + payoff ratio).
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like running a shop: the number of sales each day matters less than income minus costs at the end of the month.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a 30% win rate with an average win of 3 and an average loss of 1 gives expectancy = 0.3 × 3 − 0.7 × 1 = 0.2 per trade. By contrast, an 80% win rate with an average win of 1 and an average loss of 5 gives 0.8 × 1 − 0.2 × 5 = −0.2: the high win rate still loses.

Who developed or popularized it?

Expected value from probability theory; no single popularizer of the trading formula verified

Expected value grew out of the “problem of points” that Pascal and Fermat discussed in 1654 and that Huygens set out in his 1657 treatise. The trading form “win rate × average win − loss rate × average loss” appears widely in trading texts; its first popularizer is unverified. cis writes that only about 30% of his trades win, yet controlling losses keeps total returns positive.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Record at least several dozen trades and compute the win rate, average win and average loss, including fees and slippage.
  2. Plug them into the expectancy formula and see whether the average trade is positive.
  3. Compute the break-even win rate from the payoff ratio and compare it with your actual win rate.
  4. When improving, be clear whether you are raising the win rate or enlarging wins and shrinking losses; when one costs the other, judge by overall expectancy.

The mistake to avoid

Chasing a high win rate while ignoring loss size, or drawing conclusions from a dozen trades, a sample far too small to rule out luck.

From the book: cis Stock Trading

Source: cis, cis Stock Trading (Chinese edition, translated by Zhu Jia, China Youth Press, 2020, ISBN 978-7-5153-6031-7; Japanese original published by KADOKAWA, 2018)

What the author says

The author says only about 30% of his trades win, yet total returns stay positive because he cuts losers quickly and lets a few trades gain ten or twenty times the loss. He stresses total return over win rate, warns that taking profits early forfeits the big wins, and frames stocks, poker and mahjong alike as act-or-fold decisions weighed by expected value. This lesson works that idea through with the expectancy formula.

This is our English summary of the author’s view; the Chinese page of this lesson carries the original excerpt. The amounts and events are the author’s own experience, mostly in Japanese markets, and nothing here is trading advice.
