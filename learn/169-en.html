Trend vs Range

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

First decide whether the market is trending or ranging, then choose tools: in trends, moving averages and breakout tools help; in ranges they whipsaw, while oscillators are more useful near the boundaries. Using them the other way round gets you hit from both sides.

DefinitionA trend is a stretch of bars in which swing highs and lows advance in one direction (higher highs and higher lows in an uptrend), most closes sit on one side, and moving averages slope clearly. A range is when highs and lows do not advance persistently, price swings within roughly fixed bounds, and averages flatten while price keeps crossing them. Indicators such as ADX can help: an ADX below about 20 is often read as a weak trend.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like telling a steady tailwind from gusts blowing every which way: you set the sails differently.

A made-up example

Illustration only: over the first 20 bars, highs of 101.9, 101.8 and 102.0 and lows of 100.2, 100.3 and 100.1 do not advance, and the average is flat: a range. Later, highs of 102.6, 103.4 and 104.1 with rising lows and an upward-sloping average mark a trend.

Who developed or popularized it?

No single inventor verified; based on Dow Theory’s trend definition and Wilder’s ADX

Defining a trend by advancing highs and lows comes from Dow Theory; J. Welles Wilder introduced ADX to measure trend strength in 1978, and E. W. Dreiss developed the Choppiness Index.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Mark recent swing highs and lows and see whether they advance in one direction.
  2. Check whether moving averages slope clearly or price keeps crossing them.
  3. If useful, add ADX: low readings mean a weak trend; rising readings mean it is strengthening.
  4. Choose tools accordingly: trend tools in trends, range thinking in ranges, and switch when the diagnosis changes.

The mistake to avoid

Chasing moving-average crossovers in a range, or fading overbought and oversold readings in a strong trend, are the most common cases of using the right tool in the wrong place.

From the book: cis Stock Trading

Source: cis, cis Stock Trading (Chinese edition, translated by Zhu Jia, China Youth Press, 2020, ISBN 978-7-5153-6031-7; Japanese original published by KADOKAWA, 2018)

What the author says

The author, mainly a day trader, argues that rising stocks tend to keep rising and falling ones keep falling, and warns against the gambler’s-fallacy belief that a streak must soon reverse. He follows the move and sells once the trend’s mood changes, often at the second sharp dip. This lesson still starts by classifying trend versus range from swing structure; chasing strength in a range gets you whipsawed.

This is our English summary of the author’s view; the Chinese page of this lesson carries the original excerpt. The amounts and events are the author’s own experience, mostly in Japanese markets, and nothing here is trading advice.
