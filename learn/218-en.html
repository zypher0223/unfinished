AI × Web3

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The surge in generative AI has created massive demand for compute, verifiable data provenance, and automated settlement, yet traditional AI remains concentrated among a few tech giants with opaque black-box inference. Conversely, Web3 suffers from complex user interfaces, tedious smart contract auditing, and fragmented data analysis. The AI × Web3 intersection explores two directions: using blockchain incentives and cryptography to aggregate distributed GPU compute (see the "DePIN: Token-Incentivised Infrastructure" lesson on this site), track data provenance, and verify model execution via Zero-Knowledge Machine Learning (zkML); and equipping AI Agents with on-chain wallets to execute automated analysis or micropayments. Currently, the field faces three major bottlenecks: first, geographically dispersed consumer GPUs suffer from bandwidth latency, making large-scale synchronous foundation model training impractical compared to inference or fine-tuning; second, cryptographic verification (zkML) carries heavy computational overhead; third, many "AI crypto" projects are thin wrappers around centralized APIs, and granting wallet keys to black-box AI agents exposes funds to prompt-injection attacks.

DefinitionThe intersection of Artificial Intelligence (AI) inference and automation with blockchain-based decentralized incentives, cryptographic verification, and on-chain settlement.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Think of AI as an automated chef and blockchain as a transparent pantry ledger and cash register: the chef automates complex cooking tasks while the ledger tracks ingredient provenance and pays suppliers automatically—yet if an attacker slips the chef a deceptive note (prompt injection), the chef might empty the cash register into a stranger's pocket.

A made-up example

Suppose a decentralized compute network routes 10,000 image-inference tasks to idle GPU nodes at $0.02 per task ($200 total), paying 85% ($170) to GPU providers and 15% ($30) to sampling verifiers. If the protocol instead required full Zero-Knowledge Machine Learning (zkML) proofs for every inference on-chain, proof generation costs could exceed $0.50 per task—over 25 times the inference cost itself (for illustrative purposes only).

Who developed or popularized it?

Distributed Compute Pioneers and Vitalik Buterin's Crypto × AI Taxonomy

Decentralized compute sharing dates back to early networks like Golem and Render Network (2016–2017). Following the late-2022 boom in large language models, the convergence of AI and crypto accelerated. On January 30, 2024, Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin published "The promise and challenges of crypto + AI applications," categorizing the intersection into four roles: AI as a player in the game (most viable short-term, such as autonomous agents), AI as an interface (helping users interpret on-chain transactions), AI as the rules of the game (requiring heavy zkML/cryptographic verification), and AI as the objective of the game, while warning of adversarial machine learning risks.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Identify the stack layer: distinguish between decentralized compute/data infrastructure, verifiable inference middleware (zkML/opML), and user-facing AI agents.
  2. Verify technical substance: check whether the protocol genuinely uses decentralized compute or on-chain verification rather than wrapping a centralized LLM API with a token.
  3. Distinguish inference from training: recognize that distributed consumer GPUs handle inference and rendering far more practically than bandwidth-heavy foundation model training.
  4. Constrain AI agent wallet permissions: isolate funds in dedicated sub-wallets with strict spending caps and limited token approvals (see the "Smart Contracts & Token Approvals" lesson on this site).
  5. Guard against adversarial inputs: watch for prompt injection and data poisoning targeting AI-driven oracles or automated execution bots.

The mistake to avoid

Misconception 1: Believing distributed consumer GPU networks can effortlessly replace hyperscale data centers for synchronous frontier model training. Misconception 2: Handing main wallet private keys to autonomous AI agents, where model hallucinations or prompt injections can drain funds. Misconception 3: Falling for fraudulent "AI trading bots" promising guaranteed returns (see the "Beginner Safety: Scams & Money Management" lesson on this site).
