Order Flow Analysis

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Order Flow Analysis (a microstructure approach that compares executed trades on the Time & Sales tape, resting limit orders in the Depth of Market order book, and footprint charts) asks how aggressive market orders and passive limit orders are actually interacting at key price levels—and whether large resting orders are being filled or canceled. Beginners frequently mistake unfilled limit orders sitting in the order book for completed trades or unbreakable support.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Think of a busy restaurant where people waiting in line outside are resting limit orders (Order Book) and paid receipts at the register are completed trades (Tape); anyone waiting outside can walk away at any second without paying.

A made-up example

Illustration only: compare the Order Book (DOM) with the Time & Sales (Tape) at the 100.0 offer. At Second 1, the DOM shows 1,000 limit sell contracts waiting at 100.0. At Second 2, the Tape prints 150 contracts bought at 100.0 (leaving 1,000 - 150 = 850 if nothing else changed). At Second 3, the 100.0 offer drops to 50 contracts with zero new trades on the Tape. Of the 1,000 - 50 = 950 contracts that vanished from the book, only 150 traded (15.8%), while 850 - 50 = 800 contracts (84.2%) were simply canceled before execution.

Market microstructure and tick-by-tick trading practice; no single originator verified

Order flow covers executed trades and resting orders, developed through trading practice and market-microstructure research rather than one verified sole inventor.

First: why look at this tool?

Order Flow Analysis separates executed trades (Time & Sales / Tape) from resting limit orders waiting in the order book (DOM / Depth of Market), helping you answer whether orders at a key price level are actually being consumed by trades or simply pulled (canceled) before execution. The golden rule is that resting limit orders can be canceled at any instant, whereas printed trades are completed facts.

When it is most useful

Useful on liquid, centralized venues when watching how price interacts with major highs, lows, or round numbers in real time. Avoid treating one exchange's order book as the entire global market when trading fragmented assets like spot crypto across dozens of venues.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

Order Book / DOM columns showing resting Bids (limit buys) and Asks (limit sells)
Lists unfilled limit orders currently sitting in the queue at each price step; every order here is tentative and can be canceled before filling.
Time & Sales (Tape) and Cumulative Volume Delta (CVD)
Logs actual completed transactions with price, size, and aggressor side as market orders hit resting limit orders.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: Heavy market buy orders print repeatedly on the tape at 100.0, yet the 100.0 offer refills and price fails to advance (absorption)

What it suggests: Passive limit sellers at 100.0 are absorbing the incoming market buy orders without letting price clear the level.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean 100.0 can never break; if the passive seller finishes selling, price can jump quickly.

You see: A large limit sell wall sitting at 105.0 vanishes as soon as price climbs to 104.8

What it suggests: The resting limit orders were canceled before price reached them rather than being bought through.

What it does not tell you yet: A disappearing wall on the DOM does not mean those contracts actually traded—always check the tape.

You see: Price ticks upward rapidly across several price steps on very small executed volume

What it suggests: The ask side of the order book is thin, allowing small market buys to move price easily.

What it does not tell you yet: Moves through a thin book can reverse just as quickly if sellers step in.

The easiest mistake to make

Trusting a massive limit buy wall on the Order Book as a safe floor when resting limit orders can be canceled in milliseconds at zero cost; assuming one exchange's order book represents the entire global market for a fragmented asset; over-trading every tick flicker on the screen.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Illustration only: watch the Order Book (DOM) and Time & Sales (Tape) as a contract tests the 100.0 level.

  1. At Second 1, the 100.0 ask displays 1,000 resting limit sell contracts; at Second 2, the Tape prints 150 contracts bought at 100.0.
  2. After 150 contracts trade, 1,000 - 150 = 850 contracts should remain if no orders were pulled; yet at Second 3 the 100.0 ask displays only 50 contracts with no further trades on the Tape.
  3. Arithmetic check: of the 1,000 - 50 = 950 contracts that disappeared from the book, only 150 traded (150 / 950 ≈ 15.8%), while 850 - 50 = 800 contracts were canceled.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Comparing the DOM against the Tape shows that 800 of the 950 vanished contracts were canceled orders, not completed sales.

But do not jump to this conclusion: Seeing 800 contracts canceled does not prove illegal spoofing, as market makers routinely pull and re-quote resting inventory.

One last question: did it click?

You see a 5,000-contract limit buy wall at 90 on the Order Book and assume 90 cannot break. One minute later, total traded volume is only 200 contracts, the 5,000-contract wall is gone, and price trades at 89. What happened to the 5,000-contract wall?

Think first, then open the answer

At most 200 contracts actually traded, while at least 4,800 resting limit buy contracts were canceled before execution; resting order-book walls can be pulled at any time and are not assured support.

A concrete practice sequence

  1. Decide whether you need executed trades or resting orders. Use trades/footprint/delta for executions and the order book or depth for resting liquidity, on the same market.
  2. Record aggressive trading, price response and book changes at the same time. Resting orders can cancel and must not be treated as completed trades.
