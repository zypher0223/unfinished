Dark Cloud Cover & Piercing Pattern

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

A dark cloud cover is like a cloud rolling over a sunny sky: price pushes to a new high, is driven back, and closes deep inside the prior bullish body. The deeper it cuts, the stronger the sellers; less than halfway does not count.

DefinitionDark cloud cover: the first bar is a long bullish bar; the second trades above the first bar’s high, then falls back to close as a bearish bar below the midpoint of the first body but above the first open. The piercing pattern is the reverse. The classical definition requires the second bar to open above the first high; 24-hour markets rarely allow that, so this lesson uses “the high exceeds the prior high” instead.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like reaching a new height and then slipping back below the halfway point of the climb.

A made-up example

Illustration only: the first bar opens at 100 and closes at 102 (midpoint 101); the second reaches 102.4 and closes at 100.7, below 101 but above 100: a dark cloud cover in this lesson’s 24-hour version.

Who developed or popularized it?

Japanese candlestick tradition; popularised in the West by Steve Nison

Candlestick patterns come from Japanese rice-trading tradition; the specific originator is unverified (often attributed to Munehisa Homma without reliable primary sources). Steve Nison’s 1991 “Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques” popularised them in the West.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Find a long bullish bar and compute its body midpoint.
  2. Check that the next bar trades above the prior high and closes bearish.
  3. Check the close is below the midpoint but above the prior open (below the open it looks more like an engulfing).
  4. Watch what follows: a close above the second bar’s high invalidates the pattern.

The mistake to avoid

Calling a bearish bar that closes above the midpoint a dark cloud cover is a common loosening; cutting less than halfway is not enough.
