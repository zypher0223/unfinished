Rising & Falling Wedges

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

In a rising wedge price still makes new highs, but each adds less while the lows climb quickly, squeezing the space. It is often read as a sign of a tiring rally, and it describes the same phenomenon as price action’s “three pushes”.

DefinitionRising wedge: swing highs and lows both rise, but the lows rise faster, so the two lines converge upward; a close below the lower line is a downside break. Falling wedge: highs and lows both fall, the highs faster, so the lines converge downward; a close above the upper line is an upside break.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like cycling uphill on a road that keeps getting narrower and harder.

A made-up example

Illustration only: highs of 103.0, 103.8 and 104.3 add 0.8 and then 0.5, while lows of 101.0, 102.2 and 103.1 rise by 1.2 and 0.9. The lows rise faster, so the lines converge upward: a rising wedge.

Who developed or popularized it?

Classical chart pattern; systematised by Richard Schabacker and by Edwards and Magee

Such patterns were described systematically by Richard Schabacker in the 1930s and popularised by Edwards and Magee’s 1948 “Technical Analysis of Stock Trends”; the earliest users are unverified.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Connect at least two highs and two lows and confirm both lines slope the same way and converge.
  2. Compare the slopes: in a rising wedge the lower line is steeper.
  3. Only a close below the lower line (rising wedge) is a breakout.
  4. If price then closes back inside the wedge and above the latest high, the pattern fails.

The mistake to avoid

Calling every upward-converging move a rising wedge without comparing slopes is a common error; equal slopes make a channel.
