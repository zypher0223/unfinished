Wyckoff Method

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The Wyckoff Method (an analytical framework combining price structure and volume through the laws of Supply and Demand, Cause and Effect, and Effort versus Result) asks whether repeated tests of a trading range's boundaries show fading selling pressure (accumulation candidate) or stalling buying power (distribution candidate). It reads the main price chart alongside the lower Volume pane. Beginners often treat textbook Wyckoff schematics as rigid scripts the market must follow.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

It is like watching an auction room by tracking both the price on the board and how loudly the crowd bids: if repeated dips to the floor price draw fewer and fewer sellers offering goods, supply may be drying up; still, buyers must actually step up and bid prices out of the room to confirm demand.

A made-up example

Illustration only: after a steep drop, an asset forms a 10-point trading range between 100 and 110. On Test 1 (Selling Climax), price drops 15 points from 115 to 100 on 5.0M volume before bouncing to 110. On Test 2 (Secondary Test), price dips to 100.5 on 1.8M volume (5.0M - 1.8M = 3.2M, or 64% lower volume). On Test 3, price pokes intraday to 98.5 (1.5 below 100) but closes back at 102 (+2 inside the range), and a pullback to 101 prints 0.9M volume. Contracting volume (5.0M → 1.8M → 0.9M) and the quick recovery from 98.5 point to fading supply, though price still needs to clear 110.

Who developed or popularized it?

Richard D. Wyckoff

Wyckoff organized price, volume and trading observations into a method. Later schematics and terminology may extend his original work.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

First: why look at this tool?

The Wyckoff Method compares price swings and bar ranges with volume across a trading range, helping you answer whether selling pressure is drying up near range lows (accumulation candidate) or buying power is stalling near range highs (distribution candidate). It provides a supply-and-demand testing framework rather than a rigid script that ensures what a 'composite operator' will do.

When it is most useful

Best used when a market enters a well-defined horizontal range after a prolonged decline or rally, allowing you to compare volume and candle spread on repeated boundary tests. Avoid forcing Wyckoff labels onto markets without reliable volume or calling every breakdown a 'Spring' before price actually reclaims the range.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

Horizontal trading-range boundaries set by the initial climax and automatic reaction on the main chart
Mark the bottom and top of the initial stopping range with horizontal lines to frame subsequent boundary tests.
Volume bars in the lower pane paired with candle range (Spread) on the price chart
Compare how much volume prints and how wide candles stretch each time price approaches the lower or upper boundary.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: Second and third visits to the range low print narrower candles and much lower volume than the initial sell-off

What it suggests: Fewer sellers are pressing offers at the bottom of the range compared with the initial drop.

What it does not tell you yet: Quieter selling does not ensure an immediate breakout; price can remain in the range or still break down later.

You see: Price briefly dips below the range low, closes back inside the range, and holds higher on a quiet pullback

What it suggests: Selling below the range low was absorbed and price reclaimed the boundary, matching a classic Spring test.

What it does not tell you yet: If price breaks the low on heavy volume and stays below it, that is a breakdown, not a Spring.

You see: Very high volume prints at the range high, yet the candle closes near its low with little net progress (effort without result)

What it suggests: Heavy trading effort failed to push price above resistance because overhead supply met the buying.

What it does not tell you yet: A single stalled bar does not prove distribution is complete.

The easiest mistake to make

Forcing every sideways range to match every letter of a textbook schematic when real ranges often skip a Spring or break down outright; calling a heavy-volume breakdown that closes at its low a 'Spring' and buying into a falling market; assuming price and volume reveal the private plan of a single mastermind.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Illustration only: after a steep drop, an asset ranges between 100 and 110; we compare price spread and volume across three tests of the 100 low.

  1. Test 1 (Selling Climax): price drops from 115 to 100 (15-point spread) on heavy volume of 5.0 million shares.
  2. Test 2 (Secondary Test): after bouncing to 110, price revisits 100.5 with a narrower 4-point spread on 1.8 million shares (down 5.0M - 1.8M = 3.2M, or 64% lower volume).
  3. Test 3 (Spring candidate): price dips intraday to 98.5 (1.5 below 100) but closes back at 102 (+2 inside the range), and the next pullback holds at 101 on 0.9 million shares.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Volume on tests of the 100 area contracted from 5.0M to 1.8M to 0.9M while the dip to 98.5 quickly reclaimed 100, showing fading downside pressure.

But do not jump to this conclusion: It does not prove a single secret operator controls the asset or prevent a future catalyst from driving price below 98.5.

One last question: did it click?

Support sits at 100. Today price plunges on massive volume and closes at the dead low of 95 with zero recovery. Can you label this 95 close a confirmed Wyckoff 'Spring' and buy immediately?

Think first, then open the answer

No. A Spring requires price to reject the breakdown and reclaim the 100 range boundary; closing at the low of 95 on heavy volume is an active breakdown, not a confirmed Spring.
