Volatility Smile & Skew

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Textbook baseline pricing models originally assumed that every strike price sharing the same underlying and expiration date should trade at the exact same Implied Volatility. Yet inspecting any live option chain (see the "Implied Volatility, IV Rank & Volatility Crush" lesson on this site) reveals that IV varies noticeably across strikes. Because real-world markets experience fat-tailed jumps and crashes, supply-and-demand imbalances for tail protection bend the horizontal line into a smile or a skew.

DefinitionVolatility Smile and Volatility Skew (or Smirk) describe the pattern in which options on the same underlying and expiration date carry different Implied Volatilities (IV) across strike prices rather than a flat line: when both low-strike and high-strike options trade at higher IVs than at-the-money (ATM) options in a U-shaped curve, it is called a Volatility Smile; when IV slopes asymmetrically toward one side (commonly higher IV at lower strikes in equity index options), it is called Volatility Skew.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Think of it as catastrophe insurance pricing: in equity markets, institutional investors hold massive long stock portfolios and fear sudden market crashes, so they persistently bid up out-of-the-money downside Puts as crash insurance while selling upside Calls—making low-strike IV much more expensive than high-strike IV and tilting the curve into a lopsided smirk (skew).

A made-up example

Illustration only: suppose an equity index trades at 5,000, and for a 30-day expiration the at-the-money (ATM) 5,000 strike option has an Implied Volatility (IV) of 18.0%. Due to heavy hedging demand, the downside 4,600 strike out-of-the-money Put trades at an IV of 25.0%—which is 25.0% - 18.0% = 7.0 percentage points above ATM—whereas the upside 5,400 strike out-of-the-money Call trades at an IV of 15.5%, or 18.0% - 15.5% = 2.5 percentage points below ATM. The 25.0% - 15.5% = 9.5 percentage-point gap between the 4,600 and 5,400 strikes illustrates classic equity downside skew.

Who developed or popularized it?

Persistent downside volatility skew in U.S. equity options emerged following the October 19, 1987 'Black Monday' market crash

Prior to October 1987, implied volatilities across U.S. equity option strikes were relatively flat, roughly matching the constant-volatility assumption of Black–Scholes. After the S&P 500 plunged over 20% on Black Monday (October 19, 1987), market makers and institutions permanently repriced left-tail crash risk, embedding a structural downside volatility skew across equity index options.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Hold expiration constant and plot Strike Price (or Delta) on the horizontal axis against Implied Volatility (IV) on the vertical axis to see whether the curve forms a symmetric U-smile or a tilted skew.
  2. Recognize standard equity index downside skew ('left side higher than right side'), where lower-strike out-of-the-money Puts carry higher IV than ATM and upside Calls.
  3. Note how foreign exchange, commodities, or FOMO-driven crypto rallies can instead display a two-sided smile or even reverse upside Call skew ('right side higher').
  4. When pricing multi-strike strategies, always check the specific IV at each individual strike rather than plugging the ATM IV into out-of-the-money contracts.

The mistake to avoid

Seeing high IV on out-of-the-money downside Puts and assuming they are 'mispriced free money' to sell naked; downside skew exists precisely because crash risk and jump illiquidity inflict severe tail losses on short put sellers.
