Supply and Demand Zones

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Supply and Demand Zone Analysis (a charting method that marks compact consolidation bases from which price previously departed with a rapid, wide-range impulse move) asks where a sharp historical imbalance launched and how price behaves on its first return to that base. A fast upward departure leaves a candidate demand zone; a fast downward departure leaves a candidate supply zone. Beginners often assume a drawn rectangle proves resting institutional limit orders are still waiting inside.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

It is like noting that a market stall sold out of goods in two minutes at $10 before prices jumped to $18: when price returns to $10 later, you watch to see whether buyers step in again rather than assuming the morning crowd is still standing in line.

A made-up example

Illustration only: on a 1-hour chart, set an upfront filter requiring a consolidation Base of at most 3 bars and a Departure leg at least 3 times the base height. Suppose two tight bars form a base between a wick low of 100.0 (Distal Line) and a body high of 102.0 (Proximal Line), giving a base height of 102.0 - 100.0 = 2.0 points. Three wide bullish candles immediately drive price from 102.0 to 110.0, a departure move of 110.0 - 102.0 = 8.0 points. The departure-to-base ratio is 8.0 / 2.0 = 4.0x (≥ 3.0x), qualifying [100.0, 102.0] as a candidate demand zone; a later close at 99.5 (0.5 below the 100.0 Distal Line) invalidates the zone.

Who developed or popularized it?

Chart application of supply-and-demand economics; no single originator verified

Supply and demand principles long predate modern zone-trading courses. Multiple educators use different zone rules; a sole first creator has not been verified.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

First: why look at this tool?

Supply and Demand Zone analysis marks compact consolidation bases from which price previously departed with a rapid, wide-range move, helping you answer where a sharp historical imbalance originated and how price behaves if it revisits that base. A fast upward departure marks a candidate demand zone; a fast downward departure marks a candidate supply zone. It highlights historical launchpads rather than proving resting institutional orders remain there.

When it is most useful

Useful when you define objective filtering rules upfront—such as a maximum number of base candles and a minimum departure-to-base ratio—so you mark only standout imbalances. Without strict rules, traders draw rectangles over every minor turn until the entire price axis is covered in zones.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

Consolidation Base rectangle bounded by the Proximal Line and Distal Line on the main chart
Frames the 1 to 4 tight base candles before the impulse; the inner edge closest to current price is the Proximal Line, and the outer extreme edge is the Distal Line (invalidation boundary).
Departure leg (Impulse move) and subsequent Fresh Retest
Compare the distance of the explosive departure leg against the height of the base box, and check whether the zone is untested ('fresh').

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: After two tight base candles, large bullish candles drive price away by several times the base's height

What it suggests: Buying strongly outweighed selling at that base, creating a well-defined candidate demand zone.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not prove unfilled institutional limit buy orders are still sitting in that box today.

You see: On its first return into the demand box, price slows between the proximal and distal lines and turns back up

What it suggests: Price respected the historical base while holding above the outer distal invalidation line.

What it does not tell you yet: Holding on the first visit does not ensure the zone will hold on a third or fourth visit.

You see: Price drops straight through the demand box and closes below the outer Distal Line

What it suggests: Sellers overwhelmed the base and broke its outer boundary, invalidating the demand zone.

What it does not tell you yet: Once broken, do not drag the bottom of the rectangle lower to pretend the zone held.

The easiest mistake to make

Drawing rectangles over every minor turn without requiring an explosive departure, until the entire chart is covered in boxes; assuming candlesticks can prove unfilled institutional orders remain in an old zone; dragging the Distal Line lower after a breakdown instead of accepting invalidation.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Illustration only: on a 1-hour chart, set a filter requiring a base of at most 3 bars and a departure move at least 3 times the base height.

  1. Two tight candles form a base between a wick low of 100.0 (Distal Line) and a body high of 102.0 (Proximal Line), giving a base height of 102.0 - 100.0 = 2.0 points.
  2. Three wide bullish candles immediately drive price from 102.0 to 110.0, a departure move of 110.0 - 102.0 = 8.0 points.
  3. The departure-to-base ratio is 8.0 / 2.0 = 4.0x (exceeding the 3.0x requirement), qualifying [100.0, 102.0] as a candidate demand zone; a later close at 99.5 (0.5 below 100.0) invalidates the zone.

Putting the observations together, you could say

With a 2.0-point base and an 8.0-point departure (4.0x ratio), the 100.0–102.0 zone and its 100.0 invalidation line are objectively defined.

But do not jump to this conclusion: A 4.0x historical departure does not prove the original buyers are still waiting at 102.0 when price returns.

One last question: did it click?

You mark a demand zone from 100 to 102 (Distal Line at 100). Today price drops and closes at 98.5. Is it valid to drag the bottom of your rectangle down to 98 and claim the demand zone is still intact?

Think first, then open the answer

No. The Distal Line at 100 was the predefined invalidation boundary; closing at 98.5 broke the zone, and moving boundaries after the fact defeats the purpose of having a rule.
