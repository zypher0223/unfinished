Historical Volatility

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Historical Volatility asks how variable recent percentage returns have been when expressed on a standardized annual scale. It plots as a percentage line in a lower pane. Beginners often misread '40% annualized HV' as a predicted 40% annual gain or a 40% maximum loss cap.

Volatility HVan indicator calculating the standard deviation of bar-to-bar logarithmic returns over N bars and scaling it to an annualized percentage
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Two cars can both average 60 km/h while one cruises steadily at 60 (low HV) and the other alternates between 110 and 10 (high HV); 'annualizing' is simply a unit conversion like converting meters per second into kilometers per hour, not a one-year forecast.

A made-up example

Illustration only: Annualized HV = Daily Return StDev × √(Trading Days per Year). Suppose an asset's 20-day standard deviation of daily returns is 2.0%. Using 252 stock trading days per year (√252 ≈ 15.87), Annualized HV = 2.0% × 15.87 ≈ 31.7%; using 365 crypto days per year (√365 ≈ 19.10), the exact same 2.0% daily StDev gives 2.0% × 19.10 ≈ 38.2% (6.5 percentage points higher). Also, if an asset gained +1% every single day, return dispersion would be 0 and HV would sit near 0%.

First: why look at this indicator?

Historical Volatility (HV) measures how unstable bar-to-bar percentage returns have been over a recent lookback window, usually scaled into an annualized percentage. It measures the dispersion of percentage returns rather than average price gain. Annualizing simply standardizes the ruler: a reading of 40% does not mean price will rise 40% over the next year, nor does it cap maximum loss at 40%.

When it is most useful

Use HV as a reference when you want to compare whether an asset's recent past has been steady or erratic. When comparing U.S. stocks with crypto, always check the annualization convention: stocks typically trade 252 days a year while crypto trades 365 days a year, and using 252 versus 365 changes the displayed number even on identical daily return data.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

The lower Historical Volatility pane and its percentage axis
A reading of 20 typically means 20% annualized, but confirm the display format, lookback length, and annual periods setting first. Calculations usually take the standard deviation of log returns between consecutive closes, which is not the same as raw price standard deviation.
Consecutive closing-price changes inside the lookback window on the main chart
HV looks at bar-to-bar return swings rather than just the net change from start to finish: if price swings wildly up and down before ending right back where it started, HV will still be high. The plotted value summarizes past return dispersion rather than promising future return distributions.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: HV climbs from a low level

What it suggests: Bar-to-bar return variations inside the lookback window have grown wider, indicating higher realized volatility.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean price is rising—a sharp sell-off also drives HV up.

You see: HV declines while price grinds higher

What it suggests: Bar-to-bar return variations have become steadier; an upward trend and lower realized volatility can coexist.

What it does not tell you yet: Low historical volatility does not mean zero risk of loss.

You see: Net price change from start to finish is zero, yet HV is elevated

What it suggests: Price experienced wide up-and-down swings along the way inside the window.

What it does not tell you yet: Finishing flat over the window does not mean the ride was calm.

The easiest mistake to make

Mistaking 40% Historical Volatility for a 40% return forecast or a hard cap on future losses; assuming low HV means price is flat when a steady +1%-per-day climb also produces low return dispersion; comparing 252-day and 365-day annualized figures without checking settings.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Hypothetical daily return standard deviation of 1%; using 252 trading days per year, annualized HV ≈ 1% × √252 = 15.87%.

  1. If the annualization factor is switched to 365 days, the displayed number becomes 1% × √365 ≈ 19.10%—the underlying price data did not change, only the annualization convention.
  2. In another window where daily return standard deviation is 2%, annualized HV under the 252-day convention is about 31.75%, reflecting wider daily return swings.
  3. Even if price started at 100 and ended back at 100 over that second window, HV remains high because of the large interim daily swings.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Standardize the formula and annualization day count first, then use HV to compare past realized volatility; price direction and net return must be read separately.

But do not jump to this conclusion: A reading of 31.75% is neither next year's expected profit, nor maximum drawdown, nor a fixed price boundary.

One last question: did it click?

If HV drops from 30% to 15%, can you say the asset price is going to rise by 15%?

Think first, then open the answer

No. A drop from 30% to 15% only shows that past bar-to-bar return dispersion narrowed under that formula. It does not determine price direction, future move size, or your holding return.

Search terms: Historical Volatility

Official built-in HV; inspect annualization and period settings. Practice setup: Add Historical Volatility and record length and annualization settings.

  1. Open the TradingView chart using the button below. Choose a stock or coin at the upper left and check the exchange. Choose a chart interval, for example 1 hour or 1 day; one daily candle represents one day.
  2. Use Indicators in the upper chart toolbar, then enter one of the search terms below. Check built-in Technicals first. Community scripts with similar names may calculate different things.
  3. Select the matching result to add it, close the search, and look for a new line or lower pane. If there is no exact built-in match, read the availability note rather than substituting a different indicator.
  4. Hover over the added indicator name and open Settings. Inputs changes calculation settings such as length; start with the defaults. Style changes visual appearance. Follow this lesson’s interpretation steps.
  5. Review completed historical candles and explain what the indicator showed at that time. Adding it to a chart does not place a trade. Practise reading it before any simulated execution.

From the book: cis Stock Trading

Source: cis, cis Stock Trading (Chinese edition, translated by Zhu Jia, China Youth Press, 2020, ISBN 978-7-5153-6031-7; Japanese original published by KADOKAWA, 2018)

What the author says

The author selects for volatility: at the same price, a stock that normally swings more is more efficient to trade. During the 2015 panic he saw Nikkei option prices jump within minutes and, judging that an extreme crash was limited by rules such as daily price limits, sold options heavily. Historical volatility in this lesson measures past swings, while option prices reflect the market’s pricing of future volatility; the two differ.

This is our English summary of the author’s view; the Chinese page of this lesson carries the original excerpt. The amounts and events are the author’s own experience, mostly in Japanese markets, and nothing here is trading advice.
