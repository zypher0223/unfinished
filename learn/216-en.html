On-Chain Identity: DID & SBT

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

In Web2, user accounts and social graphs belong to centralized platform databases and cannot be ported across apps. In early Web3, an identity is merely a hexadecimal wallet address (see the "Wallets & Exchange Custody" lesson on this site) that tracks token balances but cannot tell whether the owner is a real human or an automated script—leading to whale-dominated governance, Sybil attacks in airdrops (see the "Airdrops & Sybil Attacks" lesson on this site), and reliance on overcollateralized lending. Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Soulbound Tokens (SBTs) aim to fill this gap: DIDs provide user-controlled cryptographic identifiers paired with Verifiable Credentials (VCs), while SBTs use non-transferable tokens to record degrees, attendance, or credit history on-chain. Currently, the field faces three main bottlenecks: first, permanent public on-chain badges create severe privacy and doxxing risks; second, recovering a soulbound identity after private key loss or theft remains complex; third, fragmented standards across chains limit real-world adoption.

DefinitionDecentralized Identifiers (DIDs) are cryptographic digital identity standards controlled directly by the user, while Soulbound Tokens (SBTs) are non-transferable on-chain tokens bound to an account to represent credentials, reputation, or affiliations.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Think of a DID as a personal digital folder you hold the keys to, and SBTs as non-transferable diplomas or merit badges stamped inside: unlike collectible coins that anyone can buy, a diploma cannot be sold to someone else—proving your experience, while also meaning that if you leave the folder open in public, anyone can trace your life history.

A made-up example

An open-source developer guild issues non-transferable SBTs to 200 contributors who pass a security audit exam. When a protocol holds a technical governance vote, addresses holding that SBT receive a 1.5x voting weight multiplier compared to 1.0x for standard addresses; any attempt to transfer or sell the SBT on a marketplace is rejected by the smart contract (for illustrative purposes only).

Who developed or popularized it?

The W3C DID v1.0 Recommendation and the Decentralized Society (SBT) Paper

Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) were standardized by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) starting in 2019, culminating in the official W3C DID Core v1.0 Recommendation on July 19, 2022. Earlier that year, in May 2022, economist E. Glen Weyl, lawyer Puja Ohlhaver, and Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin published the paper "Decentralized Society: Finding Web3's Soul," borrowing the "soulbound" item concept from the game World of Warcraft to propose non-transferable Soulbound Tokens (SBTs).

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Distinguish identifiers from credentials: recognize that a DID is the base identifier (e.g., did:ethr), whereas Verifiable Credentials (VCs) and SBTs are attestations attached to it.
  2. Inspect transfer restrictions: check on a block explorer whether the SBT contract disables standard transfer functions and whether issuer revocation is supported.
  3. Evaluate privacy architecture: check whether the system stores sensitive data off-chain and uses Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs) for selective disclosure.
  4. Review key recovery mechanisms: determine whether social recovery (guardians) or issuer re-issuance is available if a wallet key is lost or compromised.
  5. Separate identity and financial wallets: avoid linking a publicly identifiable DID/SBT address directly to primary vaults holding sensitive funds.

The mistake to avoid

Misconception 1: Equating a naming service (like .eth) with a complete decentralized identity—a domain is a human-readable alias, not a non-transferable credential. Misconception 2: Indiscriminately minting public event SBTs on a main wallet, which builds a permanent profile for phishing and social engineering targets. Misconception 3: Assuming non-transferable SBTs are always safe—interacting with airdropped scam SBTs can still trigger malicious contract approvals (see the "Smart Contracts & Token Approvals" lesson on this site).
