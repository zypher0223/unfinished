McGinley Dynamic

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The McGinley Dynamic (a smoothing line that adjusts its tracking speed using the ratio of price to the line) asks how to reduce moving-average lag when price accelerates suddenly. It plots directly on the main price chart. Beginners often misread claims about reduced lag as meaning zero lag or predictive ability.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

It acts like a self-adjusting tether that adjusts its tension as the person ahead changes pace; however, the tether still follows the leader and can never step ahead of price.

A made-up example

Illustration only: using D = prior D + (Close - prior D) / [N × (Close / prior D)^4] with N = 10 and prior D = 100, if Close jumps to 110, 110 / 100 = 1.1, 1.1^4 = 1.4641, denominator = 14.641, and D = 100 + 10 / 14.641 ≈ 100.68; if Close drops to 90, 0.9^4 = 0.6561, denominator = 6.561, and D = 100 - 10 / 6.561 ≈ 98.48. It adjusts speed dynamically, but still lags behind 110 or 90.

Who developed or popularized it?

John R. McGinley

TradingView identifies John R. McGinley as the developer.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

First: why look at this indicator?

Use a dynamic line whose tracking speed adjusts to its ratio with current price to read overall direction and deviation. McGinley Dynamic aims to reduce some of the whipsaws and lag of fixed-speed moving averages, though it cannot eliminate lag altogether.

When it is most useful

Helpful when you want a single clean reference line on the main chart and want to see how it catches up after price stretches away; inspect it across sharp rallies, steep drops, and sideways ranges rather than cherry-picking only smooth historical stretches.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

The McGinley Dynamic curve on the main price chart
The right-hand axis shares the same price unit as the stock or coin; it is a recursively calculated dynamic price reference, not institutional cost basis or live resting limit orders.
The distance between closing prices and the curve, plus the curve's slope
Measure distance in actual price points or percentages rather than screen millimeters; zooming the vertical axis makes the same price slope look steeper without changing the underlying data.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: The curve slopes upward while consecutive closes stay above it

What it suggests: The smoothed historical direction and current closing position are both aligned upward.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not assure the line will hold as support on the next pullback.

You see: Price flips back and forth across the line while the curve flattens out

What it suggests: Direction over the current window is unclear, and minor noise easily triggers crosses.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean every cross is worth trading.

You see: Price drops sharply below the line before the curve visibly bends down

What it suggests: The sudden price move outpaced the recursive line's adjustment speed.

What it does not tell you yet: Never dismiss a real price drop just because the indicator reacts slowly.

The easiest mistake to make

Assuming 'reduced lag' means zero lag and expecting instant signals at market tops or bottoms; trading every line cross during sideways chop where repeated whipsaws still occur; treating the line as a assured support floor.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Hypothetical daily chart: the McGinley Dynamic line climbs from 98 to 102 as price rises from 100 to 106; then price drops on a single bar to 97 while the line still sits near 101.

  1. During the initial stretch, both price and the line rise together, showing aligned upward direction.
  2. After the sharp drop to 97, price sits well below the 101 line, breaking the prior above-line state.
  3. If price later bounces to 102, a single cross back above the line is not enough by itself to declare the uptrend restored—check whether higher highs and higher lows resume.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Dynamic speed adjustment helps the reference line adapt across regimes, but the reading order remains actual price first and smoothed backdrop second.

But do not jump to this conclusion: Price sitting above the line does not mean every holder is in profit, and breaking below it does not prove institutions exited.

One last question: did it click?

If price crosses back and forth between 99 and 101 across a McGinley Dynamic line sitting near 100, can you confirm a new trend?

Think first, then open the answer

No. Repeatedly swapping sides inside a tight range shows weak net direction; look for sustained directional follow-through rather than counting line crosses as trend evidence.

Once you can say “what I see → what it means → what is still uncertain”, practise with the platform walkthrough below.

TradingView

Search terms: McGinley Dynamic

TradingView has official documentation for this indicator; first search built-in technical indicators by the English name. Keep default length and compare price with the line on the main chart; sudden turns can still lead it.

  1. Open the TradingView chart using the button below. Choose a stock or coin at the upper left and check the exchange. Choose a chart interval, for example 1 hour or 1 day; one daily candle represents one day.
  2. Use Indicators in the upper chart toolbar, then enter one of the search terms below. Check built-in Technicals first. Community scripts with similar names may calculate different things.
  3. Select the matching result to add it, close the search, and look for a new line or lower pane. If there is no exact built-in match, read the availability note rather than substituting a different indicator.
  4. Hover over the added indicator name and open Settings. Inputs changes calculation settings such as length; start with the defaults. Style changes visual appearance. Follow this lesson’s interpretation steps.
  5. Review completed historical candles and explain what the indicator showed at that time. Adding it to a chart does not place a trade. Practise reading it before any simulated execution.
