Dow Theory

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Dow Theory (the foundational framework of technical analysis that separates price movement into the primary trend, secondary reactions, and minor daily ripples, while checking for confirmation across related averages) asks how to distinguish a multi-week pullback inside a bull market from a true primary trend reversal. It is applied on daily and weekly price charts. Beginners often try to force classic two-index confirmation onto unrelated assets or expect Dow Theory to pick exact tops.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

It is like watching the ocean tide come in: even during a rising tide (primary trend), individual waves wash back down the beach between surges (secondary reactions); watching one wave retreat does not mean the tide has turned.

A made-up example

Illustration only: on a weekly chart, suppose an index advances over several months from 1,000 to 1,300 (+300 points), then pulls back for three weeks to 1,150, retracing 1,300 - 1,150 = 150 points (150 / 300 = 50%, squarely inside the classic 1/3 to 2/3 secondary reaction range while holding well above 1,000). When the index rallies to close at 1,340 (+40 above the 1,300 peak) and the companion Transport average also clears its own prior peak (say from 650 to 665, +15), both averages confirm primary trend continuation. Confirmation is intentionally late and never predicts the exact top.

Who developed or popularized it?

Charles H. Dow; later compiled by Nelson, Hamilton, Rhea and others

The foundation comes from Dow's newspaper editorials; Nelson, Hamilton, Rhea and others systematized it later. Dow did not publish today's complete formulation himself.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

First: why look at this tool?

Dow Theory separates market movement into three scales—the primary trend (tide), secondary reactions (waves), and minor daily fluctuations (ripples)—while checking whether related averages confirm each other, helping you answer whether a pullback is a normal pause inside a broad trend or a structural reversal. It provides a framework for trend classification rather than exact top-and-bottom picking.

When it is most useful

Best applied on daily or weekly charts to keep the big picture clear during multi-week pullbacks, or when comparing broad indices (such as Industrials and Transports). Avoid forcing classic two-index confirmation onto two random altcoins that lack the economic link between manufacturing goods and shipping them.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

Primary Swing Highs/Lows versus Secondary Reactions on daily or weekly charts
Primary trends unfold across multi-month swings, whereas secondary reactions retrace roughly one-third to two-thirds of the prior primary swing over several weeks.
Paired index comparison panes (such as DJIA and DJTA)
Mark each index's prior swing high or low on matching timeframes to check whether both averages break their respective levels.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: After a multi-month advance, price pulls back about half of the last swing and then resumes making higher highs

What it suggests: The pullback acted as a secondary reaction within an intact primary uptrend.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean every 50% drop is harmless; if the next bounce fails below the old high and breaks the reaction low, the primary trend is questioned.

You see: Index A breaks above its prior swing high while Index B remains below its own prior swing high (non-confirmation)

What it suggests: The two related averages are out of step, warranting caution until both align.

What it does not tell you yet: Non-confirmation is not an automatic short signal; Index B may simply lag by a few bars before breaking out too.

You see: A bounce stalls below the prior peak and price then closes below the prior reaction low

What it suggests: The swing sequence has shifted to lower highs and lower lows, meeting the classic Dow reversal structure.

What it does not tell you yet: Because Dow confirmation waits for a lower high and a broken low, it always sacrifices the first portion of the turn.

The easiest mistake to make

Expecting Dow Theory to catch the exact top tick or bottom tick when its confirmation rule (waiting for a lower high and a broken reaction low) is designed to confirm after a turn is already underway; pairing two random crypto tokens and claiming they replicate the economic link between manufacturing and rail/truck shipping.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Illustration only: on a weekly chart, Average A rallies from 1,000 to 1,300 and pulls back to 1,150, while Average B rallies from 500 to 650 and pulls back to 570.

  1. Average A gained 1,300 - 1,000 = 300 points and retraced 1,300 - 1,150 = 150 points, a 150 / 300 = 50% pullback (within the classic 1/3 to 2/3 secondary range).
  2. Average A then climbs to 1,340, clearing its prior 1,300 peak by 1,340 - 1,300 = 40 points.
  3. At that moment Average B sits at 635 (still 650 - 635 = 15 points below its 650 peak); two weeks later Average B closes at 660 (+10 above 650), completing mutual confirmation.

Putting the observations together, you could say

When Average A hit 1,340 while Average B sat at 635, the breakout was unconfirmed; once Average B closed at 660, both averages confirmed continuation.

But do not jump to this conclusion: Mutual confirmation describes the trend state established so far; it does not prevent future macro shocks, and it confirms well above the 1,150/570 pullback lows.

One last question: did it click?

An index rallies from 1,000 to 1,300 (+300 points) and then pulls back over two weeks to 1,200 (-100 points) without forming a lower high or breaking a prior swing low. Does Dow Theory classify this two-week drop as a confirmed bear market?

Think first, then open the answer

No. Retracing 100 of 300 points (one-third) without a failed rally and subsequent breakdown is classified as a secondary reaction within the primary uptrend, not a confirmed primary reversal.
