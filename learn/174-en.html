Liquidity & Market Impact

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

DefinitionLiquidity is the ability to buy or sell a given quantity without moving the price much, commonly gauged by the bid–ask spread, order-book depth and volume. Market impact is the cost of your own orders pushing price against you, up when buying and down when selling; it can be estimated as average fill price − the mid price before the order (reversed for sells). Slippage is the gap between the planned price and the actual fill, and impact is one of its sources.

The price on the chart is not necessarily the price you can trade at. The larger your order relative to the book, the more it eats through pricier orders; thin markets, late hours and violent moves widen both spread and impact. Check how much you can fill before deciding how much to trade.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like buying every tomato at a small market stall at once: the first few kilos are at the normal price, then the seller starts raising it.

A made-up example

Illustration only: the ask has 300 shares at 100.0, 400 at 100.1 and 500 at 100.3. A market buy of 1,000 fills 300 × 100.0 + 400 × 100.1 + 300 × 100.3 = 100,130, an average of 100.13, which is 0.13 (about 0.13%) above the best ask of 100.0 before the order: the order’s impact cost, before fees.

Who developed or popularized it?

Market microstructure research; no single originator verified

Liquidity and impact are core questions in market microstructure, and Albert Kyle’s 1985 model is often cited as one theoretical starting point. cis describes how his own large orders moved prices: buying from 1,000 yen could end at 1,100, for an average cost of about 1,060.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Before ordering, check the spread and the size on the first few levels, and estimate how many levels your quantity will consume.
  2. Split large orders, or work them with limit orders, accepting that they may not fill completely.
  3. Avoid the thinnest hours; spreads often widen around major data releases.
  4. After the fill, record impact as average fill minus the pre-order price and include it in your expectancy.

The mistake to avoid

Assuming you can always trade at the chart price just from candles and volume, or sending one large market order in a thin market and getting filled at ever worse prices.

Source: cis, cis Stock Trading (Chinese edition, translated by Zhu Jia, China Youth Press, 2020, ISBN 978-7-5153-6031-7; Japanese original published by KADOKAWA, 2018)

What the author says

Once the author’s account grew large, his own orders moved prices against him: starting to buy at 1,000 yen could push the stock to 1,100, for an average cost around 1,060. He therefore caps how far he will chase and works orders while watching the book. This lesson uses such numbers to explain market impact and liquidity.

This is our English summary of the author’s view; the Chinese page of this lesson carries the original excerpt. The amounts and events are the author’s own experience, mostly in Japanese markets, and nothing here is trading advice.
