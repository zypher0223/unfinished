Climaxes & Exhaustion

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Late in a trend, a few unusually large bars appear on heavy volume, like a final sprint. Afterwards price often stalls or pulls back: that is exhaustion. A large bar alone does not mark the end; what follows decides.

DefinitionA climax is a run of unusually large bars in the trend’s direction at the end of a move, with ranges well above the recent average (here, more than twice the average range of the prior 20 bars), usually on rising volume. Exhaustion is when price then fails to continue and turns sideways or pulls back; it is judged only after later bars close without a new high (or low).
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like the last sprint of a marathon: afterwards the runner usually stops to catch breath.

A made-up example

Illustration only: the average range of the prior 20 bars is 0.6. A bullish bar with a range of 1.5 exceeds 2 × 0.6 = 1.2 and trades twice the usual volume, so it can be treated as a climax bar. If the next five bars make no new high, that is exhaustion.

Who developed or popularized it?

No single inventor verified; covered in Wyckoff’s buying/selling climax and Al Brooks’s price-action teaching

In the Wyckoff method a climax is the high-volume extreme at the end of a trend; price-action courses call the stall after consecutive large bars exhaustion.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Compute the average range of roughly the last 20 bars as the “normal size”.
  2. Find bars in the trend’s direction with ranges above twice that average and check for higher volume.
  3. Watch the next few bars for any close at a new high (or low).
  4. Only treat it as exhaustion if the move stalls or pulls back; new highs mean it was acceleration.

The mistake to avoid

Calling a top on one big bullish bar is a common misjudgement; climaxes can also lead to further acceleration, so exhaustion needs later confirmation.
