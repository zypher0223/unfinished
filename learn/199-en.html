Exchange Risk: Lessons from Mt. Gox, FTX & Bybit

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

An exchange feels like a bank, but most have no deposit insurance or bank-style capital rules. Large exchanges have failed repeatedly in the past, some through hacks and some through owners misusing client funds. Treating an exchange as a transit point, not a safe, is the first habit to build.

DefinitionA centralized exchange (CEX) holds users’ assets and matches trades internally: the balance you see is a debt the exchange owes you, not coins in your own on-chain wallet. The main risks are misuse or shortfalls by the operator (counterparty risk), theft by hackers (security risk) and freezes or shutdowns by regulators (compliance risk). A proof of reserves shows assets held at one moment; on its own it does not prove liabilities.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Keeping coins on an exchange is like leaving cash at a private storage counter: convenient day to day, but if it fails or is robbed you are just one creditor in the queue.

A made-up example

Illustration only: you hold a 1 BTC balance on an exchange that suspends withdrawals. Liquidation later shows it holds only 60% of client assets, so you may eventually recover about 0.6 BTC after years of waiting. Had that 1 BTC been moved to a wallet whose keys you control, the exchange’s collapse would not directly touch it.

Who developed or popularized it?

Three landmark events: Mt. Gox (2014), FTX (2022), the Bybit hack (2025)

In February 2014 Mt. Gox, then the largest bitcoin exchange, halted withdrawals and filed for bankruptcy with about 850,000 BTC missing; creditors only began receiving repayments in 2024. On 2 November 2022 a media report showed that FTX’s sister firm Alameda held a balance sheet dominated by FTT; a run followed and FTX filed for bankruptcy on 11 November. Founder Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud in November 2023 and sentenced to 25 years in March 2024. On 21 February 2025 a Bybit Ethereum cold wallet was compromised during a transfer, losing about 400,000 ETH (about USD 1.5 billion); the FBI attributed it to North Korea’s Lazarus group.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Keep on the exchange only what you plan to trade soon; move long-term holdings to a wallet whose keys you control.
  2. Prefer platforms regulated in major jurisdictions that publish proofs of reserves, and check whether the proof also covers liabilities.
  3. Turn on withdrawal allow-lists and two-factor authentication, and watch for scammers posing as support.
  4. If withdrawals pause, the exchange token crashes or executives leave suddenly, consider withdrawing first.

The mistake to avoid

Believing an exchange is too big to fail. Mt. Gox in 2014 and FTX in 2022 were both leading exchanges; size and fame do not replace segregated assets and outside audits.
