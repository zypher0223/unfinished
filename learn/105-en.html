New Highs/New Lows

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

New Highs/New Lows (a market-breadth indicator counting how many stocks in an exchange or index reach a new 52-week high versus a new 52-week low each day) asks whether leadership at the top of the market is expanding or whether stocks breaking down to yearly lows are quietly multiplying. It plots as separate lines or a net difference around zero in a lower pane. Beginners often compare raw counts across exchanges of very different sizes.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

It counts how many athletes in a league set a personal yearly best today versus how many hit a yearly worst: if the league's headline score edges up while personal bests dry up and personal worsts climb, underlying strength is thinning.

A made-up example

Illustration only: Net New Highs = 52-Week New Highs - 52-Week New Lows. In a 500-stock index, suppose on Rally A at index 5,000 there are 110 New Highs and 5 New Lows, giving Net New Highs = 110 - 5 = +105 (105 / 500 = 21% of the index). Two months later on Rally B, the index edges to 5,050, yet only 15 stocks hit 52-week highs while 25 hit 52-week lows, giving Net New Highs = 15 - 25 = -10. Yearly highs shrank by 110 - 15 = 95 stocks and fell below yearly lows, showing narrower leadership without ensureing an immediate drop tomorrow.

Who developed or popularized it?

Generic breadth statistic

Counting new highs and lows is established market practice. Authors of derivative indicators are not necessarily inventors of the underlying count.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

First: why look at this indicator?

New Highs–New Lows counts how many stocks inside a defined universe reached a new high over a specified lookback period (commonly 52 weeks) versus how many reached a new low over that same period. It checks whether strength or weakness is broadening out across individual stocks—like counting how many students in a class set a new personal-best score today. A 'new high' here always refers to a stated lookback span such as a 52-week high—not simply closing higher than yesterday, and not drawing a line on a single stock.

When it is most useful

Use it when checking whether an index advance is pulling a growing number of constituent stocks to fresh 52-week highs. Hold the stock universe, lookback period (e.g., 52-week vs. 20-day), intraday-high versus closing-high convention, and timeframe constant; because raw counts from a 500-stock universe and a 3,000-stock exchange cannot be compared directly, convert to percentages of total issues when comparing across different universes.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

The New Highs line, New Lows line, or net histogram in the breadth pane
Check the legend to see whether New Highs and New Lows are plotted as two separate lines or combined into a single net difference (New Highs minus New Lows); the right-hand unit is number of stocks (issues). A positive net difference means more stocks hit 52-week highs than 52-week lows—not that every stock in the market rose.
The universe name, lookback period label, and matching market index above
Verify whether the feed tracks 52-week or 1-month highs/lows and which exchange or index it covers. Always pair the breadth series with the price index of that same market rather than an unrelated asset.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: New High counts expand consecutively while New Lows remain near zero

What it suggests: A broadening group of constituent stocks is reaching the top of its lookback range.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean the rally can never end or that every new entry today is low-risk.

You see: The market index prints a fresh high while the number of New Highs keeps shrinking

What it suggests: Fewer individual stocks are sharing in the index's new record, indicating narrower leadership.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not prove the index will top out tomorrow.

You see: New Lows spike sharply

What it suggests: A growing share of stocks has fallen to the bottom of its lookback range, showing broadening downside pressure.

What it does not tell you yet: You cannot automatically call a bottom or buy blindly just because the count looks ugly.

The easiest mistake to make

Comparing raw counts between a 3,000-stock exchange and a 500-stock index without converting to percentages; assuming a drop in new highs forces an immediate market crash when narrow mega-cap rallies can persist for months; confusing a 52-week window with a 20-day window.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Hypothetical fixed 500-stock index universe tracking 52-week highs and 52-week lows.

  1. On Day 1, 100 stocks hit 52-week highs (20% of the 500 stocks) and 10 hit 52-week lows (2%), giving a net difference of 100 − 10 = +90.
  2. Several days later, the cap-weighted index edges to another new high, but only 20 stocks hit 52-week highs (4%) while 40 hit 52-week lows (8%), flipping the net difference to 20 − 40 = −20.
  3. Both facts are real at the same time: the index price reached a new high (often carried by a few mega-caps) while fewer constituent stocks made 52-week highs and more made 52-week lows.

Putting the observations together, you could say

New Highs–New Lows adds an internal participation scorecard underneath the headline index, prompting a closer look at breadth divergence.

But do not jump to this conclusion: Issue counts alone cannot prove manipulation or schedule a reversal date, and changing the lookback window (e.g., from 52 weeks to 20 days) will produce completely different numbers.

One last question: did it click?

If Market A prints 100 new 52-week highs and Market B prints 200 new 52-week highs on the same day, is Market B automatically stronger in breadth?

Think first, then open the answer

You cannot tell from raw counts alone. If Market A has 500 total stocks, 100 new highs is 20% of the market; if Market B has 3,000 total stocks, 200 new highs is only about 6.7%. Always normalize by total universe size and align the lookback rules before comparing.

Once you can say “what I see → what it means → what is still uncertain”, practise with the platform walkthrough below.

Where to find it: TradingView & AICoin

These paths describe desktop web charts, checked against official help on 19 October 2026. App layouts and account/data permissions can differ.

TradingView

Search terms: New highs / New lows / Market breadth

Official breadth fields and data symbols vary by market; this is not a universal single-asset indicator.

  1. Open the TradingView chart using the button below. Choose a stock or coin at the upper left and check the exchange. Choose a chart interval, for example 1 hour or 1 day; one daily candle represents one day.
  2. Use Indicators in the upper chart toolbar, then enter one of the search terms below. Check built-in Technicals first. Community scripts with similar names may calculate different things.
  3. Select the matching result to add it, close the search, and look for a new line or lower pane. If there is no exact built-in match, read the availability note rather than substituting a different indicator.
  4. Hover over the added indicator name and open Settings. Inputs changes calculation settings such as length; start with the defaults. Style changes visual appearance. Follow this lesson’s interpretation steps.
  5. Review completed historical candles and explain what the indicator showed at that time. Adding it to a chart does not place a trade. Practise reading it before any simulated execution.

AICoin

Search terms: 新高新低 / 市场广度 / New Highs New Lows

Search hints only; equity breadth coverage unverified. A coin's new high is not a market-wide count.

  1. Open the AICoin web chart below. Select an exchange and pair, such as BTC/USDT. Check whether you selected spot or a perpetual contract: their data can differ.
  2. Look for the chart-top Indicators button, labelled 指标 in Chinese. Search the abbreviation below first, then try the other terms. Common indicators may also appear in the shortcut bar below the chart.
  3. Add only an exact matching result. Check whether it appears over price or in a separate pane. Similar names do not guarantee the same formula.
  4. If the implementation exposes parameters/settings, open them and start with the defaults, then follow the interpretation steps on this page. Check the official help if your interface differs.
  5. No result may mean the feature is unavailable in your version. Check existing Custom indicators (自定义指标) only if you can verify their formula. Otherwise learn it on a platform that supports it; do not assume every indicator here is built in.
