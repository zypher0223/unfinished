Cost Distribution (Chip Distribution)

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Cost distribution tries to answer “at what prices did current holders buy?”. It infers this from volume and turnover; the chart looks precise, but every bar is an estimate. A volume profile only counts how much traded at each price, without inferring who still holds.

DefinitionCost distribution (“chip distribution”) is an estimate: it assumes each day’s trading replaces part of the existing holdings according to turnover, allocates volume across the day’s price range, accumulates it day by day while decaying older “chips”, and arrives at where current holders’ costs are roughly distributed. It rests on assumptions about turnover and allocation and is not a record of real holdings; it differs from a volume profile, which simply tallies volume traded at each price over a period.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like estimating, from the number of people entering and leaving a cinema each hour, what time the people still inside arrived.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a price traded heavily 10 days ago, but turnover has been high since, so cost distribution decays that portion sharply; a volume profile still counts all those 10 days’ volume at that price.

Who developed or popularized it?

No single inventor verified; common in Chinese charting software

Cost distribution (CYQ) is popular in Chinese stock software, with algorithms varying by vendor; its originator is unverified.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Confirm whether you are looking at cost distribution or a volume profile, and the platform’s assumptions.
  2. Find where the main concentrations sit and whether current price is above or below them.
  3. Use them as reference “possible cost zones” together with price action.
  4. Expect the distribution to change with the data source or decay settings; never treat it as real holdings.

The mistake to avoid

The most common misunderstanding is treating cost distribution as real position data, as in “the big players’ cost is right here”; it is an assumption-based estimate.

What the author says

The author says he never tries to decide whether a stock is being manipulated; he only watches the price, treating supply–demand imbalance and abnormal moves as short-term opportunities and selling early into them. Cost distribution in this lesson is an estimate from trades and likewise cannot prove who is buying.
