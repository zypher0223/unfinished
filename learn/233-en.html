Pig Butchering, Rug Pulls & Ponzi Schemes

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

All three promise steady, high or risk-free returns and let you watch a balance grow. The US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) puts it plainly: only scammers demand payment in cryptocurrency, only scammers guarantee profits, and never mix online dating and investment advice. In pig butchering the “platform” is often a site the scammer controls, and the balance is just a number in their database; when you ask to withdraw, you are told to pay “tax”, a “deposit” or an “unlock fee” first. Rug pulls are common with new tokens: the contract keeps powers to mint or block selling, or the team controls most of the liquidity and can pull it at any time.

DefinitionThree scams sold as “investments”: pig butchering (building a relationship or trust online, then steering you to a fake platform where your balance keeps rising but cannot be withdrawn); rug pulls (the team raises money or pumps the price, then pulls the liquidity, mints new tokens or blocks selling, and disappears); and Ponzi schemes (paying earlier participants’ “returns” with later participants’ money, kept alive by recruiting, collapsing once new money stops).
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Pig butchering is a “friend” of several months who takes you to a “bank” only they know, where your passbook grows daily but the counter is always “under maintenance”; a rug pull is a mall with a grand-opening sale that empties overnight after selling prepaid cards; a Ponzi scheme is pass-the-parcel, and whoever holds it when no new player joins loses.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a “platform” promises 1% a day. Compounded, after a year 1 × 1.01^365 ≈ 37.8 times, which legitimate investments practically never deliver over time. Zhou deposits 10,000 USDT; after 30 days the balance shows 10,000 × 1.01^30 ≈ 13,478 USDT. To withdraw he is asked to pay a 20% “tax” first: 13,478 × 20% ≈ 2,696 USDT. After paying, a “risk-control deposit” is demanded. The right stopping point is the first time you are told to pay before you can withdraw: stop sending money, keep the chats and transfer records, and report to the police or an anti-fraud channel. Anyone who later offers to “recover your funds” for a fee is very likely a second scam.

Who developed or popularized it?

The Ponzi scheme is named after Charles Ponzi in 1920s America; “pig butchering” translates a Chinese slang term for romance-plus-investment scams

Paying old investors with new investors’ money predates Ponzi, but the name comes from his large 1920 scheme. “Pig butchering” likens victims to pigs fattened before slaughter; English-language media and law enforcement adopted the literal translation. “Rug pull” is crypto slang for a team running off with funds and has no single originator.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Do the maths: convert the promised daily or monthly return into an annual compounded figure; an absurd number is a red flag.
  2. Check the platform: extra fees before withdrawal, support only in private chats, or a web address you did not find yourself as the official one all point to fraud.
  3. Check the token: see whether the contract keeps powers to mint, blacklist or block selling, whether liquidity is locked, and whether a few addresses hold most of the supply; a token you can buy but not sell is a honeypot.
  4. If you were scammed: stop sending money, keep evidence and report to the police; do not trust anyone who charges to recover funds.

The mistake to avoid

Treating the on-screen balance as real money, or paying more fees to “get the earlier money out”. The number is controlled by the scammer, and each payment is just another loss; paying a “recovery expert” afterwards often means being scammed twice.
