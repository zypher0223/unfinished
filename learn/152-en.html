Rectangles

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

DefinitionA rectangle forms when price moves between two roughly horizontal lines, touching the top at least twice and the bottom at least twice; a close beyond either is a breakout. It is the classical-pattern name for what price action calls a trading range (lesson 134); the rectangle view puts more weight on the breakout direction and reference distance.

A rectangle is price moving back and forth in a box. It is the same thing as a trading range; classical pattern analysis just cares more about which way the box breaks and roughly how far (often using the box height as a reference).

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like going up and down the stairs between two floors until one day you leave the building.

A made-up example

Illustration only: the top at 104.5 is touched three times and the bottom at 103.0 twice; the box is 1.5 tall. A close above 104.5 is an upside breakout, with a reference near 104.5 + 1.5 = 106.0.

Who developed or popularized it?

Classical chart pattern; systematised by Richard Schabacker and by Edwards and Magee

Such patterns were described systematically by Richard Schabacker in the 1930s and popularised by Edwards and Magee’s 1948 “Technical Analysis of Stock Trends”; the earliest users are unverified.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Draw the two horizontal lines, each touched at least twice.
  2. Measure the box height as the reference distance after a breakout.
  3. Record a breakout when a bar closes beyond the top or bottom.
  4. If price closes back inside after the breakout, treat it as a failure.

The mistake to avoid

Assuming a rectangle must break in the direction of the prior trend is a common misconception; it can break either way.
