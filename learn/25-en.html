Keltner

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Keltner Channels (in their modern form, an overlay placing upper and lower bands a multiple of Average True Range above and below an EMA midline) ask whether price is trading inside or outside a corridor sized by typical bar-to-bar range (ATR). They plot three lines directly on the main price chart. Beginners often confuse Keltner Channels with Bollinger Bands, overlooking that ATR-based width reacts much more smoothly than standard deviation.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Whereas Bollinger Bands flare out sharply whenever price scatters away from the mean, Keltner Channels size the road width using average step size (ATR), keeping the outer rails smoother and more uniform.

A made-up example

Illustration only: Middle Line = 20-bar EMA, Upper Band = EMA + Multiplier × ATR, and Lower Band = EMA - Multiplier × ATR. If the 20-bar EMA is 100, 10-bar ATR is 2.0, and Multiplier is 2, Upper = 100 + 2 × 2.0 = 104 and Lower = 100 - 2 × 2.0 = 96 (total width = 8). If a sudden wide bar lifts 10-bar ATR slightly from 2.0 to 2.3, the Upper Band edges to 104.6, allowing a strong close at 106 to finish outside the channel. This shows an expansion beyond normal ATR range, not a assured reversal.

Who developed or popularized it?

Chester Keltner; modern form popularized by Linda Bradford Raschke

Keltner described the basic idea in his 1960 book. Raschke popularized the modern EMA-and-ATR version; these should not be treated as one unchanged original formula.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

First: why look at this indicator?

Modern Keltner Channels typically use an EMA as the middle line and set the upper and lower bands at a multiple of Average True Range (ATR) above and below that EMA. The EMA tracks average direction while ATR measures typical single-bar volatility, combining into an envelope that expands and contracts with true range. Unlike Bollinger Bands, width is governed by smoothed ATR rather than price standard deviation.

When it is most useful

Helpful when you want to view average trend direction alongside how far price has stretched relative to recent bar range. Riding the upper band can reflect sustained trend strength, while pulling back to the middle line can be a routine pause; check the channel slope and whether price stays consistently on one side before interpreting band touches.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

The EMA middle line and the upper/lower bands on the main chart
For example, a 20-period EMA with a 10-period ATR and a multiplier of 2, all read in price units. Confirm that the script uses ATR for band width, as older Keltner variants use different range formulas that produce different numbers.
The distance between the upper/lower bands and the latest closing position
Wider band spacing means ATR has expanded; width itself carries no directional sign. Look left across multiple bars to check the middle line's slope and see whether price oscillates across the middle or stays in the upper or lower half.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: The middle line slopes upward while closes consistently stay in the upper half or near the upper band

What it suggests: The average direction is rising and recent prices are consistently stronger than the average.

What it does not tell you yet: Touching the upper band does not automatically mean you should sell or that price has topped.

You see: Channel width expands noticeably as price drops into the lower half

What it suggests: Bar volatility has expanded alongside weaker prices—separate volatility size from price direction.

What it does not tell you yet: Wider bands are not evidence of a price rally.

You see: The middle line is flat and price crosses back and forth across it

What it suggests: Average direction is unclear, making the channel mainly a volatility reference.

What it does not tell you yet: Every cross of the middle line is not a new trend.

The easiest mistake to make

Failing to check whether a platform uses the modern EMA ± ATR formula or Keltner's 1960 SMA ± High-Low range formula; shorting blindly when price closes above a rising Keltner upper band during a strong breakout; treating outer bands as fixed walls.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Hypothetical EMA = 100, ATR = 2 yuan, and multiplier = 2, placing the upper band at 104 and the lower band at 96.

  1. A close at 103 sits in the upper half of the channel, 3 yuan (or 1.5× ATR) above the middle line.
  2. Later, the EMA rises to 102 and ATR expands to 3, shifting the upper band to 108 and keeping the lower band at 96; a close at 107 stays in the upper half inside a wider channel.
  3. On the next bar, price closes at 101 below the middle line (102)—that single dip does not establish a long-term downtrend; watch subsequent closes and whether the middle line turns down.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Read average slope, ATR width, and relative price placement together to judge how standout a move is in the current volatility environment.

But do not jump to this conclusion: The upper band at 108 is not a cap on future highs, the lower band at 96 is not a guaranteed stop fill price, and an ATR multiple does not contain every possible price swing.

One last question: did it click?

If the Keltner upper band and Bollinger upper band both happen to read 104 on a bar, can you treat the two indicators as identical?

Think first, then open the answer

No. That is merely a momentary numerical coincidence: Keltner width scales with ATR (average bar range including gaps), whereas Bollinger width scales with closing-price standard deviation. As subsequent bars print, their widths will diverge—and because both derive from the same price series, they are not two independent forecasts.

  2. Use Indicators in the upper chart toolbar, then enter one of the search terms below. Check built-in Technicals first. Community scripts with similar names may calculate different things.
