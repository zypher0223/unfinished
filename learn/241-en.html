Calendar Spreads

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

A calendar spread exploits the fact that near-term options lose value faster than longer-dated ones (lesson 209: time decay accelerates near expiry). OIC’s notes say its maximum loss is the net premium paid, its maximum gain comes when the underlying sits right at the strike as the near-term option expires, and a sharp rise in implied volatility helps it greatly. Risk comes from two directions: if the underlying moves far from the strike, the two options’ values converge and the whole debit can be lost; and if the near option goes deep in the money it may be assigned early, leaving an unexpected long or short stock position to deal with.

DefinitionA calendar (horizontal) spread sells a near-term option and buys an option on the same underlying, same strike and same type with a later expiry. Opening it usually costs a net premium, which is the maximum loss. It does best when the underlying sits near the strike as the near-term option expires: the near option’s time value decays faster while the far option keeps more of its value; rising implied volatility also helps.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like subletting: you lease a flat long-term (the far option) and rent it out month by month (the near option), keeping the rent minus your lease cost; but if the neighbourhood changes drastically, the short-term contract can eat that margin.

A made-up example

Illustration only (multiplier 100): the stock is at 100; you sell the 30-day 100 call (3) and buy the 60-day 100 call (4.5), paying 4.5 − 3 = 1.5 net, i.e. 150, the maximum loss. After 30 days the stock is still 100: the near call expires worthless; suppose the far call is now worth 3.2 and you sell it, a gain of 3.2 − 1.5 = 1.7, i.e. 170. If instead the stock rises to 120 after 30 days: the near call must be bought back at its intrinsic value of 20, and suppose the far call is worth 21 (20 intrinsic + about 1 time value); the pair nets 21 − 20 = 1, less than the 1.5 paid, a loss of 0.5 × 100 = 50. The further the stock runs, the closer the two values get, and the loss approaches the 150 cap.

Who developed or popularized it?

The calendar spread is a standard option combination with no single inventor; this lesson follows OIC’s strategy notes

OIC’s “Long Call Calendar Spread (Call Horizontal)” notes: short one call and long a call with a more distant expiry; maximum loss is the net premium paid; the best result comes with the underlying near the strike at the near expiry; a sharp rise in implied volatility has an extremely positive effect.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Confirm the two legs: same underlying, strike and type; sell the near month, buy the far month.
  2. Work out the net debit before opening; that is the maximum loss.
  3. While holding, watch whether the underlying is near the strike as the near option expires, and whether implied volatility is rising or falling.
  4. At the near expiry, decide whether to close the whole spread or sell the next near-term option; do not leave an assigned stock position unmanaged.

The mistake to avoid

Thinking “the option I sold will expire anyway, so it is free money”. A large move away from the strike makes the two options converge and can cost the whole debit, and a deep in-the-money near option can be assigned early.
