Trend Bars & Doji

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

DefinitionA trend bar has a body covering most of its range and a close near one end of the range; a doji has an open very close to its close and a small body, here taken as a body smaller than one tenth of the range.

A trend bar steps firmly in one direction and closes near that end, while a doji hesitates in place and closes almost where it opened, showing only a temporary balance between buyers and sellers.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Watch whether a person takes a large step forward or probes both ways and ends up back where they started.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a trend bar opens at 105.99, closes at 106.89, with a high of 106.99 and a low of 105.84, so its 0.90 body covers most of its 1.15 range and closes near the high; a doji opens at 106.89, closes at 106.91, with a high of 107.36 and a low of 106.49, so its 0.02 body is small.

Who developed or popularized it?

No single inventor verified; Al Brooks is a key contemporary educator

The doji is an old Japanese candlestick concept, taught systematically in modern price-action courses by educators such as Al Brooks, who is not presented here as its sole inventor.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. First compare the body with the whole range, then note whether the close sits near the top, bottom or middle.
  2. Read a doji together with the preceding trend, not on its own.
  3. Write down the observation location: after a trend bar, near a key level, or in the middle of a range.
  4. Wait for the next close to confirm or invalidate the reading, and keep waiting when there is no confirmation.

The mistake to avoid

A common mistake is to read a doji directly as a reversal signal. It only shows a temporary balance in that bar; direction depends on location and later closes.
