Straddles & Strangles

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Straddles and strangles are often used before events that might cause big moves, such as earnings or central-bank decisions. Per OIC, a straddle’s two breakevens are the strike ± the total premium; a strangle’s are the call strike + total premium and the put strike − total premium. Time decay hurts them badly (both options lose time value at once), while rising implied volatility helps. The catch: before the event everyone expects a big move, so implied volatility is usually already high and the premiums expensive; after the announcement implied volatility often drops sharply (the volatility crush in lesson 210), and if the price does not move enough, both options lose value together. A strangle is cheaper than a straddle but needs a bigger move to break even.

DefinitionA long straddle buys a call and a put on the same underlying with the same expiry and the same strike; a long strangle buys a higher-strike call and a lower-strike put. Both profit from a large move in either direction, and the maximum loss is the total premium paid.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like buying two insurance policies: one pays if prices rise, the other if they fall, but you pay both premiums, and if nothing happens both are wasted.

A made-up example

Illustration only (at expiry, multiplier 100): the stock is at 100; before earnings you buy the 100-strike call (5) and put (4.5), total 9.5, i.e. 950. Breakevens are 100 + 9.5 = 109.5 and 100 − 9.5 = 90.5. At expiry the stock is 106: the call is worth 6 and the put 0, so 6 − 9.5 = −3.5, a loss of 350 even after a 6% rise. A strangle: buy the 105 call (2) and the 95 put (1.8), total 3.8 i.e. 380, breakevens 105 + 3.8 = 108.8 and 95 − 3.8 = 91.2; at 106 the call is worth 1, so 1 − 3.8 = −2.8, a loss of 280.

Who developed or popularized it?

Straddles and strangles are standard option combinations with no single inventor; the breakeven formulas match OIC’s strategy notes

OIC’s long-straddle notes give a maximum loss of both premiums, call time decay “extremely important, negative effect”, and say rising implied volatility helps; its long-strangle (long combination) notes give breakevens at the call strike plus premiums and the put strike minus premiums.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Work out both breakevens first: how far the underlying must move to recover the cost.
  2. Compare that move with the move implied by option prices; expensive premiums mean the market already expects a big swing.
  3. Remember implied volatility often falls sharply after the event (lesson 210), so you can lose even if the price moves.
  4. Decide in advance when to exit: time value bleeds every day, so do not hold indefinitely.

The mistake to avoid

Thinking “I win whether it goes up or down” means you cannot lose. If the move is too small, both premiums are lost; buying right before the event means paying peak implied volatility and then suffering its drop.
