What a Stock Is

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Buying one share means owning a very small piece of the company. If it earns money, pays dividends or gains favour, shareholders benefit; if it loses money or goes bankrupt, the price can fall close to zero. The price moves every day, but behind it is a real operating business.

DefinitionA share of stock is a unit of ownership in a company. Holders are shareholders: they share in profits (for example through cash dividends) in proportion to their stake and bear the risk if the business fails, and common shares usually carry voting rights. Market capitalization = share price × shares outstanding; the price is set by buyers and sellers trading on an exchange, not fixed daily by the company.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like a few friends opening a noodle shop and splitting profits by how much each put in; a listed company simply has thousands of such partners, and their stakes can change hands in the market at any time.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a company has 1 billion shares outstanding at 25 each, so its market cap is 25 × 1 billion = 25 billion. If you hold 1,000 shares, you own 0.0001% of it; a dividend of 0.5 per share pays you 1,000 × 0.5 = 500 before tax.

Who developed or popularized it?

The joint-stock company; the Dutch East India Company (1602) is often cited as one of the first to issue shares to the public

Splitting ownership into transferable shares is the heart of the joint-stock company. The Dutch East India Company, founded in 1602, sold shares to the public that traded in Amsterdam, and is often cited as one starting point of modern stock trading.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Know which company you are buying, where it is listed and its ticker.
  2. Look at market cap, not just the share price: a low price does not mean a small or cheap company.
  3. Learn how the company makes money and whether it pays dividends before deciding to hold it.
  4. Remember that stocks are not capital-protected; at worst the company fails and the shares become nearly worthless.

The mistake to avoid

Judging cheap or expensive by the share price, for example thinking a 2-dollar stock is “cheaper” than a 200-dollar one; compare market cap and earnings, not the price per share.
