Implied Volatility, IV Rank & Volatility Crush

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

An Implied Volatility reading of 40% might be historically expensive for a quiet utility stock yet historically cheap for a high-beta tech stock or cryptocurrency. To judge whether an asset's options are rich or cheap relative to its own past (distinct from realized past movement—see the "Historical Volatility" and "Options Basics" lessons on this site), traders compare IV Rank and IV Percentile, while watching out for post-event Volatility Crush around catalysts (see the "Earnings Season & Guidance" lesson on this site).

DefinitionImplied Volatility (IV) is the annualized expected volatility backed out from an option's live market price using a pricing model. IV Rank (IVR) measures where current IV sits within its 52-week high-low range: (Current IV - 52-Week Low IV) ÷ (52-Week High IV - 52-Week Low IV) × 100%. IV Percentile (IVP) counts the percentage of trading days over the past year on which IV closed below the current reading. Volatility Crush (IV Crush) is the sharp collapse in IV immediately after a major catalyst (such as an earnings release) resolves uncertainty, deflating option time value.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Think of IV as the live price of umbrellas before a storm; IV Rank marks where today's price sits on the ruler between the year's cheapest and most expensive price tags; IV Percentile counts how many days over the past year umbrellas cost less than today; and IV Crush is the morning the storm warning is canceled, when panic demand vanishes and prices drop instantly.

A made-up example

Illustration only: suppose a stock's 52-week IV ranged from a low of 20% to a high of 60%, and ahead of earnings current IV reaches 50%. Its IV Rank is (50% - 20%) ÷ (60% - 20%) × 100% = 30% ÷ 40% × 100% = 75%; if 210 out of the past 252 trading days had IV below 50%, its IV Percentile is 210 ÷ 252 × 100% ≈ 83.3%. If the stock is at $100 and a trader pays $5.50 for a $105 Call, and the next morning after earnings the stock rises to $103 while IV plunges from 50% to 25% (IV Crush), collapsing time value can drop the option price to $1.80, leaving a $5.50 - $1.80 = $3.70 loss despite the stock rising.

Who developed or popularized it?

Implied volatility was formalized by Latané and Rendleman in 1976; Cboe launched the VIX index in 1993

In 1976, economists Henry Latané and Richard J. Rendleman introduced implied standard deviations backed out from the Black–Scholes formula. In 1993, Cboe launched the VIX index designed by Professor Robert Whaley (see the "VIX Volatility Index" lesson on this site), and in recent years retail options platforms popularized IV Rank and IV Percentile.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Compare current IV against its own 52-week history using IV Rank and IV Percentile rather than judging a standalone IV percentage in isolation.
  2. Account for the formula difference: a single extreme outlier spike during the past year stretches the high-low denominator and depresses IV Rank, so cross-check with IV Percentile.
  3. Inspect the option chain before scheduled catalysts (such as earnings or macro decisions), where front-week IV is often heavily elevated.
  4. Compare the option-implied move against realistic underlying price action: if the post-event move falls short of what the pre-event premium priced in, IV Crush will erode long option values.

The mistake to avoid

Assuming an 80% IV Rank guarantees volatility will drop immediately, or buying pre-earnings options for direction alone while ignoring IV Crush; a high IV Rank does not rule out an even larger realized tail move.
