Bollinger Bands

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Bollinger Bands (an overlay placing upper and lower bands two standard deviations above and below a 20-bar simple moving average) ask how wide recent price dispersion has been and where current price sits relative to that dynamic envelope. They plot middle, upper, and lower bands directly on the main price chart. Beginners often treat the upper band as a rigid ceiling and the lower band as a solid floor.

A small worked example, then three interpretations

Separate worked example: these numbers are for hand calculation, not the plotted data.

Assume middle SMA=100, population standard deviation σ=2, k=2: upper=104 and lower=96. Touching the upper band does not ensure a fall.

  1. Near the upper band: relatively strong; band-walking can persist.
  2. Near the lower band: relatively weak, not an automatic buy.
  3. Narrowing bands: volatility contraction, without a direction forecast.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

They act like flexible lane markers that narrow when driving is calm and widen when swings grow rough; however, the markers only summarize recent dispersion and do not physically block a car from riding along the outer edge.

A made-up example

Illustration only: Middle Band = 20-bar SMA, Upper Band = Middle + 2 × SD, and Lower Band = Middle - 2 × SD. Suppose the 20-bar SMA is 100. In a quiet period where standard deviation (SD) is 2, the Upper Band is 100 + 2 × 2 = 104 and the Lower Band is 100 - 2 × 2 = 96 (total width = 104 - 96 = 8). In a volatile period where SD rises to 5, the bands widen to 100 + 2 × 5 = 110 and 100 - 2 × 5 = 90 (width = 20). Touching 104 does not force a drop; strong trends can 'walk the band' higher.

Who developed or popularized it?

John Bollinger

Bollinger developed the bands in the early 1980s, as described on his own website. His first live trade is not established here.

First: why look at this indicator?

Bollinger Bands plot a recent average price line in the middle, then draw upper and lower bands at a multiple of price standard deviation above and below it. The wider the dispersion of recent prices, the wider the bands spread apart. They help you read both 'where price sits relative to its recent range' and 'whether volatility is expanding or contracting'—the upper and lower bands are not solid walls that force price to turn around.

When it is most useful

Helpful when you want to distinguish quiet periods from active ones and see whether price is hugging one side of the band or oscillating around the middle line. Touching the upper band can mean persistent strength in a trend or simply reaching the top of a range in a sideways market; always identify the broader context first.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

The three lines on the main chart: middle band, upper band, and lower band
Typically set to a 20-period SMA plus or minus 2 standard deviations, all read in price units on the right axis. Standard deviation measures recent price dispersion, not trading volume. Identify each band by its label rather than by color.
The width between the upper and lower bands and the closing position of each bar
Check whether the bands are expanding or narrowing, then see which side the close sits near; an intraday upper wick poking the band is a very different picture from multiple bars closing outside the band. On an unclosed bar, the bands themselves are still shifting.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: The bands narrow tightly while price moves within a small range

What it suggests: Recent price dispersion has shrunk, placing the market in a relatively quiet state.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not tell you whether the next move will be up or down, and quiet conditions can persist.

You see: Price repeatedly hugs the upper band while the bands widen and the middle line slopes up

What it suggests: Directional momentum is strong and recent volatility is expanding.

What it does not tell you yet: Touching the upper band alone is not an automatic sell signal.

You see: One bar pokes outside the lower band and the next bar closes back inside the bands

What it suggests: A downward deviation occurred and then pulled back inside; watch whether subsequent bars hold.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not assure a bottom is in—the next bar can still resume falling.

The easiest mistake to make

Mechanically shorting every upper-band touch and buying every lower-band touch, which gets run over when volatility expands and price walks the band in a strong breakout; assuming market prices follow a textbook normal distribution where 95% of future bars must stay inside the bands.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Hypothetical 20-period average price of 100 yuan, price standard deviation of 2, multiplier of 2, giving an upper band at 104 and a lower band at 96.

  1. A close at 103 sits above the middle band and below the upper band, relatively high within the current window.
  2. Later, the average rises to 102 and standard deviation expands to 3, shifting the bands to 108 and 96; a close at 107 is higher in price yet still sits inside the widened bands.
  3. If the next bar closes at 109 (above 108), it simply means price exceeded the current statistical band—and that new price will widen the band further on recalculation.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Read band position, band width, and middle-band slope together rather than fixating on a single band touch. Because the bands recalculate with new data, never treat historical band lines as fixed future ceilings or floors.

But do not jump to this conclusion: Two standard deviations do not lock in a fixed probability or force price back to the middle line, as financial markets do not strictly follow a simple normal distribution.

One last question: did it click?

If price touches the upper band and then keeps rising for three more bars, does that mean Bollinger Bands failed?

Think first, then open the answer

Not necessarily. The upper band is a rolling dispersion reference, not a hard ceiling. 'Walking the upper band' as the bands widen is a classic strong-trend pattern; check closing prices and band expansion together.

Official built-in BB; the similarly named strategy is a different tool. Practice setup: Add Bollinger Bands with Length 20 and multiplier 2.

  1. Open the TradingView chart using the button below. Choose a stock or coin at the upper left and check the exchange. Choose a chart interval, for example 1 hour or 1 day; one daily candle represents one day.
  2. Use Indicators in the upper chart toolbar, then enter one of the search terms below. Check built-in Technicals first. Community scripts with similar names may calculate different things.
  3. Select the matching result to add it, close the search, and look for a new line or lower pane. If there is no exact built-in match, read the availability note rather than substituting a different indicator.
  4. Hover over the added indicator name and open Settings. Inputs changes calculation settings such as length; start with the defaults. Style changes visual appearance. Follow this lesson’s interpretation steps.
  5. Review completed historical candles and explain what the indicator showed at that time. Adding it to a chart does not place a trade. Practise reading it before any simulated execution.
