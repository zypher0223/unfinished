Dividends & Corporate Actions

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Dividends are not free money. Cash payouts reduce corporate net equity, prompting stock exchanges to mark down ex-dividend open prices. Unadjusted charts display artificial cliff-like gaps that must be corrected via adjusted pricing.

DefinitionEx-dividend and ex-rights represent the regulatory adjustment of a stock's market trading price following cash dividend distributions, bonus shares, or rights offerings. Ex-dividend reference price = Prior close - Dividend per share. Forward-adjusted charts scale historical prices to current levels to remove artificial gaps, while backward-adjusted charts preserve original historical baselines to track total compounding returns.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Dividends resemble transferring cash between pockets in the same wallet; total net worth remains exactly unchanged.

A made-up example

Illustration only: A stock closes at $102.50 on its record date with a declared $2.50 cash dividend. On the ex-dividend date, the baseline opening price adjusts to $100.00 ($102.50 - $2.50). The investor's equity position drops by $2.50 per share but receives $2.50 in cash dividends, keeping pre-tax wealth balanced. An unadjusted chart displays an artificial gap down, which forward-adjusted charts eliminate.

Who developed or popularized it?

Joint-stock Corporate Dividend Law and Exchange Ex-Dividend Conventions

The Dutch East India Company, founded in 1602, was among the first companies to pay recurring dividends to public shareholders. Today's ex-dividend rules are set by exchanges and clearing houses and change with settlement cycles; for example, after the US moved to T+1 settlement in May 2024, the ex-dividend date usually falls on the record date.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Identify the key corporate timeline: Announcement Date, Record Date, Ex-Dividend Date, and Payment Date.
  2. Check eligibility rules: in China A-shares you must be on the register at the record-date close, and the ex-date is the next trading day; in the US, since the 2024 move to T+1 settlement, the ex-date is usually the record date itself, so you must buy before the ex-date. Buying on or after the ex-date does not earn this dividend.
  3. Toggle forward-adjusted pricing on charting software to maintain continuous trendlines and accurate moving averages.
  4. Analyze corporate action types: share buybacks boost EPS by retiring shares, whereas unexercised rights offerings dilute your holdings.
  5. Avoid high-dividend-yield traps where the underlying stock continually decays post-dividend without achieving capital recovery.

The mistake to avoid

Assuming cash dividends represent free windfalls, ignoring that stock prices adjust downward dollar-for-dollar upon the ex-dividend date.
