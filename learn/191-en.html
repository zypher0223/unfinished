Trading Plan & Journal

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Live market fluctuations easily trigger emotional impulses that override risk discipline. A written trading plan makes rational decisions before placing an order, while a trading journal provides an objective audit trail to diagnose execution flaws.

DefinitionA Trading Plan is a pre-trade written rulebook specifying the target asset, entry trigger, position size (Max Acceptable Dollar Risk / Stop Distance per Share), invalidation stop-loss, and exit criteria; a Trading Journal is a post-trade ledger documenting execution metrics, rule deviations, and emotional states.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

A trading plan is a pilot's pre-flight checklist, while a trading journal is the post-flight black box recorder.

A made-up example

Illustration only: A trader with a $100,000 account limits single-trade risk to 1% ($1,000). Planning to buy a stock at $50 with an invalidation stop-loss at $48 ($2 risk per share), the maximum position size is $1,000 / ($50 - $48) = 500 shares ($25,000 capital). After closing the trade, the trader logs the exact fill prices, fees, and rule adherence to compute win rate and payoff ratios over time.

Who developed or popularized it?

Alexander Elder and Modern Trading Psychology Practice

No single originator verified; psychiatrist and trader Alexander Elder popularized structured trading diaries in his 1993 book 'Trading for a Living', alongside performance psychologist Brett Steenbarger.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Draft a pre-trade plan before entry: define the ticker, objective trigger conditions, and maximum position size.
  2. Set the invalidation stop-loss price before calculating share size, committing never to widen stops impulsively mid-trade.
  3. Log every closed trade immediately: record entry/exit timestamps, net P&L after fees, setup tags, and emotional notes.
  4. Conduct a weekly review separating 'plan-compliant trades' from 'impulse trades' to isolate strategy flaws from discipline errors.
  5. Avoid selective logging; omitting losing trades corrupts statistical expectancy calculations and masks recurring behavioral leaks.

The mistake to avoid

Confusing a vague directional bias with a concrete trading plan, or selectively logging winning trades while ignoring losses, which destroys statistical feedback.
