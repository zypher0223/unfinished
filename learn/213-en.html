Vertical Spreads

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

When buying a standalone Call or Put (see the "Options Basics" lesson on this site), hefty upfront premiums and daily time decay (Theta) create a high hurdle to break even. If a trader expects a moderate move toward a target rather than a runaway explosion, they can buy one option and simultaneously sell a further out-of-the-money option in the same expiration to subsidize the cost—forming a Vertical Spread. Knowing what a vertical spread adds and what it gives up relative to a single-leg option is essential for managing risk and reward (see the "Expectancy & Win Rate" lesson on this site).

DefinitionA Vertical Spread involves simultaneously buying 1 option and selling 1 option of the exact same type (both Calls or both Puts) on the same underlying asset and with the same expiration date, but at different strike prices. Common debit vertical spreads include the Bull Call Spread (buy a lower-strike Call, sell a higher-strike Call) and the Bear Put Spread (buy a higher-strike Put, sell a lower-strike Put).
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

A debit vertical spread is like buying a concert ticket while immediately reselling the voucher for the late-night encore to someone else: the cash from selling the encore voucher lowers your upfront ticket cost and makes it easier to get your money's worth, in exchange for giving up any upside if the band plays an unforgettable two-hour encore.

A made-up example

Illustration only: suppose a stock trades at $100 with 30 days to expiration. Example 1 (Bull Call Spread): you pay $4.00 to buy the $100 Call and collect $1.50 by selling the $105 Call, resulting in a Net Debit of $4.00 - $1.50 = $2.50; expiration breakeven is Lower Strike $100 + Net Debit $2.50 = $102.50 (lower than the $104.00 breakeven of buying the $100 Call alone); Maximum Loss is capped at the $2.50 Net Debit (if stock ≤ $100 at expiry); Maximum Profit is Strike Width ($105 - $100) - Net Debit $2.50 = $5.00 - $2.50 = $2.50 (if stock ≥ $105 at expiry). Example 2 (Bear Put Spread): you pay $4.20 to buy the $100 Put and collect $1.70 by selling the $95 Put, for a Net Debit of $4.20 - $1.70 = $2.50; expiration breakeven is Higher Strike $100 - Net Debit $2.50 = $97.50; Maximum Loss is $2.50 (if stock ≥ $100), and Maximum Profit is ($100 - $95) - $2.50 = $2.50 (if stock ≤ $95).

Who developed or popularized it?

The name "Vertical" spread is commonly explained by quote tables listing strikes vertically

The usual explanation: option quote tables list strikes for one expiration vertically in a column and spread expirations horizontally, so a same-expiry, two-strike combination became a Vertical Spread, while a same-strike, two-expiry combination is a Horizontal or Calendar Spread.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Verify that both legs share the exact same underlying asset, expiration date, and option type (both Calls or both Puts), differing only by strike price.
  2. Compute debit vertical spread bounds: Net Debit = Long Leg Premium - Short Leg Premium; Maximum Loss = Net Debit; Maximum Profit = Strike Width - Net Debit.
  3. Compute expiration breakeven: Bull Call Spread Breakeven = Lower Strike + Net Debit; Bear Put Spread Breakeven = Higher Strike - Net Debit.
  4. Compare against a single long option: a vertical spread adds premium subsidy, a closer breakeven point, and partial offsetting of Theta decay and Vega exposure, while giving up uncapped gains beyond the short strike.

The mistake to avoid

Oversizing positions simply because a vertical spread costs less per contract, or ignoring pin and assignment risk when the underlying finishes between the two strikes at expiration; if the stock fails to cross breakeven, 100% of the net debit is still lost.
