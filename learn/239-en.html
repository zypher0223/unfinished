Option Seller Risk

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Selling options appeals because time value bleeds away every day (Theta, lesson 209): as long as the underlying does not move far past the strike, the seller keeps the premium, so the common outcome is a small gain most of the time. The price is an occasional large loss. The Options Industry Council (OIC) strategy notes state that a naked call’s maximum loss is unlimited, while a naked put’s is limited but substantial, with breakeven at the strike minus the premium. Sellers also face assignment: early assignment of calls tends to happen before the underlying goes ex-dividend, and puts are more likely to be assigned early when deep in the money. Brokers require margin on the position, the requirement can rise as the underlying moves against you, and failing to meet it can mean a forced close.

DefinitionAn option seller (also called the writer) collects the premium up front and takes on the obligation to perform at the strike if the buyer exercises. Selling a call without owning the stock is a naked call: the higher the underlying goes, the bigger the loss, with no theoretical limit. A naked put’s maximum loss is (strike − premium received) × contract multiplier, reached only if the underlying falls to 0. Assignment means the seller is chosen to perform, and it can happen before expiry.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Selling options is like selling insurance: you collect premiums every month and profit while nothing happens, but one disaster can wipe out years of premiums at once.

A made-up example

Illustration only: the stock is at 100 and you sell 1 call with a 110 strike for a premium of 2, multiplier 100, collecting 2 × 100 = 200. Breakeven = 110 + 2 = 112. Expiry at 105: the option expires worthless and you keep 200. Expiry at 130: loss = (130 − 110 − 2) × 100 = 18 × 100 = 1,800. Expiry at 160: loss = (160 − 110 − 2) × 100 = 4,800; the higher the stock, the bigger the loss. Now a naked put: strike 90, premium 3, breakeven 90 − 3 = 87; in the worst case the stock goes to 0 and the loss is (90 − 3) × 100 = 8,700.

Who developed or popularized it?

Sellers’ rights and obligations are set by exchange and clearing rules; the loss and breakeven formulas here match the Options Industry Council (OIC) strategy notes

OIC’s naked-call notes state an unlimited maximum loss and breakeven at strike plus premium; its naked-put notes describe a limited but substantial maximum loss, breakeven at strike minus premium, and name assignment as the seller’s chief worry.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Before selling, write down the worst case: unlimited for a naked call; (strike − premium) × multiplier for a naked put.
  2. Check your account’s margin requirement and option permissions, and keep cash aside for margin calls if the underlying moves sharply.
  3. Watch assignment risk: calls before the underlying goes ex-dividend and puts that are deep in the money are more likely to be assigned early.
  4. To cap the maximum loss, use a vertical spread (lesson 213) or a covered call (lesson 212) instead.

The mistake to avoid

Treating option selling as steady income because the win rate is high, and scaling up. A high win rate does not mean positive expectancy (lesson 172); one large move can erase everything collected before.
