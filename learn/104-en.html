Advance/Decline

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

The Advance/Decline Line asks whether a stock-index move is supported by broad participation across many stocks or driven by just a few mega-cap heavyweights. It plots as a cumulative line in a lower pane. Beginners often confuse it with the volume-based Accumulation/Distribution Line (which shares the ADL acronym).

Line A/D Linea market-breadth indicator that adds each day's number of advancing stocks minus declining stocks to a running cumulative total
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Whereas a market-cap-weighted index lets two billionaire students dominate the class average, the Advance/Decline Line gives every student one equal vote (+1 for an up day, -1 for a down day) to show how many classmates actually improved.

A made-up example

Illustration only: Daily Net Breadth = Advancing Issues - Declining Issues (unchanged issues count as 0), and A/D Line = prior A/D + Net Breadth. Across a 500-stock index starting at A/D = 10,000: on Day 1, 320 stocks rise, 160 fall, and 20 are flat, so Net Breadth = 320 - 160 = +160 and A/D = 10,000 + 160 = 10,160. On Day 2, two mega-cap stocks rally enough to push the index to a new high, while 150 stocks rise, 330 fall, and 20 are flat; Net Breadth = 150 - 330 = -180, pulling the A/D Line down to 10,160 - 180 = 9,980. Participation narrowed even as the index rose, though divergence alone does not time an exact top.

Who developed or popularized it?

1930s market-breadth practice; Richard Russell (popularized)

TradingView records use in the 1930s and popularization through Richard Russell's Dow Theory Letters in the 1960s. Secondary sources say Leonard P. Ayres of Cleveland Trust analyzed advance-decline data as early as 1926 and Barron's published the figures from 1931; a sole inventor is still not firmly established.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

First: why look at this indicator?

The Advance/Decline Line (A/D Line) measures whether a broad universe of stocks is participating in a market move—it is not derived from a single stock's candlesticks. Each day it counts how many stocks in the universe closed up (Advancing Issues) minus how many closed down (Declining Issues), and adds that daily net difference to a running cumulative line. Think of it as counting how many students in the whole class improved their scores rather than looking only at the top student: a few mega-cap giants can pull a cap-weighted index higher even while the majority of stocks fall. The unit is number of stocks (issues)—not dollar gains or share volume.

When it is most useful

Use it when checking whether a market index rally is broadly supported across many stocks or carried by a handful of heavyweight components. Always specify the exact stock universe first (e.g., NYSE issues or S&P 500 constituents) and pair it with the matching market index; plotting U.S. equity breadth underneath a single cryptocurrency tells you nothing about internal participation inside that coin.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

The market breadth curve, data ticker, and universe description in the lower pane
Verify first that the series genuinely pulls market-wide Advancing minus Declining issue counts—rather than a deceptive custom script that merely counts up-bars minus down-bars on a single ticker. The latter is not market breadth.
The slope and swing highs/lows of the cumulative A/D Line versus the market index above
Because the cumulative total starts from an arbitrary historical start date, the raw number on the right axis has no standalone meaning—focus on multi-week slope and whether the A/D Line confirms new highs in the price index. Never compare the numerical height of the two axes directly.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: Both the market index and the Advance/Decline Line reach new highs together

What it suggests: As the index climbs, a broad majority of constituent stocks are participating on a cumulative basis.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not guarantee every single stock rose today or that the index cannot pull back tomorrow.

You see: The market index prints a new high while the Advance/Decline Line stays below its prior peak

What it suggests: Participation in the rally has narrowed, suggesting a smaller group of large-cap weights may be carrying the index.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean the index must crash immediately.

You see: The Advance/Decline Line trends steadily downward

What it suggests: Over this stretch, days and net counts with more declining stocks than advancing stocks have dominated.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean every single stock in the universe fell every day.

The easiest mistake to make

Thinking the Advance/Decline Line counts green versus red candles on a single stock rather than advancing versus declining stocks across an entire exchange; comparing an NYSE breadth line against a narrow tech index; confusing it with the Accumulation/Distribution volume indicator.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Hypothetical 500-stock index universe where 50 stocks close unchanged each day and yesterday's cumulative A/D Line stood at 1,000.

  1. Today 300 stocks advance and 150 stocks decline, giving a daily net breadth of 300 − 150 = +150 and lifting the cumulative A/D Line to 1,000 + 150 = 1,150.
  2. Tomorrow 200 stocks advance and 250 stocks decline, giving a daily net breadth of 200 − 250 = −50 and pulling the cumulative A/D Line down to 1,150 − 50 = 1,100.
  3. If the cap-weighted index still rose from 100 to 101 on Day 2, it shows the index gained even while more constituent stocks fell than rose (250 down vs. 200 up)—pointing to large-cap weight leadership rather than broad-based strength.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Separating the cap-weighted index price from constituent breadth counts lets you see how widely shared a market move really is.

But do not jump to this conclusion: A two-day sample cannot time a market top, and index constituent changes or delistings over decades can affect ultra-long-term breadth comparisons.

One last question: did it click?

If you add a script labeled 'ADL' onto a single stock chart, does that automatically show you the Advance/Decline breadth of the entire U.S. stock market?

Think first, then open the answer

Not necessarily. First, 'ADL' on a single stock is often the Accumulation/Distribution Line (Lesson 77); second, some scripts count up-candles minus down-candles on that single stock. A true market Advance/Decline Line must pull an external breadth data feed counting advancing and declining stocks across an index or exchange.

TradingView

Search terms: Advance Decline Line

Official documentation exists; verify the underlying breadth market and avoid confusing it with Advance/Decline Ratio (Bars).

  1. Open the TradingView chart using the button below. Choose a stock or coin at the upper left and check the exchange. Choose a chart interval, for example 1 hour or 1 day; one daily candle represents one day.
  2. Use Indicators in the upper chart toolbar, then enter one of the search terms below. Check built-in Technicals first. Community scripts with similar names may calculate different things.
  3. Select the matching result to add it, close the search, and look for a new line or lower pane. If there is no exact built-in match, read the availability note rather than substituting a different indicator.
  4. Hover over the added indicator name and open Settings. Inputs changes calculation settings such as length; start with the defaults. Style changes visual appearance. Follow this lesson’s interpretation steps.
  5. Review completed historical candles and explain what the indicator showed at that time. Adding it to a chart does not place a trade. Practise reading it before any simulated execution.
