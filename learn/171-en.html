Averaging Down vs Adding to Winners

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Averaging down brings your break-even closer, so it feels comforting, but it raises your bet on an idea the market has already rejected. Adding to winners raises your bet in the direction the market has confirmed; the hard part is having the nerve to buy again at a higher price.

DefinitionAveraging down means buying more at a lower price while a position is losing, which lowers the average cost. Adding to winners (pyramiding) means adding while the position is already profitable and price is moving as expected, usually in smaller amounts each time. Both change the average cost and total risk; this lesson compares them with average cost = total spent ÷ total shares and total risk = shares × (average cost − invalidation price).
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Averaging down is loading more cargo onto a leaking boat in the hope it reaches shore sooner; adding to winners is raising another sail in a following wind.

A made-up example

Illustration only: buy 100 shares at 100 and another 100 at 90, for an average cost of (10,000 + 9,000) ÷ 200 = 95. If price falls to 85, the loss is 200 × 10 = 2,000, 1.33 times the loss without averaging (100 × 15 = 1,500). Conversely, buy 100 at 100 and add 50 at 110: the average cost is (10,000 + 5,500) ÷ 150 ≈ 103.3.

Who developed or popularized it?

No single originator verified; similar in spirit to the casino martingale

The martingale, doubling the stake after each loss to recover everything with one win, was popular in 18th-century France. Pyramiding is often attributed to old-school speculators such as Jesse Livermore, though the earliest user is unverified. cis calls averaging down “the worst technique” in his book.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Write down the trade’s invalidation price and recompute total risk before any add.
  2. Before averaging down, ask whether the original idea still holds; if the invalidation has occurred, exit instead.
  3. Add only when price moves as expected and after raising the stop, usually in smaller lots each time.
  4. Record the new average cost and total risk and confirm the worst case is still affordable.

The mistake to avoid

Averaging all the way down lowers the average cost but multiplies total risk; or adding to a winner without raising the stop, so one pullback gives back both the profit and the capital.

What the author says

The author calls averaging down the worst technique: adding to a position that has already proved wrong instead of admitting the mistake and exiting. This lesson contrasts averaging down with adding to winners and uses average-cost and total-risk arithmetic to show the difference.

This is our English summary of the author’s view; the Chinese page of this lesson carries the original excerpt. The amounts and events are the author’s own experience, mostly in Japanese markets, and nothing here is trading advice.
