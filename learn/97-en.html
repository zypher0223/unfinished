Williams Fractals

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Williams Fractals (an indicator marking a 5-bar local peak when the middle 3rd bar's high exceeds the 2 highs to its left and 2 highs to its right, and vice versa for lows) ask where minor 5-bar swing highs and lows have formed. They plot small arrows above highs and below lows on the main price chart. Beginners often assume an arrow sitting above Bar 3 appeared while Bar 3 was forming, overlooking the 2-bar confirmation delay.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Among five people lining up one by one, you can only crown the 3rd person the tallest of the five after the 4th and 5th people have arrived and stood next to them; when the 3rd person first stepped up, the last two were not even there yet.

A made-up example

Illustration only: in the standard 5-bar fractal (2 bars left, 2 bars right), suppose daily highs from Monday to Friday are 10, 11, 13, 12, and 11. Wednesday's high of 13 exceeds Monday (10) and Tuesday (11) by 3 and 2 points, and exceeds Thursday (12) and Friday (11) by 1 and 2 points. However, on Wednesday's close no arrow exists; only after Friday (Bar 5) closes does the indicator reach back 2 bars and draw the fractal arrow above Wednesday's 13 high. Every fractal arrow is confirmed 2 bars after the peak or trough.

Who developed or popularized it?

Bill Williams

Williams Fractals belong to Bill Williams's trading framework; this does not attribute mathematical fractal theory to him.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

First: why look at this indicator?

Williams Fractals mark local five-bar (or N-bar) peaks and valleys on a price chart. In the standard 5-bar version, an Up Fractal forms when the middle bar's high is higher than the highs of the 2 bars to its left and the 2 bars to its right; a Down Fractal forms when the middle bar's low is lower than the lows of the 2 bars on each side. The word 'Fractal' here simply means 'a small local peak or valley flag'—not a mystical mathematical prophecy—and it summarizes turns that have already occurred rather than predicting the next turn in advance.

When it is most useful

Useful for mapping swing structure and marking prior local highs and lows to watch later. Confirm the bar-count setting first, and pay attention to arrow conventions: an upward-pointing arrow above a bar marks a local high fractal (a peak), not an immediate command to buy on that bar.

Step 1: where exactly do you look?

Find the location first, then the units. Colours differ between platforms, so rely on names and meanings rather than colours.

The small arrows plotted above highs and below lows on the main chart
Arrows above bars mark local high fractals (peaks) and arrows below bars mark local low fractals (troughs). The arrow is placed on the middle bar (Bar 3 of 5), which is when the peak occurred—not when confirmation finished; the price level is that bar's high or low.
The 2 comparison bars to the left and 2 comparison bars to the right of the arrow
In the standard 5-bar rule, the 2 bars to the right must finish closing before the arrow is back-painted onto the middle bar. At the far right edge of the chart, any provisional mark on an unclosed bar is not yet final.

Step 2: what does a change mean?

You see: Successive confirmed high fractals and low fractals step higher

What it suggests: Local 5-bar turning points are stepping upward in a stair-step structure.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not mean every down-fractal arrow was a profitable real-time entry.

You see: Price pushes above a previously confirmed high fractal

What it suggests: Price has exceeded the high of that prior local peak; watch whether closes hold above it.

What it does not tell you yet: It does not guarantee a sustained breakout or tell you execution slippage.

You see: Up and down fractal arrows print densely inside a tight horizontal band

What it suggests: Small 5-bar turns are happening back-and-forth inside a narrow range.

What it does not tell you yet: More arrows do not mean higher-quality trade setups.

The easiest mistake to make

Assuming a fractal arrow on a historical peak was visible on that peak bar rather than 2 bars later; trading every single 5-bar fractal arrow in a noisy sideways range; confusing Bill Williams's 5-bar chart pattern with Mandelbrot's mathematical fractal geometry.

Step 3: read one chart together

This is a made-up reading exercise, not live market data. The point is to move from what you see to a reasonable explanation.

Hypothetical five consecutive bars with highs of 10, 11, 13, 12, and 11 under the standard 2-left / 2-right rule.

  1. On Bar 3 (High = 13), the high exceeds Bar 1 (10) and Bar 2 (11), making it only an unconfirmed candidate peak at that moment.
  2. Only after Bar 5 closes—confirming that Bar 4 (12) and Bar 5 (11) both stayed below 13—does the indicator draw the high-fractal arrow above Bar 3.
  3. When a later bar closes at 13.5, it breaks above that confirmed 13 fractal high; if the next bar drops back to close at 12.8, the breakout failed to hold.

Putting the observations together, you could say

Confirm the historical fractal peak after its right-hand bars complete first, then use that level to observe subsequent breakouts or rejections—never reverse the timeline.

But do not jump to this conclusion: Printing a high fractal at 13 does not make 13 a major cycle top, and poking above a fractal does not guarantee a winning trade.

One last question: did it click?

In the standard 5-bar Williams Fractal, if an arrow is drawn above Bar 3, was that arrow visible when Bar 3 closed?

Think first, then open the answer

No. When Bar 3 closed, Bars 4 and 5 had not happened yet—if Bar 4 had printed a higher high, Bar 3 would never have become a fractal. The arrow is drawn on Bar 3 only after Bar 5 completes.

TradingView

Search terms: Williams Fractals

Official built-in; allow for confirmation delay.

  1. Open the TradingView chart using the button below. Choose a stock or coin at the upper left and check the exchange. Choose a chart interval, for example 1 hour or 1 day; one daily candle represents one day.
  2. Use Indicators in the upper chart toolbar, then enter one of the search terms below. Check built-in Technicals first. Community scripts with similar names may calculate different things.
  3. Select the matching result to add it, close the search, and look for a new line or lower pane. If there is no exact built-in match, read the availability note rather than substituting a different indicator.
  4. Hover over the added indicator name and open Settings. Inputs changes calculation settings such as length; start with the defaults. Style changes visual appearance. Follow this lesson’s interpretation steps.
  5. Review completed historical candles and explain what the indicator showed at that time. Adding it to a chart does not place a trade. Practise reading it before any simulated execution.
