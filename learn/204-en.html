Tokenomics: Supply, FDV & Unlocks

My learning & reviewBeginner route

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

DefinitionTokenomics covers how a token is issued, allocated, released and used. Circulating market cap = price × circulating supply; fully diluted valuation (FDV) = price × maximum (or total) supply. Early allocations to the team and investors usually have a cliff and linear vesting; each unlock raises circulating supply and can bring selling pressure.

Many new tokens launch with only about a tenth of supply circulating, so the circulating cap looks small while the FDV is already high. Over the following years locked allocations unlock on schedule, and early holders who got tokens very cheaply may sell. Understanding the supply structure is the first step to judging whether a token is expensive and whether heavy selling lies ahead.

First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

It is like a company listing only 10% of its shares while founders and investors hold the other 90% and can sell over the next few years; today’s price reflects supply and demand for that 10% alone.

A made-up example

Illustration only: a token trades at USD 2 with 100 million circulating and a 1 billion maximum supply. Circulating cap = 2 × 100 million = USD 200 million; FDV = 2 × 1 billion = USD 2 billion. Next month 50 million team and investor tokens unlock, raising circulating supply by 50% at once; if demand does not keep up, the price may come under pressure.

Who developed or popularized it?

Primary-market evolution: ICO (2017) → IEO (2019) → IDO (2020) → venture rounds and “high FDV, low float” (2021–2023) → fair launches (from 2023)

Ethereum’s 2014 crowdsale raised about USD 18 million. Many projects from the 2017 ICO boom failed, and in September that year the People’s Bank of China and six other agencies banned ICOs. Exchange-led IEOs rose in 2019, DeFi brought decentralized IDOs in 2020, and in 2021–2023 many venture-backed, high-FDV, low-float tokens fell sharply after unlocks.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Look at both circulating cap and FDV, and work out circulating supply as a share of the maximum.
  2. Check the allocation: how much goes to team, investors, community and ecosystem funds.
  3. Find the unlock schedule and mark large unlocks and their size relative to circulating supply at the time.
  4. Learn what the token is actually used for (fees, governance, staking) and whether there are buybacks or burns.

The mistake to avoid

Judging by unit price. A USD 0.01 token with a trillion units still has a USD 10 billion FDV; compare market cap and FDV, not the price per token.
