SocialFi: Tokenising Social Graphs

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

On traditional Web2 social networks, a creator's follower list (social graph) and content history sit inside proprietary corporate databases: platforms capture most ad revenue, can deplatform accounts at will, and prevent users from porting their audience to competing apps (see the "On-Chain Identity: DID & SBT" lesson on this site). SocialFi addresses this across two layers: first, decentralized social graph protocols (such as Farcaster and Lens) store profiles, follows, and posts on-chain or on hybrid decentralized nodes so users can switch client apps while keeping their social graph intact; second, financial monetization mechanisms turn posts into collectible NFTs, enable crypto tipping, or tokenize access to a creator's chat room via Automated Bonding Curves (where the price of a creator's 'Key' or 'Share' rises quadratically as more people buy). However, financializing social relationships introduces severe failure modes: steep bonding curves invite sniper bots to buy up cheap initial supply and dump on late-arriving fans, causing rapid liquidity collapse; token and point incentives attract massive Sybil bot farming (see the "Airdrops & Sybil Attacks" lesson on this site) that floods feeds with spam; and speculative friction often alienates genuine social users.

DefinitionThe combination of decentralized identity, on-chain social graphs, and tokenomics to give users ownership over their social connections and monetize content or influence.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Web2 social media is like renting a booth in a mall where the landlord owns your customer list and locks it away if you leave; a decentralized social graph lets you carry your own portable customer Rolodex to any storefront; and bonding-curve creator keys act like fan-club passes whose price ratchets up with every buyer—allowing automated scalper bots to buy the first ten passes in milliseconds and dump them on genuine fans at the top.

A made-up example

Suppose a SocialFi app prices a creator's n-th access key along the quadratic bonding curve P = n^2 / 100 USD. As key supply grows from n = 10 to n = 30, the key price jumps from 100 / 100 = $1.00 to 900 / 100 = $9.00, while the protocol and creator charge a combined 10% fee on every buy and sell. If a fan buys at n = 30 for $9.90 (including the 10% buy fee) and early holders sell 10 keys to push supply back to n = 20 ($4.00 spot price), selling the key nets only $4.00 * (1 - 0.10) = $3.60, leaving a $6.30 loss (for illustrative purposes only).

Who developed or popularized it?

From Steemit Content Rewards (2016) to Decentralized Graphs and Bonding Curves (2022–2023)

Early blockchain social experiments began with token-incentivized blogging platforms like Steemit in 2016. After 2020, protocols such as Farcaster and Lens focused on portable social graph infrastructure, while Friend.tech's August 2023 launch on Base popularized bonding-curve trading of creator 'Keys' before experiencing sharp speculative decay.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Distinguish protocol graphs from speculative apps: separate portable social graph infrastructure from short-term bonding-curve trading games.
  2. Calculate bonding-curve slope and fee drag: account for steep quadratic price impact and round-trip trading fees (often 10%–20% total).
  3. Check for sniper-bot concentration: inspect on a block explorer whether automated bots bought the earliest keys in the creation block.
  4. Evaluate organic engagement versus Sybil noise: check whether platform activity reflects human conversation or automated airdrop farming.
  5. Use delegated session keys or burner wallets: keep primary asset vaults disconnected from daily social signing.

The mistake to avoid

Misconception 1: Confusing a steep bonding-curve paper price with deep market liquidity—when early holders exit, the curve slides down just as fast. Misconception 2: Ignoring heavy two-way protocol and creator taxes on every trade. Misconception 3: Clicking malicious phishing links or unverified interactive frames embedded directly inside decentralized social feeds (see the "Smart Contracts & Token Approvals" lesson on this site).
