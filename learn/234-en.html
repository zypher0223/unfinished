On-Chain Data Basics

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

An Ethereum transaction records fields such as the sender (from), recipient (to), amount (value), sequence number (nonce) and gas (the fee paid to execute it), and is broadcast to the whole network, so anyone can look it up. A block explorer is a lookup tool, not a wallet: it does not hold your private keys and cannot reverse a transfer. Explorers and data firms attach labels to some addresses, such as “exchange hot wallet” or “whale” (an address holding a large amount); these are their inferences and can be wrong. When reading flows, remember that a deposit to an exchange is not the same as a sale: exchanges also move funds between cold wallets (kept offline) and hot wallets (online, used for withdrawals), which creates large transfers too.

DefinitionOn-chain data is the public record of transactions, address balances and contract calls on a public blockchain. Anyone can inspect it transaction by transaction in a block explorer such as Etherscan, or aggregate it into dashboards on a data platform such as Dune, which uses SQL queries. Typical uses: checking whether a transfer succeeded, seeing what an address holds, and watching large flows between exchange addresses.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

On-chain data is like a public wall of bank statements: every movement is posted, but the account names are strings of characters, and the sticky note saying “this belongs to X” is someone’s guess.

A made-up example

Illustration only: an address labelled “exchange hot wallet” receives 5 transfers totalling 3,000 ETH in a day. At 1 ETH = USD 2,000 (illustration only), 3,000 × 2,000 = USD 6,000,000. That could be users preparing to sell, the exchange moving its own funds, or a market maker topping up inventory; the number alone cannot tell you which. Check the same window: did the exchange’s cold wallet send 3,000 ETH to this hot wallet? If so, net inflow = 3,000 − 3,000 = 0, most likely an internal transfer rather than outside money arriving.

Who developed or popularized it?

No single inventor: block explorers and on-chain analytics grew out of public blockchain ledgers

Public-chain transactions are broadcast to the whole network for validation, so the record is public by design; block explorers present it as web pages, and data platforms turn it into tables you can query with SQL. Address labels are maintained by each company and follow no common standard.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Check a transfer: paste the transaction hash (TxHash, the transaction’s ID) into a block explorer; read the status first, then from, to, amount, token and network.
  2. Read the address page: balance, token holdings and history are there; labels come from the explorer or a data firm, so treat them as clues, not facts.
  3. Read flows: add up all addresses of the same entity and look at the net figure, ruling out internal transfers before talking about outside money.
  4. With ready-made dashboards, open the query and check its scope: which chains, which addresses, which period; when two dashboards disagree, the scope usually differs.

The mistake to avoid

Reading “a big deposit to an exchange” as “heavy selling is coming”, or treating an explorer label as a confirmed identity. The first is often an internal transfer; the second is a third party’s inference.
