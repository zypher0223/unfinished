Prediction Markets & InfoFi

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

On traditional social platforms, making loud, inaccurate predictions carries zero financial cost, while rigorous research rarely captures direct economic value. Information Finance (InfoFi) uses financial mechanisms to price and verify information, led by on-chain Prediction Markets: a future verifiable event is split into binary 'Yes' and 'No' outcome shares collateralized by $1.00 of stablecoins (see the "Stablecoins Basics" lesson on this site); upon resolution, the winning share redeems for $1.00 and the losing share expires at $0.00. Consequently, a 'Yes' share trading at $0.65 reflects a market-implied probability of roughly 65%. The relationship between prediction markets, derivatives, and gambling is heavily regulated and varies sharply across jurisdictions: while economists study them as information-aggregation and hedging tools, regulators in different countries classify them either as regulated event-contract derivatives (such as under the U.S. CFTC) or as restricted online gambling. Beyond prediction markets, InfoFi explores tokenizing social mindshare and reputation, yet the sector faces three major bottlenecks: first, thin liquidity in long-tail markets allows small orders to distort implied probabilities; second, ambiguous event wordings can trigger oracle governance disputes (see the "Bridges & Oracles" lesson on this site) where whale voters override objective outcomes; third, tokenizing attention frequently incentivizes AI-generated spam and insider front-running.

DefinitionInformation Finance (InfoFi), a sector using on-chain prediction markets, attention incentives, and reputation staking to aggregate, price, and verify real-world event probabilities and information.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

An internet poll is costless talk, whereas a prediction market is a binary voucher that pays $1.00 if an event happens and $0.00 if it does not: if traders pay $0.70 for 'Rain Tomorrow' and $0.30 for 'No Rain,' that $0.70 price acts like a thermometer reading a 70% market-implied chance of rain—yet if the pool holds only $10, one person spending $5 can artificially spike the reading to 90%, and if sleet falls instead of rain, the payout depends on how the oracle referees vote.

A made-up example

A binary prediction market asks whether a quarterly macro print will exceed 3.0%: 'Yes' shares trade at 0.60 USDC and 'No' shares trade at 0.40 USDC (summing to 1.00 USDC, implying a 60% probability). A trader spends 300 USDC to buy 500 'Yes' shares at $0.60: if the official release prints at 3.2% and resolves cleanly via the oracle, the 500 shares redeem for 500 * $1.00 = 500 USDC (a net profit of 500 - 300 = 200 USDC); if the print is 2.9%, the shares expire worthless for a 300 USDC loss (for illustrative purposes only).

Who developed or popularized it?

From the Iowa Electronic Markets (1988) and Hanson's Futarchy to On-Chain Prediction Markets

Modern academic prediction markets began with the University of Iowa's Iowa Electronic Markets (IEM) in 1988, followed by economist Robin Hanson's work on 'Futarchy' (decision markets). Between 2014 and 2018, Augur and Gnosis built early Ethereum prediction markets and the Conditional Token Framework, laying the groundwork for stablecoin-settled platforms like Polymarket and regulated event exchanges like Kalshi after 2020.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Read the exact resolution rules: verify the designated official data source, cutoff timestamp, and edge-case definitions before trading.
  2. Inspect order-book depth and spread: check whether the market has deep liquidity or whether a small order can skew the implied probability (see the "Liquidity & Market Impact" lesson on this site).
  3. Examine the oracle dispute process: understand how optimistic oracles (such as UMA) handle challenges and token-holder dispute votes.
  4. Check local regulatory rules: verify how your jurisdiction regulates event contracts, derivatives, and online wagering.
  5. Filter out attention-farming spam: distinguish data-backed research from AI-generated noise in mindshare-reward programs.

The mistake to avoid

Misconception 1: Treating the price in an illiquid prediction market as an objective scientific probability when a single whale can skew thin order books. Misconception 2: Trading on the headline question without reading the fine-print resolution criteria, risking losses during ambiguous oracle disputes. Misconception 3: Ignoring jurisdictional restrictions on event contracts and wagering.
