Cutting Losses

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

A stop is not “giving up”; it caps how much any one trade can cost. Beginners rarely lose from too many small losses; they lose when one trade they refuse to exit keeps growing. Decide where you would be wrong before deciding to buy.

DefinitionCutting a loss means writing down, before entry, the condition that proves the trade wrong (the invalidation, either a price or a closing condition) and exiting as planned when it happens, rather than delaying because you do not want to admit the loss. This lesson treats the stop as part of the trade plan, not an emotional decision after the fact; it limits the size of a single loss and does not guarantee a fill exactly at the stop price.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

Like knowing where the brake is before you drive: not because you plan to crash, but so you can stop if something goes wrong.

A made-up example

Illustration only: buy at 102 with the invalidation at 100.7, just below the prior low of 100.8, so risk per share is 102 − 100.7 = 1.3. With a 10,000 account risking 1% (100), size is 100 ÷ 1.3 ≈ 76 shares. Price closes at 100.5 and you sell as planned; the actual loss is about 76 × 1.5 = 114, and the extra 14 comes from filling below the stop price.

Who developed or popularized it?

No single originator verified; a long-standing risk-control principle among traders

“Cut your losses and let your profits run” is a long-standing trading maxim whose first source is unverified. A common fixed-percentage version is William O’Neil’s 7–8% rule in “How to Make Money in Stocks”; cis, whose book this lesson draws on, says he keeps no fixed numeric stop and sells when he expects a fall within the hour.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Before entry, write the invalidation: which price or close shows the idea is wrong.
  2. Compute unit risk as entry − invalidation, then size the position from the amount you accept to lose.
  3. Act when the condition occurs; do not move the stop further away during the session.
  4. If price meets your entry conditions again after the stop, you may re-enter under a new plan; it is a new trade, not a second admission of failure.

The mistake to avoid

Moving the stop further away, or refusing to sell because “I have already lost so much,” is how small losses become large ones; the opposite mistake is a stop so tight that ordinary noise keeps hitting it.

From the book: cis Stock Trading

Source: cis, cis Stock Trading (Chinese edition, translated by Zhu Jia, China Youth Press, 2020, ISBN 978-7-5153-6031-7; Japanese original published by KADOKAWA, 2018)

What the author says

The author keeps no fixed numeric stop: if he thinks price may fall within the hour, he sells regardless of his entry. He readily buys back a stock he stopped out of if it rises again, and treats small losses as normal while avoiding large ones. This lesson turns that into a testable routine: define the invalidation in advance, act on it, then judge any re-entry by fresh conditions.

This is our English summary of the author’s view; the Chinese page of this lesson carries the original excerpt. The amounts and events are the author’s own experience, mostly in Japanese markets, and nothing here is trading advice.
