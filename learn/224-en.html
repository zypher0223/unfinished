On-Chain Derivatives

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

Derivatives are financial contracts whose value derives from an underlying asset for hedging or leveraged exposure (see the "Spot, Margin & Leveraged Contracts" lesson on this site). In crypto, the dominant instrument is the perpetual futures contract, which has no expiration date and uses periodic funding rates to anchor contract prices to spot while tracking capital commitments via open interest (see the "Funding Rate" and "Open Interest" lessons on this site). Unlike centralized exchanges that custody user funds in opaque internal databases (see the "Exchange Risk: Lessons from Mt. Gox, FTX & Bybit" lesson on this site), on-chain derivatives lock margin inside smart contracts using two primary architectures: first, Order-Book protocols (on rollups or dedicated app-chains) that match limit and market orders off-chain or on a high-speed consensus layer while settling on-chain; second, Liquidity-Pool (LP) protocols where liquidity providers deposit a basket of assets to act directly as the counterparty to traders at oracle-fed prices (see the "Bridges & Oracles" lesson on this site). When a position breaches its maintenance margin, independent liquidation bots (Keepers) trigger forced closure in exchange for a liquidation fee. Key failure modes include oracle latency or price manipulation causing unfair liquidations, one-sided market trends where profitable traders drain the counterparty LP pool, and sequencer downtime blocking emergency order cancellations.

DefinitionDerivatives protocols deployed on smart contracts or app-chains that primarily offer perpetual futures with self-custodial margin and transparent on-chain liquidation rules.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

A centralized exchange is like handing your chips to a closed casino cashier; an on-chain order book is like locking chips in a glass vault while an electronic board matches buyers and sellers; and an LP-pool protocol is like a public syndicate vault that takes the opposite side of every trader's bet—earning fees when traders lose, but paying out of its own capital when traders win big in a one-way trend.

A made-up example

A trader deposits 1,000 USDC margin into an LP-based perpetual protocol and opens a 5x long position of 2.5 ETH at $2,000 (notional value $5,000), paying a 0.1% opening fee ($5) to the LP pool under a 2% maintenance margin requirement ($100). If ETH drops to $1,645, the unrealized loss is (2,000 - 1,645) * 2.5 = $887.50, leaving remaining equity of 1,000 - 5 - 887.50 = $107.50—only $7.50 above the $100 liquidation line. A further drop below about $1,642 would push equity under $100, and a keeper bot would liquidate the position (for illustrative purposes only).

Who developed or popularized it?

From Robert Shiller's Perpetual Futures Concept (1992) to Decentralized Perp Protocols

Economist Robert Shiller first proposed perpetual futures in 1992 to track illiquid assets without contract rollovers, ; the crypto exchange ICBIT launched the first inverse bitcoin perpetual in 2011, and exchanges such as BitMEX popularized perpetual swaps from 2016. After 2020, protocols such as dYdX, GMX, and Hyperliquid pioneered Layer 2 order books, multi-asset counterparty liquidity pools, and high-throughput app-chain order books.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Identify the execution model: determine whether the protocol uses an order book on a rollup/app-chain or an oracle-priced counterparty liquidity pool.
  2. Check margin and liquidation parameters: review maintenance margin thresholds, liquidation penalties, and auto-deleveraging (ADL) rules.
  3. Assess LP counterparty exposure: before depositing into a derivatives liquidity pool, evaluate how one-sided market trends impact pool net asset value.
  4. Verify oracle and sequencer resilience: inspect price-feed update latency and fallback mechanisms during network congestion.
  5. Isolate margin accounts: use isolated margin or dedicated sub-accounts so a single liquidated position cannot drain your entire portfolio.

The mistake to avoid

Misconception 1: Assuming derivatives LP vaults only collect risk-free trading fees—LPs act as the counterparty to traders and suffer principal losses when traders profit in strong trends. Misconception 2: Thinking self-custody prevents liquidations—automated Keeper bots liquidate underwater positions immediately on-chain. Misconception 3: Overlooking cumulative funding rates and borrowing fees paid to the pool over multi-week holds.
