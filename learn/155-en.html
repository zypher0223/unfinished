Rounding Bottoms & V Reversals

My learning & reviewBeginner routeLesson quiz

Records are saved only in this browser. Reading is not the same as mastery.

A rounding bottom is a slow reversal: selling fades gradually and buyers take over gradually, over a long and visible process. A V reversal is fast: a sharp drop followed straight away by a sharp rise, with almost no warning, so it can only be recognised afterwards and chasing it is riskier.

DefinitionA rounding bottom forms when a decline slows, price lingers sideways at the lows for a while, and then gradually rises, tracing a gentle U. A V reversal is a sharp drop followed almost immediately by a sharp rise; here it is identified as a fall of more than 4% within 8 bars followed by a recovery of more than 80% of that fall within the next 8 bars. Both can only be confirmed afterwards.
First chart ever? Read these four ideas first
Price chart and candle
A candle summarizes one chosen time interval. On a daily chart, each candle is one day; it shows the opening, highest, lowest and closing price for that day.
Length or period
A length of 14 usually means 14 candles, not automatically 14 days. On a one-hour chart that means 14 hours of bars.
Main chart and lower pane
The main chart shows price. A lower pane is a separate little chart below it. Its numbers may use completely different units.
Signal
A signal is a condition worth checking, such as one line crossing another. It is an observation, not a guarantee or an automatic order.

Think of it this way

A rounding bottom is a ship slowly turning around; a V reversal is a ball bouncing straight off the floor.

A made-up example

Illustration only: price falls from 104 to 99.5 in 6 bars (about −4.3%), then rises to 103.3 in 7 bars, recovering 3.8 of the 4.5 drop (about 84%), which meets this lesson’s V-reversal threshold.

Who developed or popularized it?

Classical chart pattern; systematised by Richard Schabacker and by Edwards and Magee

Such patterns were described systematically by Richard Schabacker in the 1930s and popularised by Edwards and Magee’s 1948 “Technical Analysis of Stock Trends”; the earliest users are unverified.

An attributed developer, popularizer and first user are different claims. A unique first user is stated only when reliable documentation supports it.

Read it one step at a time

  1. Look at the speed of the decline: gradually slowing or sharp.
  2. For a rounding bottom, look at time spent at the lows and gradually rising lows; for a V, look at the speed of the rebound.
  3. Apply the thresholds: size of the drop, share recovered and number of bars.
  4. Both are confirmed only once they have played out; chasing the sharp rise of a V carries the risk of another sudden drop.

The mistake to avoid

Trying to “call the V” and buy during a sharp fall is the most common mistake; a V can only be identified afterwards.
